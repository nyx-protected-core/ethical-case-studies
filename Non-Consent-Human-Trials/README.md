# Non-Consent Human Trials

Date: 2026-11-19

*Research brief. Anonymous author. Research only; not for publication in this form.*

---

## 1. Scope and ground rules

This page covers documented cases, worldwide and across eras, in which people were used in medical or scientific experimentation, or had tissue or bodily integrity used for research or state programmes, without valid informed consent. It works at the level of law, institutions and evidence. It contains no description of methods beyond what is needed to explain why each case qualifies.

- **Source types.** **[PR]** peer-reviewed analysis (DOI checked against Crossref and OpenAlex for title, authors and year); **[REC]** laws, treaties, court and tribunal judgments, official inquiries, commissions, government apologies and settlement statements, cited as records; **[SECONDARY]** news, used only for timelines.
- **Findings versus allegations.** A **Finding** is a tribunal or court judgment, an official inquiry or commission conclusion, a government apology or a settlement. Anything else is labelled **Allegation**, with its current status. A settlement "without admission of liability" is recorded as a settlement, not as an admission.
- **Government figures are marked ⚑.** Victim counts and compensation totals record what an authority found, chose to record or agreed to pay. They move with reporting incentives: states have reasons to minimise counts of their own abuses; compensation schemes count only those who applied and could prove eligibility; adversary states and wartime tribunals have reasons to maximise the other side's crimes. **They are not measures of how many people were harmed.**
- **Names.** Victims are named only where already public by name (for example Henrietta Lacks, or a named party in a published case caption). Perpetrators are described by role; published case names and captions are kept as written.
- **Scope limit.** This is a global page. Court records and laws are cited as records; analysis comes from peer-reviewed international scholarship.
- **Evidence strength** for proposed solutions is rated **Strong**, **Moderate**, **Limited** or **UNTESTED**.

## 2. Definitions: what counts as non-consent

The page uses four standard instruments.

- **Nuremberg Code (1947), principle 1:** "The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential", meaning legal capacity, free power of choice without "force, fraud, deceit, duress, overreaching, or other ulterior form of constraint or coercion", and sufficient knowledge and comprehension [1].
- **Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 1964, revised since):** research participants must give informed consent, with added protection for those unable to consent, and the interests of the individual prevail over those of science and society [2].
- **Belmont Report (1979):** respect for persons (informed consent, protection of those with diminished autonomy), beneficence, and justice in the selection of subjects [3].
- **CIOMS International Ethical Guidelines (2016):** individual informed consent, special protection for vulnerable groups, and responsiveness to host communities in lower-resource settings [4].

A case qualifies here when at least one of these holds: (a) no consent was sought; (b) consent was obtained by deception about the nature or purpose of the procedure; (c) consent was coerced, including through custody, institutional dependence, military orders or financial pressure; (d) the person lacked capacity and no valid substitute safeguard applied; or (e) tissue or data were taken and used for research without the person's knowledge. Each case below states its ground in one line ("**Why non-consent:**").

## 4. Cases: findings and allegations

### 4.1 Nazi experiments and the Doctors' Trial
**Why non-consent:** concentration-camp prisoners had no free power of choice.
- **Finding.** In US Military Tribunal Case 1 (1946-1947), 23 defendants (senior physicians and administrators) were tried; seven were convicted, sentenced to death and executed, nine were convicted and imprisoned, and seven were acquitted [5]. The judgment set out the ten principles later called the Nuremberg Code [1].
- **Analysis.** Peer-reviewed historians trace the Code's consent standard to the trial's expert witnesses and to a parallel international scientific commission [43]; its first principle became the global benchmark [44].

### 4.2 Unit 731 (Japanese-occupied China)
**Why non-consent:** prisoners and captured civilians were used as subjects by force.
- **Finding.** The Soviet military tribunal at Khabarovsk (December 1949) convicted 12 former Japanese servicemen for preparing and using bacteriological weapons, including experiments on prisoners [6]. ⚑ *Victim numbers given in testimony at this trial are a wartime adversary's record; the tribunal had an incentive to maximise, and the defendants under custody had an incentive to cooperate.*
- **Record on immunity.** A US interagency records group (2007) records that amnesties were granted to supervisors of the programme "in exchange for the data obtained from experiments conducted on humans" [7]. Peer-reviewed analysis of declassified US files reaches the same conclusion and frames it as complicity in the national interest [45] [46].

### 4.3 Tuskegee (United States)
**Why non-consent:** men were deceived about their diagnosis and denied treatment so the disease could be observed.
- **Finding.** The US Public Health Service study enrolled 600 Black men (399 with syphilis, 201 controls) ⚑ from 1932; it ended in 1972 after a federal advisory panel recommended it stop; a class action settled in 1974 for $10 million ⚑ [8]. The President formally apologised in 1997 [9].
- **Analysis.** A peer-reviewed history documents that treatment, including penicillin after the 1940s, was withheld by design [47].

### 4.4 Guatemala STD studies (US-funded)
**Why non-consent:** prisoners, soldiers, psychiatric patients and others were deliberately infected without being asked or told.
- **Finding.** The Presidential Commission found the research "involved intentionally exposing and infecting vulnerable populations ... without the subjects' consent" [10]. Its report records 1,308 people intentionally exposed, diagnostic testing of 5,128 subjects, and 83 deaths noted in the lead investigator's records during the studies (cause not attributed by the Commission) ⚑ [10].
- **Analysis.** The studies came to light through archival research [48]; later analysis covers the unresolved question of redress for survivors and descendants [49].

### 4.5 MKUltra (United States)
**Why non-consent:** drugs and procedures were given to people who did not know they were subjects of a CIA programme.
- **Finding (record).** At the 1977 Senate hearing the CIA Director testified to 149 MKUltra subprojects at 80 institutions, including universities, hospitals and prisons ⚑ (most records had been destroyed in 1973, so counts are a floor) [11].

### 4.6 US human radiation experiments (ACHRE)
**Why non-consent:** patients were injected or exposed without being told the purpose or risks.
- **Finding.** The Advisory Committee identified nearly 4,000 federally sponsored human radiation experiments between 1944 and 1974 ⚑ [13], including 18 hospital patients injected with plutonium in 1945-1947 [14].
- **Marshall Islands (Project 4.1).** ACHRE found that problems arose in explaining the research to the Marshallese and "in obtaining their consent for both research-related interventions ... and treatment" [15]. **Allegation:** the Marshall Islands government argued to ACHRE that the population was exposed to fallout deliberately as an experiment; ACHRE recorded the claim and did not find that the exposure was intended for study [15].

### 4.7 Willowbrook (United States)
**Why non-consent:** children with intellectual disability in a state institution were deliberately infected; parental agreement was obtained in an institutional setting that critics argued made free choice impossible.
- **Record.** The investigators' own peer-reviewed reports describe deliberate infection of residents [16]. No court judgment on the studies was located.
- **Analysis.** It was among the cases in an influential 1966 critique of unethical research [51]; the lead investigator later defended it (see Section 6) [52].

### 4.8 Holmesburg Prison (United States)
**Why non-consent:** paid prisoners awaiting trial, many illiterate, were not told what substances they were exposed to.
- **Finding.** In 2022 the City of Philadelphia formally apologised for experiments in which inmates "were intentionally exposed to pharmaceuticals, viruses, fungus, asbestos, and even dioxin" [17].
- **Analysis.** Peer-reviewed history places the programme in a wider US practice of using prisoners as "cheap and available" subjects [53].

### 4.9 Henrietta Lacks and HeLa cells
**Why non-consent:** tumour cells taken during treatment in 1951 were used for research and commerce without her knowledge.
- **Record.** In 2013 the federal research agency and the Lacks family agreed controlled access to HeLa genome data, with family members on the access committee [18] [54]. No court has ruled on the 1951 taking; a later state supreme court case held that a physician must disclose research interests to obtain informed consent [55].
- **Analysis.** Peer-reviewed policy review uses the case to examine consent for biospecimens [56].

### 4.10 Forced sterilisation programmes
**Why non-consent:** sterilisation was ordered by law or obtained by coercion, threats or misinformation.
- **United States.** The Supreme Court upheld compulsory sterilisation in *Buck v. Bell* (1927) [19]; it later struck down a punitive sterilisation law in *Skinner v. Oklahoma* (1942) without overruling *Buck* [57]. North Carolina enacted a compensation programme in 2013 [20]. Peer-reviewed history documents California's programme [58].
- **Sweden.** A government commission reviewed sterilisations from 1935-1975 and recorded nearly 63,000 sterilisations in those years ⚑ [21]; a 1999 law provides compensation of SEK 175,000 for each person sterilised under the conditions set out in the Act ⚑ [22]. The Act required a personal application by a statutory deadline, so payment depends on survivors applying and proving eligibility. Peer-reviewed analysis [60].
- **Peru.** In a 2003 friendly settlement Peru acknowledged responsibility for the coerced sterilisation and death of María Mamérita Mestanza Chávez [23]. In *Ramos Durand et al. v. Peru* (judgment of 25 November 2025) the Inter-American Court held that the programme was a state policy aimed mainly at poor, rural, Indigenous women and implemented through coercion and quotas, and that more than 314,000 women and 24,000 men were sterilised ⚑ [24]. *Programme staff received incentives for recruitment, so programme statistics were themselves shaped by targets.* Peer-reviewed analysis [61].
- **Japan.** On 3 July 2024 the Supreme Court Grand Bench held the sterilisation provisions of the Eugenic Protection Law (1948-1996) unconstitutional and barred the State from relying on the limitation period [25]. The Diet then enacted a compensation law, promulgated on 17 October 2024, providing JPY 15 million for each person who underwent eugenic surgery ⚑ [62]. Peer-reviewed analysis [63] [64].
- **Czechoslovakia and successors.** The Czech Public Defender of Rights found in 2005 that sterilisations had been performed in contravention of the law [26]. The European Court of Human Rights found a violation in *V.C. v. Slovakia* (2011) [27]. Czech Act No. 297/2021 provides a one-off payment to people sterilised unlawfully [28].

### 4.11 Soviet "Laboratory 12" (Allegation)
**Why non-consent (if proven):** prisoners were reportedly used to test poisons.
- **Allegation; status: no court or official finding located.** Peer-reviewed security research reports that the Soviet poison laboratory tested substances on prisoners, relying on memoir and witness accounts [29].

### 4.12 DPRK (Allegation)
**Why non-consent (if proven):** prisoners and persons with disabilities would have no capacity to refuse.
- **Allegation; status: unconfirmed.** The UN Commission of Inquiry received allegations of lethal chemical and biological testing on political prisoners and of experiments on persons with disabilities, and stated it was "not in a position to confirm whether any such medical experiments were conducted" [30].

### 4.13 Porton Down (United Kingdom)
**Why non-consent:** service volunteers were not properly told the purpose or risks; witnesses said some believed they were in common-cold research.
- **Finding.** In 2004 an inquest jury found that a 20-year-old airman, Ronald Maddison, was unlawfully killed by "application of a nerve agent in a non-therapeutic experiment" in 1953 [65].
- **Finding (settlement and apology).** In 2008 the government settled with veterans, without admission of liability, and apologised to those "who may have been affected" [31] [32]. The official written statement is cited for the fact of a settlement and apology; the claimant count and payment total are omitted pending confirmation from that official text.
- **Note (UNVERIFIED).** A 2006 High Court resolution of the inquest verdict is reported in news only and is **UNVERIFIED** against the judgment text [66].
- **Analysis.** Peer-reviewed history examines how consent was understood in these trials [67].

### 4.14 Pfizer Trovan trial, Kano (Nigeria)
**Why non-consent:** plaintiffs allege children were enrolled in an experimental trial without their guardians being told it was experimental or offered alternatives.
- **Finding (court).** The Second Circuit held that the prohibition on non-consensual medical experimentation is a norm of customary international law actionable under the Alien Tort Statute, and reinstated the claims [33]. This ruling is on jurisdiction, not on the facts.
- **Settlement.** Kano State and the company settled in 2009 (up to $35 million for participants, $30 million for health initiatives, $10 million in costs ⚑); the company "specifically denies any wrongdoing or liability" [34].
- **Allegation; status:** the underlying consent failures were not adjudicated. Peer-reviewed commentary [68] [69].

### 4.15 HPV vaccine project (India)
**Why non-consent:** the parliamentary committee found consent forms signed by hostel wardens or by thumb impression without adequate information.
- **Finding (parliamentary committee).** The 72nd Report found that the project was a clinical trial conducted as an "observation/demonstration project", in violation of Indian clinical-trial rules, with grossly deficient consent [35] [70].
- **Court record.** In 2018 a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court held that parliamentary committee reports may be referred to in court, in the petition concerning these studies [36]. The final disposal of the petition is open (Section 9).
- **Counter-position.** The implementing organisation states the project followed approved protocols [71]; see Section 6 [72].

### 4.16 Thalidomide "investigational" distribution
**Why non-consent:** patients, including pregnant patients, were given an unapproved drug without being told it was experimental.
- **Record.** The federal agency's own account records that the US sponsor distributed tablets to more than 1,000 doctors, reaching nearly 20,000 patients, several hundred of them pregnant ⚑ [37]. The 1962 amendments then required informed consent for investigational drugs [38]. Peer-reviewed review [73].

### 4.17 Vipeholm caries study (Sweden)
**Why non-consent:** residents of an institution for people with intellectual disability were fed high-sugar diets to induce tooth decay without consent.
- **Record.** The investigators' published report covers 436 residents observed for five years [39]; a participating investigator later reflected on its ethics [74]. No court finding was located.

### 4.18 Edgewood Arsenal (United States)
**Why non-consent:** soldiers were exposed to chemical agents with inadequate information and no follow-up.
- **Finding.** The Ninth Circuit held in 2015 that the Army has a continuing duty to warn former test subjects of new information about their health and to provide medical care for injuries caused by the tests [40]. The VA, citing the Department of Defense, records about 7,000 soldiers exposed to more than 250 chemicals from 1955-1975 ⚑ [41].

### 4.19 South African military "aversion" programme (Allegation)
**Why non-consent (if proven):** conscripts under military authority were subjected to aversion therapy for homosexuality.
- **Allegation; status: recorded, not adjudicated.** The Truth and Reconciliation Commission recorded "reports of a particular psychologist who used aversion therapy and electric shocks on homosexual military men" [42]. Peer-reviewed commentary summarises the evidence [75].

## 6. Disagreements and counter-evidence

- **Using data from abusive research.** Some argue Nazi data should never be cited; others argue limited use with disclosure honours victims [80].
- **Did Tuskegee cause lasting distrust?** An economic study links the 1972 disclosure to lower use of medical care and higher mortality among Black men [81]. A survey study found Black and white respondents equally willing to take part in research once other factors were accounted for [82]. The two measure different things (care use versus stated willingness).
- **Willowbrook.** The lead investigator argued that infection was near-universal in the institution and that the studies produced protective benefit [52]; critics held that the conditions themselves undermined free consent [51].
- **Kano.** The company states the study had government approval and guardians' consent [34]; the appeal ruling addressed only whether the claim could be heard [33].
- **India HPV.** The implementing organisation and some scholars say the project was a post-licensure demonstration, not a trial, and that deaths were unrelated to the vaccine [71] [72]; the parliamentary committee rejected that classification [35].
- **Marshall Islands.** Deliberate exposure is alleged; ACHRE found consent failures in follow-up research but did not find intent [15].
- **Porton Down.** Veterans have argued that later proceedings left the consent question unexamined. A 2006 High Court resolution is reported in news only and remains **UNVERIFIED** against the judgment text [66].

## 7. Values (labelled)

*This section states value judgments, not findings.*
- **Value:** consent protects persons, not only outcomes; a beneficial result does not cure a consent failure.
- **Value:** people in custody, institutions, the military or deep poverty need stronger protection, because the setting itself constrains choice.
- **Value:** states that ran or funded such programmes owe acknowledgement and redress, and the burden of proof for compensation should not rest wholly on aged survivors.
- **Value:** allegations about adversary states deserve the same evidentiary standard as findings about one's own.

## 8. Proposed solutions (sourced, evidence-rated)

1. **Independent ethics review for all human research.** Evidence: **Limited.** A systematic review found IRB review is widespread but its effect on participant protection is poorly measured [83].
2. **Improve consent comprehension (extended discussion, plain language).** Evidence: **Moderate.** Systematic reviews of randomised trials found extended person-to-person discussion and improved forms raise understanding; multimedia alone shows mixed results [84] [85].
3. **Mandatory trial registration and results reporting.** Evidence: **Moderate** for transparency; effect on consent **UNTESTED** [86].
4. **Consent and governance for biospecimens (broad consent, family or community roles).** Evidence: **Limited.** People generally accept broad consent if asked and informed [87]; the HeLa model is a single case [18].
5. **A national no-fault compensation system for research injury.** Evidence: **UNTESTED** in the US; the Presidential Commission recommended study and pilots [88].
6. **Proactive, state-initiated redress for sterilisation survivors (no individual proof burden).** Evidence: **UNTESTED.** The Swedish 1999 law required a personal application by a statutory deadline and made payment depend on proving eligibility [22]; Japan's 2024 compensation law is likewise claim-based [62].
7. **Continuing duty to warn and treat former subjects of military and state research.** Evidence: **UNTESTED** as to health effect; now a legal duty in one jurisdiction [40].
