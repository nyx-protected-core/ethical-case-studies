# Falsified Reports, Research, Data and Technology Findings

Date: 19 November 2026
Author: anonymous

## Summary

This case collects documented falsification from around the world: made-up or altered research data, clinical trial records, product and safety test results, company accounts, official statistics, forensic evidence and claims about what a technology can do. It is organised by domain. Each item has a type label:

- **FINDING**: a conviction, guilty plea, court judgment, regulator or institutional misconduct finding, or a journal retraction for fabricated or manipulated data.
- **SETTLED-NO-ADMISSION**: a settlement or order accepted without admitting the facts, or with an express statement that liability was not determined.
- **ALLEGATION**: a claim that was not proven, is still being tried, was reversed, or was settled without a liability finding. Its current status is always given.
- **REPORTED (SECONDARY)**: an outcome described in news reporting where the court record itself was not opened. It is not a FINDING.

Court, regulator, inquiry and institutional records are cited as records. Analysis comes from peer-reviewed work. News is used only where no primary record could be opened, and is marked SECONDARY. Every figure issued by a government body carries a [GOV-flag] note on its reporting incentive. People named here are described only as far as the cited records go. Where a person disputes a finding, that is stated.

**Overall evidence strength.** Strong for the guilty pleas, convictions, regulator orders and the full institutional reports that were opened (Bell Labs, the Stapel committees, the Japanese Society of Anesthesiologists). Moderate where only a press release or summary of a longer report could be opened (for example Seoul National University, RIKEN through the *Nature* retraction note, Karolinska Institutet and Duke). Weak for outcomes known only from news, which are labelled REPORTED (SECONDARY).

## How falsification is defined and detected

**Definition.** The US Office of Research Integrity (ORI) defines research misconduct as "fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism in proposing, performing, or reviewing research, or in reporting research results." **Fabrication** "is making up data or results and recording or reporting them." **Falsification** "is manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes, or changing or omitting data or results such that the research is not accurately represented in the research record." **Plagiarism** is taking another person's ideas, processes, results or words "without giving appropriate credit." Research misconduct "does not include honest error or differences of opinion" [R1]. This case uses fabrication and falsification in the same sense outside research too: test results, accounts or statistics that were made up, or altered so they no longer show what happened.

**How common it is.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of surveys found that a pooled weighted average of 1.97% of scientists admitted they had "fabricated, falsified or modified data or results at least once", and up to 33.7% admitted other questionable research practices [P1]. A review of all 2,047 biomedical and life-science articles that PubMed listed as retracted on 3 May 2012 found that 67.4% of retractions were due to misconduct: fraud or suspected fraud (43.4%), duplicate publication (14.2%) and plagiarism (9.8%). Only 21.3% were due to error. The authors also found that "incomplete, uninformative or misleading retraction announcements" had led to the role of fraud being underestimated [P2].

**How it was caught in these cases.**
- Duplicated or impossible figures and data: Schön [R2], Lesné [R10] and Obokata [R6].
- Statistical analysis of published data: Fujii, where a journal analysis of 168 papers found patient characteristics bunched unnaturally [R17], and the Stapel committees [R5].
- Independent re-testing: Nissan's own tests of Mitsubishi minicars [N3], and the transport ministry's re-tests [R27].
- Insiders and whistleblowers: qui tam suits at Duke and Ranbaxy [R14, R20], the junior researchers who reported Stapel [R5b], and the Olympus chief executive who challenged his board [N2].
- Outside data analysts: the Data Colada researchers, whose allegations started the Harvard Business School process [R11].
- Regulator reviews and audits: the Greek deficit figures [R34], Argentina's CPI and GDP [R35], Phoenix VA wait times [R37] and the FBI hair review [R40].

## 1. Scientific and medical research misconduct

**FINDING: Hendrik Schön, Bell Labs (US, 2002).** A committee formed by Bell Labs management examined 24 final allegations about 25 papers involving 20 coauthors. It found that Schön "committed scientific misconduct as defined by the falsification or fabrication of data" in 16 of the 24 cases, "intentionally or recklessly and without the knowledge of any of his coauthors." It said he "showed reckless disregard for the sanctity of data," and noted that the primary data files no longer existed [R2]. Contemporary reporting says Bell Labs management had noticed problems in autumn 2001 but put them down to sloppiness [N10 SECONDARY].

**FINDING: Hwang Woo-suk, Seoul National University (South Korea, 2005–06).** The university's investigation committee found that the data in the 2005 *Science* paper, "including test results from DNA fingerprinting, photographs of teratoma, embryoid bodies, MHC-HLA isotype matches and karyotyping have all been fabricated." It found that the team "does not possess patient-specific stem cell lines or any scientific bases for claiming having created one." It also found that the NT-1 cell line in the 2004 paper "was not derived from nuclear transfer" as claimed [R3]. *Science* published an editorial retraction in January 2006 [R4]. **REPORTED (SECONDARY):** in 2009 a Seoul court gave Hwang a two-year prison term suspended for three years, but found him not guilty of fraud over research funding [N1]. The court record was not opened. A criminal fraud acquittal and an institutional fabrication finding use different standards and answer different questions (see Where people disagree).

**FINDING: Diederik Stapel (Netherlands, 2011–12).** Three committees (Levelt, Noort and Drenth) reviewed all of Stapel's publications. They counted a publication as fraudulent if fraud was "proven beyond reasonable doubt" in at least one study, and they "encountered a total of fifty-five publications in which fraud has been established" [R5]. A committee rejoinder adds that 10 more papers had "serious evidence of fraud", that 10 dissertations he supervised were at least partly fraudulent, and that "young whistleblowers" finally exposed the fraud after 70 coauthors, many reviewers and 18 PhD committees had noticed nothing [R5b].

**FINDING: Haruko Obokata and the STAP papers, RIKEN (Japan, 2014).** The retraction note in *Nature* says that critical errors "led to an in-depth investigation by the RIKEN Institute. The RIKEN investigation committee has categorized some of the errors as misconduct." The authors listed further errors as well, and both papers were retracted [R6].

**FINDING: Dong-Pyou Han (US, 2015).** Han worked on an HIV vaccine. The federal court judgment sentenced him to 57 months on each of two counts, to run concurrently, and ordered him to repay $7,216,890.12 to the NIH [R7] [GOV-flag: a court-ordered restitution figure based on the prosecution's loss case].

**FINDING: Paolo Macchiarini and the synthetic trachea papers (Sweden, 2018 and 2023).** On 25 June 2018 the President of Karolinska Institutet found seven researchers responsible for scientific misconduct in six articles, including articles in *The Lancet*. Macchiarini was one of the main authors [R8]. In 2023 the Svea Court of Appeal convicted the surgeon of three counts of gross assault over synthetic trachea transplants carried out in 2011–12, and sentenced him to two years and six months in prison [R9]. The criminal case concerned harm to patients, not the falsified papers.

**FINDING (disputed by one author): the Aβ\*56 paper, Lesné et al. (US; published 2006, retracted 2024).** *Nature* retracted the paper on 24 June 2024. It said figures "show signs of excessive manipulation, including splicing, duplication and the use of an eraser tool" and that "the data cannot be verified from the records." Six coauthors agreed with the retraction, "Sylvain Lesné disagrees with the retraction", and one author did not respond [R10].

**FINDING (disputed by the researcher): Francesca Gino, Harvard Business School (US, 2023–26).** The allegations began with three researchers who publish as Data Colada. In January 2025 a Harvard hearing committee "found that there was clear and convincing evidence that Plaintiff's research misconduct constituted grave misconduct or neglect of duty", and in May 2025 Harvard revoked her tenure. On 2 November 2026 a federal court granted Harvard's motion for summary judgment "as to all of Plaintiff's claims" [R11]. Her separate defamation claims against the Data Colada researchers were dismissed in 2024 [N9 SECONDARY]. Gino disputes the findings, which is why she sued (see Where people disagree).

**FINDING on the data; ALLEGATION as to who altered it: the 2012 "signing first" paper (US).** *PNAS* retracted the 2012 paper by Shu et al. [R12]. Coauthor Dan Ariely has published a statement approved by Duke University. It says that Duke's investigation committee "found no evidence to support claims that I falsified data or knowingly used falsified data" in that paper, but determined that he "should have done more to prevent faulty data from being published" [R13]. This is the subject's own account, approved by the university. The full Duke report was not opened. No finding about who altered the data was found.

**SETTLED-NO-ADMISSION: Duke University grants (US, 2019).** Duke agreed to pay $112.5 million to resolve allegations that grant applications and progress reports to the NIH and EPA contained "falsified or fabricated" data from a research technician's mouse studies in its Airway Physiology Laboratory, across 30 grants between 2006 and 2018. The DOJ release states: "The claims settled by this agreement are allegations only, and there has been no determination of liability." The case began as a whistleblower suit by a former Duke employee [R14] [GOV-flag: a settlement amount announced by the prosecuting agency].

## 2. Clinical and pharmaceutical trial data

**FINDING: Andrew Wakefield and the 1998 *Lancet* MMR paper (UK).** PubMed lists the 1998 paper as a "Retracted Publication." The *Lancet* retraction was published on 6 February 2010 [R15]. On 24 May 2010 a General Medical Council panel found serious professional misconduct, including that it was "dishonest and misleading" of him not to disclose information to the Legal Aid Board. The panel imposed erasure from the medical register [R16].

**FINDING: Yoshitaka Fujii (Japan, 2012).** Fujii was an anaesthesiologist. The Japanese Society of Anesthesiologists reviewed 212 of his papers and found 3 with no fabrication, 172 fabricated and 37 that could not be judged. It found the fabrication ran for 19 years, from 1993 to 2011, and that in most papers no patients or animals existed and no drugs were given: papers were written "as if writing a novel" (translated) [R17]. In October 2012 the Society reclassified one paper, which changed the totals to 171 fabricated and 38 undetermined. Its reason was that, under the education ministry's guidelines, an admission by the accused cannot be the only evidence of misconduct [R18].

**FINDING (ORI), settled without admission: Anil Potti, Duke (US, 2015).** ORI found research misconduct: Potti reported cancer-genomics predictors and their validation "by disregarding accepted scientific methodology so that false data were reported" in papers including the *New England Journal of Medicine* and *Nature Medicine*. Those papers were retracted after Duke's investigation. He signed a Voluntary Settlement Agreement and "neither admits nor denies ORI's findings" [R19].

**FINDING: Ranbaxy (India and US, 2013).** Ranbaxy USA pleaded guilty to three felony counts under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and to "four felony counts of knowingly making material false statements to the FDA" about drugs made at two plants in India. It paid a $130 million criminal fine, $20 million in forfeiture, and $350 million to settle civil claims. The civil case began as a whistleblower suit [R20] [GOV-flag: amounts announced by DOJ, the prosecuting agency].

**FINDING: Purdue Pharma (US, 2020).** Purdue pleaded guilty to three felonies. As part of the plea it admitted that from May 2007 to at least March 2017 it "represented to the DEA that it maintained an effective anti-diversion program when, in fact," it did not. The penalties included a criminal fine of $3.544 billion and $2 billion in criminal forfeiture [R21] [GOV-flag: headline amounts announced by DOJ; how much was actually paid depended on the forfeiture and crediting terms described in the same release]. **ALLEGATION, settled:** the Sackler family paid $225 million to resolve civil False Claims Act liability. The DOJ release states that the resolutions "do not include the criminal release of any individuals, including members of the Sackler family" [R21]. No individual family member pleaded guilty in this resolution.

## 3. Corporate and engineering test results

**FINDING: Volkswagen diesel defeat device (Germany and US, 2017).** VW agreed to plead guilty to three felony counts and pay a $2.8 billion criminal penalty for selling about 590,000 diesel vehicles in the US "by using a defeat device to cheat on emissions tests," and for lying and obstructing justice. It also paid $1.5 billion in civil resolutions [R22] [GOV-flag: amounts announced by DOJ].

**FINDING: Takata airbag inflators (Japan and US, 2017).** Takata agreed to plead guilty to wire fraud and pay $1 billion: a $25 million fine, a $125 million fund for injured people and an $850 million fund for carmakers. The head of DOJ's Fraud Section said the company "repeatedly and systematically falsified critical test data related to the safety of its products" for more than a decade [R23] [GOV-flag: amounts and characterisation from the prosecuting agency].

**FINDING (admitted facts), charge later dismissed: Boeing 737 MAX (US, 2021–25).** In a 2021 deferred prosecution agreement, "Boeing admitted in court documents" that two of its 737 MAX Flight Technical Pilots deceived the FAA's Aircraft Evaluation Group about MCAS. As a result, a key FAA document, and the manuals and pilot training based on it, lacked information about MCAS. The total monetary amount was over $2.5 billion, including a $243.6 million penalty [R24] [GOV-flag]. In November 2025 the court granted the government's motion to dismiss the charge after DOJ signed a non-prosecution agreement with Boeing. Some victims' families opposed the dismissal [R25].

**FINDING: Kobe Steel product data (Japan, 2019).** Prosecutors indicted Kobe Steel under the Unfair Competition Prevention Act over its product misconduct. The Tachikawa Summary Court fined the company 100 million yen. The company accepted the decision and pointed to its March 2018 "Report on Misconduct in Kobe Steel Group" [R26].

**FINDING: Mitsubishi Motors fuel economy (Japan, 2016).** After the falsification came to light, the transport ministry ran its own tests on four minicar models. None of the vehicles reached its listed fuel economy; the shortfalls ranged from about 5% to about 16%, averaging about 11% [R27] [GOV-flag: regulator's own test results, published after a scandal]. Mitsubishi admitted that employees had falsified the data. The problem came to light after Nissan's own tests did not match Mitsubishi's figures [N3 SECONDARY].

## 4. Audits and financial reporting

**FINDING: Enron (US).** A jury found former chief executive Jeffrey Skilling "guilty of 19 counts," and the district court sentenced him to 292 months. In 2010 the Supreme Court rejected his fair-trial argument but vacated the honest-services conspiracy conviction, because that statute covers only bribes and kickbacks, and sent the case back to the lower court [R28] [GOV-flag: sentence imposed by the court]. The SEC also charged Kenneth Lay, Richard Causey and others [R30].

**ALLEGATION (conviction reversed): Arthur Andersen.** Andersen, Enron's auditor, told its employees to destroy documents, and a jury convicted it of obstruction. In 2005 the Supreme Court reversed the conviction because "the jury instructions failed to convey properly the elements" of the offence [R29].

**ALLEGATION (SEC complaint) and REPORTED (SECONDARY) conviction: WorldCom (US).** In June 2002 the SEC charged WorldCom with "a massive accounting fraud totaling more than $3.8 billion," alleging that pre-tax income was overstated by about $3.055 billion in 2001 and $797 million in the first quarter of 2002 [R31] [GOV-flag: figures alleged in an initial complaint]. Former chief executive Bernard Ebbers was reported as sentenced to 25 years for what reports called an $11 billion fraud [N4 SECONDARY]. The court record was not opened.

**SETTLED-NO-ADMISSION: Satyam (India, 2011).** The SEC alleged that former senior managers created "more than 6,000 phony invoices," backed by bogus bank statements, which produced more than $1 billion in fictitious cash and cash-related balances. Satyam settled without admitting or denying the allegations and paid a $10 million penalty [R32] [GOV-flag]. The release notes that a criminal trial was then under way in India.

**REPORTED (SECONDARY): Olympus (Japan, 2013).** News reports say three former executives pleaded guilty to falsifying accounts to hide losses of $1.7 billion and received suspended sentences, and that Olympus was fined 700 million yen. The scandal came to light in 2011 after chief executive Michael Woodford was dismissed for challenging the board over unusually large payments [N2]. The court record was not opened.

**FINDING (regulator recommendation): Toshiba (Japan, 2015).** Japan's Securities and Exchange Surveillance Commission recommended an administrative monetary penalty of 7,373,500,000 yen for false statements in Toshiba's annual securities reports [R33] [GOV-flag: the regulator's recommended amount; the final payment order was not opened].

**ALLEGATION (criminal trial ongoing): Wirecard (Germany).** In June 2020 Wirecard's own management board said that 1.9 billion euros in trust account balances were likely not to exist, and the company applied for insolvency. The criminal trial of former chief executive Markus Braun and two co-defendants began in Munich on 8 December 2022 and had no verdict as of 12 November 2026. Braun denies the charges. In civil proceedings the court declared the 2017 and 2018 accounts void in 2022, but that judgment was not final [N5 SECONDARY].

## 5. Government and official statistics

**FINDING: Greece deficit statistics (EU, 2010).** The European Commission report on Greek government deficit and debt statistics found "severe irregularities in the EDP notifications of April and October 2009, including submission of incorrect data." It called one item "a case of deliberate misreporting of figures." In October 2009 the reported 2008 deficit was revised from 5.0% to 7.7% of GDP, and the planned 2009 deficit from 3.7% to 12.5% [R34] [GOV-flag: figures from the Commission, which oversees member-state deficit reporting; the original figures were reported by national authorities whose standing under EU deficit rules depended on them, and the report itself mentions "budgetary slippages in an electoral year"].

**FINDING (inaccuracy, not proven falsification): Argentina CPI and GDP (IMF, 2013–16).** On 1 February 2013 the IMF Executive Board found that Argentina's remedial progress on "the inaccuracy of CPI-GBA and GDP data" was insufficient, and issued a declaration of censure for breach of its obligations to the Fund [R35] [GOV-flag]. The IMF later removed the censure, in November 2016 [R36]. The IMF finding is about inaccurate data, not a ruling on intent.

**FINDING: Phoenix VA wait times (US, 2014).** The VA Inspector General found 1,700 veterans waiting for primary care who were not on the electronic wait list. In a sample, veterans had waited an average of 115 days for a first appointment, against the 24 days Phoenix had reported [R37] [GOV-flag: the inspector's own sample; the original figures were self-reported by the facility being measured].

**FINDING: Atlanta Public Schools test scores (US, 2011).** A Georgia special investigation of the 2009 state tests found cheating in 44 of 56 schools examined, and found that 178 teachers and principals cheated, 82 of whom confessed. It said thousands of children were denied remedial education because of inflated scores [R38] [GOV-flag: investigators appointed by the state]. **REPORTED (SECONDARY):** in 2015, 11 former educators were found guilty of racketeering [N8].

## 6. Forensic and laboratory evidence

**FINDING: Annie Dookhan, Hinton State Laboratory (US, 2013–17).** On 20 April 2017 the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court announced that "21,587 cases affected by the misconduct of chemist Annie Dookhan" had been ordered dismissed, following *Bridgeman v. District Attorney for the Suffolk District*, 476 Mass. 298 (2017) [R39] [GOV-flag: a case count from the court, based on lists from the prosecutors]. **REPORTED (SECONDARY):** Dookhan pleaded guilty in 2013 and was sentenced to three to five years [N6, N7].

**FINDING (erroneous testimony, not proven falsification): FBI microscopic hair analysis (US, 2015).** In a review with the Justice Department and others, the FBI reported that in 268 trials where examiners gave testimony used against a defendant, "erroneous statements were made in 257 (96 percent) of the cases." Errors were found in 33 of the at least 35 cases where the defendant received the death penalty [R40] [GOV-flag: the agency reviewing its own examiners' work]. These were overstatements of what the science could show, not proven deliberate falsification.

## 7. Technology product claims

**FINDING: Theranos (US, 2018–22).** In 2018 the SEC charged Theranos, Elizabeth Holmes and Ramesh Balwani with raising more than $700 million through false statements. The SEC said the analyzer "could complete only a small number of tests." Holmes settled, paying a $500,000 penalty and accepting a 10-year officer-and-director bar [R41] [GOV-flag]. In the criminal case Balwani was convicted on all twelve counts. The court sentenced Holmes to 135 months on 18 November 2022 and Balwani to 155 months on 7 December 2022 [R42].

**FINDING, then pardoned: Nikola (US, 2021–25).** Nikola paid a $125 million penalty without admitting or denying the SEC's findings that it misled investors about its products and technology [R43] [GOV-flag]. Founder Trevor Milton was convicted at trial of securities and wire fraud and sentenced in December 2023 to four years [R44]. He received a presidential pardon dated 27 March 2025 [R45]. A pardon ends the punishment; it is not a finding that the conduct did not happen.

## Patterns across cases

1. **Incentives.** Reported figures were tied to rewards in many of these cases: performance pay (Phoenix wait times were used in records for awards and pay [R37]), school ratings (Atlanta [R38]), stock prices (Nikola, WorldCom, Satyam [R43, R31, R32]), grant funding (Duke, Han [R14, R7]) and production schedules (Takata [R23]). Publication pressure and high-impact findings feature in Schön, Hwang, Stapel and Fujii [R2, R3, R5, R17].
2. **Failed oversight.** Peer review, coauthors and doctoral committees missed Stapel's fraud for years [R5b]. Bell Labs first put Schön's problems down to sloppiness [N10]. EU statistical rules assumed good faith: "deliberate misreporting or fraud is not foreseen in the regulation" [R34]. Auditors failed at Satyam, and the SEC charged the audit firm's Indian affiliates [R32].
3. **Missing raw data.** Schön's raw files no longer existed [R2]. The Drenth committee had no original data [R5]. *Nature* said the Aβ\*56 data "cannot be verified from the records" [R10].
4. **Whistleblowers and outside checkers.** Many of these cases were exposed by people outside the chain of command: junior researchers (Stapel), qui tam relators (Duke, Ranbaxy), a competitor running its own tests (Nissan), a chief executive who was fired (Olympus), and independent data analysts (Data Colada).
5. **Slow correction.** Wakefield's paper was retracted 12 years after publication [R15]. The Aβ\*56 paper was retracted 18 years after [R10]. Fujii's fabrication ran for 19 years [R17].

## Where people disagree

- **Lesné:** six coauthors accepted the retraction and Lesné did not [R10].
- **Gino:** Harvard found misconduct, and the court ruled for Harvard on all her claims [R11]. Gino disputes the findings. The court ruling concerned her contract and discrimination claims, not a retrial of the research.
- **Ariely:** the retraction shows the data were invalid [R12]. Duke, as reported in a statement it approved, found no evidence that Ariely falsified them [R13]. No public finding says who did.
- **Hwang:** the university found fabrication [R3], but the criminal court acquitted him of fraud over research funding [N1]. Different standards of proof and different charges explain this, but people still disagree about what it means.
- **Argentina:** the IMF found inaccurate data and a breach of obligations [R35], not intent. Whether this amounts to falsification is a matter of judgment.
- **FBI hair:** the review found erroneous testimony [R40]. Whether this was falsification or systemic overstatement is debated. It is not labelled falsification here.
- **Boeing:** the government said the non-prosecution agreement "secures meaningful accountability." The court granted dismissal but recorded its own concerns about accountability, and some families opposed it [R25].
- **Milton:** convicted by a jury [R44] and later pardoned [R45].
- **Stapel and social psychology:** professional associations criticised the committees' implications for the whole field, not the facts. The committees replied that they were "unable to make any statement" about social psychology as a whole [R5b].

## Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Require raw data and lab records to be kept and deposited.** Evidence: *limited*. This rests on case records: Schön's raw files no longer existed [R2], and the Drenth committee had no original data [R5]. UNTESTED as a policy.
2. **Screen submitted clinical trial data statistically.** Evidence: *limited*. A journal's statistical analysis led to the Fujii findings [R17]. The effect of routine screening is UNTESTED.
3. **Write retraction notices that say why.** Evidence: *moderate*. Unclear notices led to fraud being underestimated [P2].
4. **Protect and reward whistleblowers, including through qui tam actions.** Evidence: *limited*. Whistleblowers started the Duke and Ranbaxy recoveries [R14, R20]. Whether stronger protections increase reporting is UNTESTED here.
5. **Do not tie pay or ratings to figures the measured unit reports about itself without independent checks.** Evidence: *limited*. See the Phoenix VA [R37] and Atlanta [R38] cases. UNTESTED.
6. **Have regulators re-test products independently instead of relying on self-certification.** Evidence: *limited*. Re-testing confirmed the Mitsubishi shortfalls [R27]. UNTESTED as a general rule.
7. **Make statistical agencies independent and assume misreporting can happen.** Evidence: *limited*. The Commission found that "deliberate misreporting or fraud is not foreseen in the regulation" [R34]. UNTESTED.
