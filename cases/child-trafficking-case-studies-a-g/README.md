## Part A: How children in Canada and the US end up trafficked

What the peer-reviewed research says about the real pathways into child trafficking, which policies protect children or fail them, why state counts measure detection as much as trafficking, and where the evidence is thin.

> **How to read the flags.** Evidence is peer-reviewed research only; laws are cited as laws. Any figure produced by a government agency, including peer-reviewed studies built on agency records, carries the tag [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives]. Sections 1 to 9 use no Canadian data or research outside section 3b, which is limited to Canadian institutions and research. ### 1. The question

How do children in Canada and the United States actually come to be trafficked, for sex or for labour, and which specific policies are shown to protect them or to fail them?

The case asks for the mechanisms that the research can show, not the ones that are easiest to picture. A recurring finding is that most exploited children are reached through people and systems already around them, not by strangers [13][63][9][10][11].

### 2. Background and definitions

- **Child sex trafficking (US):** Causing a person under 18 to engage in a commercial sex act. For a minor, no proof of force, fraud or coercion is required [L1]. Surveys of youth who trade sex therefore overlap heavily with the legal category, though not with the popular image of trafficking [44][45].
- **Child labour trafficking (US):** Forced labour still requires proof of force, fraud or coercion, even for minors [L6]. This is one reason labour cases are under-recorded [54].
- **Trafficking of a minor (Canada):** Recruiting, transporting, harbouring or controlling a person under 18 for exploitation; consent is not a defence [L4].
- **Familial trafficking:** Trafficking in which a parent or other relative is the trafficker or facilitator [10][11].
- **Sextortion:** Threats to expose sexual images to force a victim to provide more images, sex or other favours [12].
- **Safe harbor law:** A state law that treats exploited minors as victims rather than offenders, by decriminalisation, diversion to services, or both [17][18].

### 3. Evidence by angle

> **Risk markers, not predictors.** Maltreatment, running away and system involvement are the best-replicated markers of risk. Nearly all the evidence is retrospective or from agency records, and most children with these experiences are never trafficked. They identify groups to protect; they cannot screen individual children on their own.

| Pathway | What the research finds | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Family abuse and breakdown | Among 273 people aged 16 and over in the sex trade in one Midwestern state, childhood sexual and emotional abuse, rape and family members in sex work were linked to entry before 18 in unadjusted analysis; after adjustment, running away and minority status remained. In Florida, sexual abuse was the strongest predictor of a trafficking report: odds 2.52 times higher for girls and 8.21 times for boys. A Florida study of 40,531 justice-involved youth (801 suspected or verified victims) again linked childhood adversity to victimisation [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives, both Florida studies]. Reviews list maltreatment, caregiver strain, family violence and conflict with parents. | [1][2][3][4][52] |
| Running away and homelessness | Running away is the most consistent marker across designs. In national survey data, sex exchange as a minor was linked to having run away (adjusted odds 2.04), substance use (1.87) and community violence. Rates of trafficking among homeless youth range from about 9% to about 41% depending on the sample and the screening tool: 8.8% of assessments with a validated tool at a New Jersey shelter, 9.6% in a related analysis at the same shelter (likely overlapping youth), 39.9% in a community survey in metro Atlanta, and 41.2% in a Kentucky and Indiana convenience sample. These samples are mostly young adults (ages 18-22 in New Jersey, 14-25 in Atlanta, 12-25 in Kentucky and Indiana), and the trafficking they report may have happened after age 18, so the rates are not child-trafficking rates. A trafficking-specific tool found more exploitation than a standard assessment. A supportive adult was linked to lower odds. | [46][48][7][47][6][49] |
| Labour exploitation of homeless youth and young adults | Among homeless youth and young adults aged 14-25 in the Atlanta survey, coerced labour (29.3%) and fraud (25.2%) while homeless were more common than commercial sexual exploitation (15.6%). At the New Jersey shelter, 46.7% of positive assessments (14 of 30, ages 18-22) involved labour trafficking. How much of this happened before 18 is not reported. Labour trafficking of minors in domestic service, factories, restaurants, peddling and farms is easily mistaken for ordinary work. | [47][48][54] |
| Foster care and child-welfare failures | In Florida records on 3,420 children with trafficking allegations, those children were more than twice as likely to have prior maltreatment and, among those known to child welfare, more than 10 times as likely to have run from placement. Of about 37,000 Florida youth placed at age 10 or older, 19% ran away at least once, and 7% of those had a trafficking allegation while on the run; for 70% of them the first allegation came during a runaway episode. Nearly half of 9,300 Florida children with a trafficking allegation were involved with child welfare at the time. [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; allegation data] | [9][50][51] |
| Online grooming and sextortion | In a national survey of young adults about their childhood, 3.5% had experienced sextortion, 5.4% online grooming by adults, and 1.7% online commercial sexual exploitation; peak vulnerability was 13-17, and most offenders were dating partners, friends and acquaintances rather than strangers. In a national sample of 5,568 US middle and high school students, about 5% had been victims of sextortion and about 3% admitted threatening others. Among minor sextortion victims in another sample, about 60% knew the perpetrator in person and half told no one. | [13][53][12] |
| Familial trafficking | In a pilot sample of 31 child-welfare-involved children with familial sex trafficking, referred for behavioural-health care in one state, family members often traded the child for illicit drugs (exact shares UNVERIFIED: full text not opened). Family-facilitated cases show more extensive maltreatment and features that block detection [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; child-welfare and case records]. | [10][11] |
| Labour and debt trafficking (wider evidence) | In San Diego, 30% of unauthorised migrant workers surveyed met the definition of labour trafficking, with immigration status the main vulnerability (adults). A systematic review found the European labour-trafficking evidence base thin and exploratory. Men, women and children in post-trafficking services in Cambodia, Thailand and Vietnam commonly reported threats (47%), violence (48%) and long hours; the abstract does not give child-only figures. | [14][15][16] |
| Institutions: schools, sport, churches, care homes | Abusers in organisations groom the child, the family and the institution itself. In one sample of 41 professional perpetrators, about 15% said they chose their job only to reach children and 41.5% said it was part of the reason. In the Netherlands and Belgium, 14% of adults who played organised sport as children reported sexual violence there. US Catholic Church records for 1950-2002, compiled for the US bishops' conference, showed 4,392 priests with abuse allegations and 10,667 people who made allegations (allegations, not adjudicated findings). A study of 361 US school-employee misconduct cases reported in the media in 2014 documents the school pathway. | [25][26][27][28][29][30][31][32] |

> **Caveat on the Florida foster-care data.** Two explanations fit these numbers and current research cannot separate them. Children in care may face more real risk, through maltreatment, unstable placements and group settings. They are also far more watched: since 2014 federal law has required child-welfare agencies to identify, document and report sex trafficking among children in their care [L2], so a trafficked child outside any system is much less likely to produce an allegation. The authors state their data are not a true prevalence [9]. No peer-reviewed source supports a specific national share of trafficked children who come from foster care, so this page gives none.

Groups already pushed to the margins carry more risk. In one five-city sample of people aged 16 and over in the sex trade in a Midwestern state, those trafficked as minors were more likely to be racial or ethnic minorities than those who were not [1]. Among homeless young adults in Arizona, LGBTQ+ youth had about twice the odds of having been sex trafficked [8]; in Atlanta, transgender and gender-nonconforming youth were among those most likely to report trafficking [47]. These studies do not test why; marginalisation, homelessness and lack of a safe adult are plausible explanations, and none of the findings point to any group being a source of traffickers.

### 3a. State counts versus state policies

> **A higher count does not mean more trafficking.** A recorded case needs three things: the trafficking happens, someone detects it, and it is recorded as trafficking rather than something else. The last two vary by state, so recorded counts measure how hard a state looks as much as how much trafficking happens there. Ranking states by raw counts can reward the states that look least.

#### What drives a state's recorded count

| Policy or practice in a state | Effect on recorded counts | What it says about real trafficking | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comprehensive trafficking law with funding, task forces, police training, victim assistance and mandatory reporting | Associated with more trafficking arrests and prosecutions in a 50-state panel, 2003-2012; the political environment at enactment also mattered [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; agency records] | Nothing directly. The policy itself produces recorded cases | [38] |
| Steps to prepare officers to identify and respond | Training and related preparation raised the odds that an agency identified any case; fewer than 10% of US agencies identified a case in 2000-2006 | Low counts in untrained areas are likely under-detection | [36] |
| Agency leaders who believe trafficking is present locally | Linked to much higher odds of identifying cases | Belief drives looking; looking drives counts | [36] |
| Reliance on victims coming forward | Victims often do not trust police and rarely seek their help | Counts depend on proactive policing and outreach | [55] |
| New trafficking statutes with few prosecutions | Few investigated cases are prosecuted as trafficking, and suspects are rarely convicted of trafficking offences [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; agency records] | Trafficking can be present yet drop out of trafficking-specific counts | [37][39] |
| Trafficking-specific screening tools | Identify more sex and labour exploitation than general assessments | Agencies that screen will record more | [48][49] |
| Child-welfare screening mandates and hotline screening (Florida example) | Generate large administrative counts of allegations | Allegations, not confirmed cases [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives] | [2][9][L2] |
| Safe harbor laws | A county-level quasi-experiment found fewer overall and juvenile prostitution arrests in safe harbor states, but the promised services had largely not appeared; an earlier four-state analysis found arrests may not fall. Without funded services some providers kept detaining youth | Juvenile arrest counts shift with the legal regime, not with how many children are exploited [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; arrest and maltreatment records] | [56][57][17] |

#### The few true-prevalence estimates, and why they cannot rank states

| Place | Type of estimate | Figure | What it can and cannot show | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| United States | Scoping review of all US prevalence studies | Only six empirical studies (1999-2017) gave a count or prevalence estimate | Different populations and definitions; the authors say to cite them cautiously | [43] |
| Minnesota | Statewide student survey, 2019 (N = 71,007) | 1.4% of high school students reported trading sex; 5.9% of transgender students and 3.1% of Native students | Closest to population prevalence for enrolled students; misses youth out of school; trading sex is not the same as third-party trafficking [GOV FIGURE: state-run survey, self-report] | [44] |
| United States (national) | Nationally representative survey, 1990s data (N = 13,294) | 3.5% of adolescents had ever exchanged sex for drugs or money; two-thirds were boys | A prevalence proxy, but decades old and school-based | [45] |
| New Orleans metro area | Multiple-systems estimate from eight agencies' lists, 2016 [GOV FIGURE (partly): possible reporting incentives; includes police lists] | About 1,000 trafficked people (95% interval 650 to 1,600) against 185 known to agencies, roughly five times more | All ages and all forms, not children only; suggests known cases are a small fraction of the total, but the method is disputed | [58] |

The method is contested. Silverman, who built the UK government's estimate, showed that results can shift with how overlaps between agency lists are modelled and proposed a Bayesian approach [59]. Critics, simulating the UK estimate, argued model selection overstates reliability and concluded the method is unsuitable for estimating numbers of potential victims [60]. The original authors published a reply [61]. An independent review across all public datasets set out the conditions under which the estimates are biased and offered guidance rather than a verdict [62].

> **These numbers cannot support a state ranking.** They come from different methods, populations, definitions and years. No peer-reviewed source gives comparable true-prevalence estimates across states, and no peer-reviewed study found here shows that any state policy reduces the true incidence of child trafficking; the evaluations measure arrests, prosecutions and diversion [38][56][57]. Where population estimates exist, they suggest a low recorded count should be read as possible under-detection, not as reassurance [58].

### 4. Strongest concerns

- Child-welfare systems meant to protect children are where many identified victims are found, especially among those who run from group care [9][50] [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; Florida agency allegation records].
- Parents and relatives can be the traffickers; these cases show more extensive maltreatment and features that make them harder to detect [10][11].
- Most online sexual offences come from people the child knows, so stranger-danger messaging misses most of the risk [13].
- Offender registries cannot stop most abuse: over 95% of sexual-offence arrests in New York were of first-time sex offenders [23] [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; arrest records]. Background checks share the same limit [22].
- Institutions can be groomed alongside children, and their cultures can shield abusers [30][31].
- Labour exploitation is common in screening of homeless youth and young adults (ages 14-25 in Atlanta, 18-22 in New Jersey), yet official systems are built around sex trafficking. How much of it happens before age 18 is not reported [47][48][54].
- A county-level study found fewer juvenile prostitution arrests after safe harbor laws, though an earlier four-state analysis did not; either way, the services the laws promised mostly did not follow [56][57] [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; arrest records].

### 5. Counter-evidence

- One registry study did find a deterrent effect: about an 11% drop in first-time adult sex-crime arrests in South Carolina after the 1995 law [24] [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; arrest records]. The registry evidence is mixed, not uniformly negative.
- Safe harbor laws were seen by experts as a real shift, and a county-level study found they redirected young victims away from arrest [17][56].
- A supportive adult is a measurable protective factor among homeless youth, which gives prevention a concrete target [7].
- States that invest in training, task forces and victim assistance do identify and prosecute more cases [38] [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; agency records].
- Brief validated screening tools now exist for homeless young adults, with 86.7% sensitivity (76.5% specificity) in one validation among 18-22-year-olds with 30 positive assessments [48]. A paediatric policy statement sets out how health professionals can recognise and respond to child trafficking [5].

### 6. Where the evidence is weak

- Prevalence: only six US studies have estimated it, and popular global figures are often not evidence-based or verifiable [43][33][34]. Measurement is improving but still contested [35].
- Hidden-population methods such as multiple-systems estimation are disputed among statisticians [59][60][62].
- Many US studies rely on convenience samples and cross-sectional designs. They show associations, not causes [1][6][7].
- Agency data count allegations and detected cases, which depend on who looks [9][36]. The foster-care link cannot be sized from data the foster-care system itself produces [9][L2].
- Surveys of trading sex measure something that overlaps with, but is not the same as, third-party trafficking, and estimates differ several-fold depending on that line [44][45].
- Mandatory reporting: universal reporting laws did not raise confirmed physical-abuse reports and lowered the share confirmed [19]. About 37% of US children are investigated by child protective services by age 18 [21]. Both studies are peer-reviewed analyses of national agency records [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives], so whether broader reporting helps or overloads the system is unresolved.
- There is little outcome evaluation of prevention programmes against trafficking itself [35].

### 7. Policy options

**Supported by associations or expert review (none yet shown to reduce trafficking itself)**
- Fund services alongside safe harbor laws; decriminalisation alone is not enough [17][56]
- Report missing foster youth fast; US law requires reporting to police and the national missing-children centre within 24 hours [L2][50]
- Reduce group-care placements and placement instability, which are associated with trafficking allegations [9]
- Train police and set protocols, so detection rises where trafficking exists [36][38]
- Use trafficking-specific screening, including for labour exploitation, in shelters and health settings [48][49]
- Mentoring and supportive-adult programmes for homeless and runaway youth, since a supportive adult is linked to lower odds of trafficking; programmes themselves are unevaluated [7]
- Two-adult rules, supervision and organisational culture change in youth-serving bodies, recommended in reviews; outcome evaluations are lacking [31][32]
- Mandatory immediate reporting to both the US Center for SafeSport and law enforcement, with limits on one-on-one adult-minor contact, as US sport law now requires [L3]

**Weak or unproven as used today**
- Offender registries and background checks as the main safeguard, since most offenders have no record [22][23]
- Stranger-danger campaigns, given who commits most online offences [13]
- Ranking states by raw trafficking counts [36][38][43]
- Universal mandatory reporting, which did not raise confirmed reports and lowered the share confirmed [19]

### 8. Ethical analysis

Duty of care: when the state removes a child from home, it takes on the duty a parent had. The Florida data linking trafficking allegations to running from care [9][50] [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; agency allegation records] make system failure an ethical question, not only a technical one, even allowing for detection effects.

Justice: punishing exploited children as offenders contradicts the law's own definition of them as victims [L1][17].

Honesty in evidence: inflated or unverifiable statistics can misdirect funding and policy [33][34]. An ethics case should prefer smaller, verified numbers to larger, unverified ones, and should not rank places by counts that measure effort.

Fairness to groups: some groups carry measurably higher risk [1][8][44][47]. The studies identify these as groups to protect, not as sources of traffickers, and none shows that identity itself causes the risk. Blaming any group without evidence would pull attention from the pathways the research shows.

### 9. Open questions

- What is the true prevalence of child labour trafficking in North America?
- Which prevention programmes reduce trafficking itself, not only knowledge, referrals or arrests?
- Can states agree on a shared, detection-adjusted measure so that counts become comparable?
- How much of the foster-care link is real risk, and how much is closer watching?
- Can multiple-systems estimation be made reliable enough for local child-trafficking estimates?

## Part B: Sexuality and gender content in kindergarten and early primary

What developmental research, law and safeguarding science say about teaching sexuality and gender to 4 to 7 year olds in Canada and the US, including the strongest concerns, the counter-evidence, and where the evidence runs out.

### Source rules used on this page

> **How to read the flags.** Evidence is peer-reviewed research only; laws, court rulings and official policy documents are cited as documents, not as evidence of effects. Government figures or government-commissioned reports carry [GOV: possible reporting incentives]. News reports are labelled SECONDARY. Canadian research and data appear only in section 3b. Canadian laws and court rulings, which are documents rather than data, are also listed in the definitions, the timeline and the ethical analysis. Safeguarding standards on this page apply to every adult who works with children, whoever they are.

### 1. The question

What content about sexuality and gender is developmentally appropriate for children aged about 4 to 7 in Canadian and US classrooms? Who should decide, and what say should parents have?

### 2. Background and definitions

- **Body-safety education:** Teaching correct names for body parts, which touches are not okay, and how to tell a trusted adult. Often called sexual abuse prevention education [6][7].
- **Gender identity content:** Lessons or books that discuss a person's sense of being a boy, a girl, or neither, including that some people's identity differs from their sex.
- **Social transition:** A child living as a different gender through name, pronouns, clothing or hair, without medical treatment [20].
- **Opt-out and opt-in:** Opt-out: the child takes part unless a parent withdraws them. Opt-in: the child takes part only if a parent agrees in advance [L4][L7].
- **Grooming:** The deceptive process by which an abuser prepares a child, and often the family and institution, for abuse and prevents disclosure [10][13][14][51].

A Cochrane systematic review found school programmes raised children's protective knowledge and skills, with gains kept at 6 months and no rise in anxiety or fear. Whether they reduce abuse itself is unknown [7][8]. This is the best-evidenced sexuality-related content for young children.

### 3c. Safety concerns about gender identity in young children

> **What this section covers.** The concerns here are clinical, developmental and safeguarding concerns about how adults respond to a young child's gender identity. They are not claims about any group of people. Government-commissioned reviews and official policies carry their flags.

#### Desistance: most referred young children did not persist in the older cohorts

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| 77 children referred at ages 5 to 12 were followed up about 10 years later: 27% were classed as persisting, 43% as desisting, and 30% did not respond or could not be traced. Children who persisted had been more extremely gender-dysphoric in childhood | [52] |
| In a larger Dutch cohort of 127 children, 47 were classed as persisting; intensity of childhood distress and early social role transition were linked to persistence | [29] |
| In interviews with 25 adolescents diagnosed in childhood, both those whose gender dysphoria persisted and those in whom it remitted described ages 10 to 13 as crucial; changes in social environment, puberty and first attraction shaped their gender feelings | [53] |
| A critique raises methodological, theoretical, ethical and interpretive concerns about four frequently cited desistance studies and cautions against using them to ground care recommendations; replies from the Dutch study authors and from a second clinician dispute the critique | [54][59][55] |

This page's summary: no test at ages 4 to 7 has been shown to predict reliably which children will persist, although intensity of childhood distress and early social role transition are associated with persistence in one clinic cohort [29], and the wider desistance literature is contested [54][55][59]. Every cohort above predates the recent change in who is referred [26][27]. That uncertainty is the main reason for caution about early, fixed decisions.

#### Caution on early social transition

- The York systematic review [GOV: commissioned via the Cass Review, NHS England] found no robust evidence of benefit or harm from social transition in childhood [20]. A peer-reviewed critique reports a high risk of bias in the York reviews [23]; a peer-reviewed fact-check of critiques of the Review found multiple incorrect or uncontextualised claims [44]. Neither side reports a high-quality study of childhood social transition that the reviews missed.
- The Cass Review says that in an NHS setting it is important to view social transition as an active intervention, because it may have significant effects on the child or young person in terms of their psychological functioning and longer-term outcomes. It suggests that avoiding premature decisions and considering partial rather than full transition can keep options open [G1].
- The Cass Review [GOV: commissioned by NHS England; possible reporting incentives] recommended that when families are deciding on social transition for pre-pubertal children, services should ensure they can be seen as early as possible by a clinical professional with relevant experience [L15][G1]. This treats transition as a decision needing clinical input, not a routine school matter.
- Early social transition was associated with persistence in one Dutch clinic cohort [29]; whether it causes persistence is unknown [39]. A US follow-up of 317 already-transitioned children has no non-transition comparison group, so it cannot test this; it found 7.3% retransitioned at least once in about 5 years, with later cisgender identities more common when the first transition was before age 6 [30].

| Jurisdiction | Change | Source |

| Sweden (2022) | Risks of puberty blockers and hormones judged likely to outweigh benefits for most minors; use limited to exceptional cases or research [GOV: possible reporting incentives] | [L17] |

These shifts concern medical treatment, mostly of adolescents, and are evidence about that treatment, not about classroom or social-transition pathways for young children. In this page's view they are relevant because several European health systems now treat early, fixed pathways with caution [L16][L17][L24][L18]. Major guidelines elsewhere, including WPATH's Standards of Care 8, the Endocrine Society and the American Academy of Pediatrics, still support gender-affirming approaches; a systematic appraisal scored many gender-dysphoria guidelines poorly on rigour [42].

#### School transitions without parental knowledge

- There are no peer-reviewed studies of 4 to 7 year olds who transitioned at school without parental knowledge; every outcome study involves families who knew [20][30].
- England's draft guidance said schools should not automatically accommodate a request for social transition, and that parents should be involved except where that would put the child at significant risk of harm [L21]. In Feb 2026 the government said it would not issue standalone guidance and proposed moving this content into statutory safeguarding guidance, citing caution and clinical advice on social transition of pre-pubertal children and the involvement of parents wherever possible [L25].
- England's statutory guidance from Sep 2026 tells schools to avoid any suggestion that social transition is a simple solution to distress, and not to teach as fact that all people have a gender identity [L22].
- In the US, California bars districts from requiring staff to tell parents [L3], while federal courts have found parents likely to succeed against policies that keep a child's gender presentation at school from them [L8][L9]. The law is unsettled: a federal appeals court upheld a middle school's non-disclosure protocol against a parental due-process claim, and the Supreme Court declined review [L26]; it also declined an earlier case on standing, over a dissent [L27].
- Counter-evidence: in 129 transgender and gender-nonconforming youth in three US cities, using a chosen name in more settings was linked to lower depression and suicidality [34]. The sample is adolescents and young adults, so it does not test 4 to 7 year olds, and it does not separate parental knowledge from school use.

#### Safeguarding standards

The safeguarding standards in section 3 apply here unchanged: supervision, clear conduct codes, recorded decisions and transparency with parents, for every adult [15]. In England, statutory guidance sets safer-recruitment and allegations duties for all school staff [L23]. England's December 2023 draft guidance treated a decision about a child's name, pronouns or presentation as one to be recorded and shared with parents unless that would put the child at significant risk of harm [L21]; it was not issued as standalone guidance, and England later proposed putting similar content into statutory safeguarding guidance [L25]. Applying that rule elsewhere is this page's policy view.

> **Not a grooming claim.** These concerns are about clinical evidence, parental responsibility and record-keeping. Grooming is defined by an individual adult's deceptive conduct toward a child to enable sexual abuse [51][11][13]. No peer-reviewed study found for this page shows that gender identity, trans people, or staff who follow transition policies groom children (see the No group-based risk callout in section 3).

### 3d. Grooming tactics, warning signs and school safeguards

> **Applies to any adult, in any setting.** This section sets out what the safeguarding literature says grooming is and how it works, and the school safeguards that follow. The research defines grooming by behaviour, not by who the adult is. It applies equally to teachers, coaches, relatives, family friends, clergy, volunteers and older children, whatever their sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, religion or role.

Grooming is the deceptive process by which a would-be abuser prepares a child for sexual abuse and prevents disclosure and detection [13][51]. It can target the child, the family and the surroundings [10]. Offenders may groom the family and community who act as gatekeepers of access, and even institutions, into believing they present no risk to children; the creation and abuse of trust is the key variable [14]. Most abused children are abused by someone well known to them, not a stranger [14], and an older estimate based on offender typologies is that 30 to 45% of child sexual abusers use grooming tactics [51]. A later survivor checklist found 99% reported at least one of 42 behaviours [47]; the two use different methods and thresholds, so they are not competing estimates of the same thing.

| Tactic (stage) | What it can look like | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Victim selection | Picking out a child who seems isolated, unsupervised or in need of attention | [11][12] |
| Trust-building, with the child and the adults | Special attention, praise, gifts or favouritism toward one child; becoming the trusted helper of the family, colleagues or the organisation to gain unsupervised access | [12][13][14] |
| Gaining access and isolation | Arranging one-to-one time, lifts, private meetings or contact outside the setting; separating the child from peers and other adults | [12] |
| Secrecy from parents | Asking the child to keep secrets, building a special 'just us' relationship, discouraging the child from telling parents or carers | [10][13][51] |
| Desensitisation | Gradually introducing touch, sexual talk, jokes or material so that each step seems normal | [11][12][13] |
| Boundary testing | Small, escalating breaches of normal limits on touch, privacy or talk, watching whether the child or other adults object (this page's description of the escalation within the desensitisation stage) | [12][13] |
| Post-abuse maintenance | Threats, guilt, rewards or continued special treatment to prevent disclosure and enable further abuse | [12][51] |

> **Why rules, not profiles.** Many grooming behaviours resemble normal adult-child interactions, which is why they are hard to detect [13]. The two vignette studies disagree: one found adults could recognise grooming but overestimated in hindsight what they would have predicted [46]; the other found adults could not tell grooming from non-grooming scenarios in advance [11]. Among 1,045 adult survivors, 99% reported at least one grooming behaviour [47]. Offender-management measures rest on a stereotype of the stranger; only a small share of new offenders have a prior sex-offence record, and about a third of abusers are themselves juveniles [18]. So safeguards must be rules that apply to everyone before anyone is suspected.

#### Good school safeguards

- Visibility: no unobserved one-to-one contact where it can be avoided; open doors or windows, two-adult rules for trips, changing and overnight stays; active supervision [15].
- No secrets: no adult asks a child to keep a secret from parents or carers, and children are taught that adults who ask for secrets should be reported (this page's synthesis of the secrecy stage [10][51]).
- Communication only through school channels, copied or visible to colleagues; no private messaging, social-media contact or gifts to individual pupils (this page's synthesis of the access and trust stages [12][13]).
- A written code of conduct on touch, language and professional boundaries, with training on the grooming stages so staff recognise patterns in colleagues as well as strangers [12][15].
- Safer recruitment, references and background checks, treated as necessary but not sufficient, plus a clear route for reporting low-level concerns and managing allegations against staff [18][L23].
- Body-safety education for children, so they know the correct names for body parts, that their body is private, and who to tell (see Body-safety education in section 3).
- Report on behaviour: concerns are raised about what an adult does, never about who the adult is [12][13].

> **Behaviour, not identity.** These are behaviours to watch for in any adult. No peer-reviewed study found for this page shows that gender identity, transgender people, LGBTQ people, or gender or sexuality content in schools is grooming or is linked to grooming, abuse or trafficking (see the No group-based risk callout in section 3). Treating a group as suspect would also weaken safeguarding, by drawing attention away from the behaviours that matter.

### 4. Strongest concerns

- Capacity: many 3 to 6 year olds pass through a period of rigid gender beliefs and appearance and are still forming gender constancy [1][3]. In this page's view, abstract content about identity may not match how they think.
- Evidence gap on social transition: the York review [GOV: commissioned via the Cass Review, NHS England] found no robust evidence of benefit or harm [20]. In this page's view, that argues for caution rather than school-led transition; the review did not draw that conclusion.
- In the Dutch clinic cohort, most referred children (80 of 127) were not classed as persisting into adolescence, though children who did not return to the clinic were counted as desisting [29]. If most referred young children would not persist, an early transition that itself raises persistence would matter.
- Early social transition was associated with persistence in one clinic cohort [29], so transition may not be a neutral act; causation is unknown [39], and the US follow-up of already-transitioned children cannot test it [30].
- Referral volumes rose sharply in Finland and the UK, with a shift toward natal girls and frequent co-occurring problems [26][27]. Whether social influence explains this is an untested hypothesis raised by parent-report research recruited partly from online forums [24]; school surveys did not show the same sex-ratio shift [28].
- Parental exclusion: policies that let schools act on a young child's gender identity without telling parents remove the people legally and practically responsible for the child. The US Supreme Court's 2026 interim order found parents likely to succeed against such policies [L9].
- Stated aims: one academic article proposes drag pedagogy for early childhood with aims that go beyond representing LGBT lives, and reports no outcome data (see section 3b). In this page's view, parents may reasonably want notice of sessions built on aims of that kind.

### 5. Counter-evidence

- Body-safety and correct-anatomy teaching at these ages raises protective skills without raising fear [6][7].
- Among children in the North American community sample (including some Canadian families), 94% held a binary transgender identity about 5 years after social transition [30]. Transgender children's gender development patterns resembled those of cisgender children [32].
- A school survey across 16 US states (Youth Risk Behavior Survey, 2017 and 2019; about 92,000 and 105,000 students) found an assigned-male to assigned-female ratio of 1.5:1 and 1.2:1 among gender-diverse adolescents, not the shift toward natal girls seen in clinics. It is not a national sample and uses government survey data [28] [GOV: possible reporting incentives].
- Chosen-name use and family acceptance are linked to better mental health in transgender adolescents and young adults [34][35]. These are teen and adult samples, not kindergarteners.
- Peer-reviewed critiques argue the Cass report misrepresented its own commissioned evidence or reflects bias [23][43]; a peer-reviewed fact-check argues those critiques contain incorrect or uncontextualised claims [44], and other clinicians have published commentary supporting the Review's caution [45].
- Comprehensive sex education reviews report benefits across grade levels [9], though mostly for older students.
- The 2026 Supreme Court order was interim and left the teachers' stay in place; the merits appeal is unresolved, so the legal question is not settled [L9].

### 6. Where the evidence is weak

- No peer-reviewed study found for this page measures the effects of gender-identity lessons on 4 to 7 year olds, positive or negative.
- No peer-reviewed study found measures the effects of drag story events on children.
- No peer-reviewed study examines 4 to 7 year olds who socially transitioned at school without their parents' knowledge.
- Parental trust: no peer-reviewed survey of parents' trust in schools over gender-identity disclosure was found; available polls are not peer-reviewed and were excluded. One policy study analyses Virginia's two model policies [36].
- Persistence studies are contested: in the Dutch study, children who did not return to the clinic were counted as not persisting [29]. The newer North American studies (one includes some Canadian families) rely on volunteer community samples of supportive, disproportionately higher-income families [30][31][40].
- Social contagion: the main parent-report study recruited from online forums [24]. A larger parent-report study was retracted in 2023 over informed consent for publication, a retraction its authors disputed [25]. Counter-studies use clinic or government survey data with their own limits [28]; a Canadian clinic study is covered in section 3b.
- The Cass Review is government-commissioned and contested [G1] [GOV: possible reporting incentives] [23][44]; the York reviews are peer-reviewed but were commissioned through the Cass Review [GOV: commissioned via the Cass Review, NHS England] [19][20][21][41][42].
- No peer-reviewed trial was found for specific school safeguarding rules such as two-adult policies; they rest on the grooming research and expert consensus [12][15].

### 7. Policy options

**Best supported for ages 4 to 7**
- Body-safety education with correct anatomical terms and how to tell a trusted adult [6][7]
- Identity-neutral safeguarding for every adult: two-adult rules, supervision, conduct codes on touch, gifts and secrets, low-threshold reporting of boundary concerns [12][13][15][47]
- Rules that also cover harm between children, since a large share of offences against children are committed by other juveniles, mostly adolescents [48][18]
- Advance notice to parents of any lesson or guest session on sexuality or gender, with opt-out at minimum; Mahmoud supports this for parents with religious objections [L1], and extending it to all parents is a policy choice
- Respect and anti-bullying rules that apply to every child

**Contested or unsupported**
- School-led social transition of young children without parental involvement [20][L9]
- Gender-identity instruction at these ages without outcome evidence either way
- Blanket bans that also block body-safety teaching
- Relying on background checks as the main safeguard [18]

### 8. Ethical analysis

Precaution: the York review found the evidence on social transition for children lacking [20]. This page's own argument, not the review's, is that where evidence is absent the burden should sit with those who want to introduce an intervention for young children.

Parental role: parents carry legal responsibility for young children, and both countries' courts and legislatures are actively defining how far that extends in school [L1][L6][L7][L8][L9].

Child welfare: some children face rejection at home, and family acceptance predicts better health in older youth [35]. A fair policy has a path for the rare case where telling a parent could cause harm. Alberta requires counselling or other support before notification where notice may cause emotional or psychological harm, but notice still follows [L7].

Fairness: safeguarding must judge behaviour, not identity. Applying it to some adults and not others would be unfair and would also leave children less safe, because grooming comes from adults of every kind [12][16][49].

### 9. Open questions

- What do 4 to 7 year olds actually understand from gender-identity content, measured directly?
- Does early social transition change the likelihood of persistence, and in which direction?
- Which notification rules protect both parental involvement and the rare child at risk at home?
- Should opt-out rights rest on religion only, as in Mahmoud, or on parental conscience generally?
- How will the US appeals courts and the Supreme Court settle school non-disclosure on full review?

## Part C: Position paper: gender and sexuality themes for young children come before readiness

An argued position, not a balanced case study. It argues that themes about gender identity and sexuality in children's media and in early-grade policy reach children before they are developmentally ready, and that adult value debates belong at a mature age. The strongest counterarguments are set out with rebuttals, and the limits of the evidence are stated.

### Position

> **The argument in brief.** Children aged about 3 to 7 cannot yet recognise persuasive intent, are still forming a stable sense of what gender is, and learn gender norms readily from media. Contested adult claims about gender identity, and sexual themes of any kind, should therefore not be presented to them as settled fact in programming or lessons. Teach facts and conduct at this age: families differ, everyone is treated kindly, bodies are private. Leave the contested value debates to adolescence, with parents informed. This principle applies to every adult worldview, whichever side of the debate it comes from.

### 1. Developmental readiness

| Finding | What it means for this position | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A meta-analysis of 178 studies found that false-belief understanding, a core test of grasping that others hold different beliefs, develops across the preschool years, moving from below-chance to above-chance performance | Children younger than about 5 have limited ability to see that a message reflects someone's point of view | [1] |
| Gender constancy, the understanding that sex stays the same over time and across appearance (a hypothesis first set out by Kohlberg in a 1966 book chapter, pp. 82-173, cited for attribution only and as described in [2][21]), is still forming between 3 and 7, and many children pass through a rigid phase about gendered appearance | Abstract claims that separate identity from body arrive while the basic concept is still being built | [2][3] |

### 2. Media effects and advertising literacy by age

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Consumer understanding develops in stages tied to cognitive and social maturity: perceptual (about 3 to 7), analytical (about 7 to 11) and reflective (about 11 to 16) | [5] |
| Among 294 children aged 8 to 12, adult-level recognition of advertising arrived around 9 to 10, but at 12 children still lacked an adult-like understanding of its persuasive intent | [25] |
| Understanding of selling intent was predicted by theory-of-mind development, linking persuasion knowledge to the milestones in section 1 | [26] |
| Embedded persuasion, woven into entertainment rather than set apart as an advertisement, poses a particular challenge for how children cope | [27] |
| A meta-analysis of 34 studies (5,473 children) found consistent moderate effects of prosocial television on social interaction, aggression, altruism and stereotyping, largest where content explicitly modelled the behaviour | [28] |
| A meta-analysis of five decades of cultivation research (406 samples) found a small but stable link between television viewing and viewers' conceptions of social reality | [29] |
| Children younger than 8 are especially vulnerable to marketing because they lack the cognitive skills to understand its persuasive intent; embedded messages within programme content are harder still to detect | [6] |
| Among 594 children aged 4 to 12, most described selling intent by 7 to 8, but awareness of persuasive intent emerged slowly and reached only 40% at 11 to 12 | [36] |
| Even when children know about advertising, that knowledge is not an effective defence for young children, given their immature executive function | [7] |
| Among 198 preschoolers, engagement with Disney Princess media predicted more female-stereotyped behaviour a year later | [8] |
| Three meta-analyses show a small but consistent link between television viewing and stereotyped gender beliefs | [9] |
| Educational television measurably teaches young children across many countries | [10] |

The argument from these findings: media shapes young children's ideas about gender [8][9] and their social attitudes [28], and teaches effectively [10], while children under about 8 do not yet understand that a message is trying to sell to them, and understanding that it is trying to persuade them comes later still: 40% at 11 to 12 [36], and not adult-like at 12 [25]. Recognising selling intent at 7 to 8 is not the same as recognising persuasion [6][36]. Embedded, story-based messages are the hardest to resist [7][27]. Value content embedded in a story is the kind of message they are least able to evaluate. Advertising regulation already accepts this reasoning; it should apply equally to value messages in children's programming.

What the law does and does not say. Meyer, Pierce and Yoder recognise parents' right to direct their children's upbringing; they do not give a veto over public instruction or adopt a readiness principle. Mahmoud recognises religious parents' right to notice and opt-out at the preliminary-injunction stage, partly on a judicial view of young children's impressionability; it does not decide what may be taught. Florida's statute and England's guidance both use developmental appropriateness as a test, and England's 'not as fact' provision is the closest legal match to this paper's principle.

### 5. What safeguarding research defines as grooming

> **Grooming is a specific abuse process, not a label for disputed content.** In safeguarding research, sexual grooming is the process by which an abuser prepares a child, the adults around the child and the environment for sexual abuse: gaining access, building trust, securing compliance and secrecy, and avoiding detection [15][18]. A validated model sets out five stages: victim selection, gaining access and isolation, trust development, desensitisation to touch and sexual content, and post-abuse maintenance [16]. Red flags are behaviours of an individual adult toward a particular child, such as special attention, gifts, time alone and requests for secrecy [17]. Disagreement about curricula or programming is a question of developmental readiness and parental rights, which this paper argues on those grounds. No peer-reviewed study found for this paper shows that gender identity, trans people, or inclusive content groom children, and no peer-reviewed evidence found shows a coordinated trafficking agenda. The safeguarding standards in Part B apply to every adult, whoever they are.

### 7. Where this position's evidence is weak

- No study found measures how gender-identity or sexuality content affects 4 to 7 year olds' identity, wellbeing or beliefs. The argument rests on developmental and media research about other kinds of content.
- The advertising-literacy research concerns commercial persuasion. Extending it to value messages is an inference, though a close one.
- Media effects on gender beliefs are small on average [9].
- The events in section 4 come from news reports, one from an advocacy outlet, and show intent or conduct, not effects.
- The Supreme Court's description of young children as accepting moral messages without question is a judicial characterisation, not a finding, and the developmental reviews show it is only partly accurate [L1][12][23].
- Cultivation and prosocial-television effects are mostly measured on other content [28][29]. Whether cultivation samples are mainly older than the children this paper is about was not confirmed from the abstract of [29] (UNVERIFIED).
- The core claim, that contested value debates belong at a mature age, is a normative judgement. Evidence can inform it but cannot settle it.

### 8. Proposals

- Ages 4 to 7: teach body safety, the fact that families differ, and kindness to everyone. Do not teach as fact contested claims about gender identity, and keep sexual themes out of materials for this age.
- Older ages: introduce contested debates no earlier than upper primary and generally in early secondary, teaching the facts, the law and the main positions as a debate. This timing is a proposal of this paper, informed by the persuasion-knowledge evidence [5][25][36]. England's guidance requires that the facts and the law be taught, and not taught as settled fact, by the end of secondary education, and it does not set a year-group cut-off [L7][L12].
- Give parents notice of, and access to, all materials on these topics, with an opt-out for sex education [L1][L7]. Extend notice and opt-out on equal terms to non-religious parents.
- Symmetry: hold every one-sided value message aimed at young children to the same standard, traditional or progressive, commercial or ideological.
- Children's media: label programming aimed at under-8s that carries themes about gender identity or sexuality, so parents can decide when to raise them, consistent with Disney's own stated reason for removing a storyline [N7].
- Schools: this paper proposes no social transition of a young child without parents, except where a documented safeguarding risk makes involving them unsafe. When families are considering social transition of a pre-pubertal child, refer them to be seen early by a clinician with relevant experience [L8].
- Safeguarding: apply the same evidence-based standards to every adult, and keep the word grooming for the abuse process it describes [15][16][17].

## Part D: Recidivism among people convicted of trafficking or sexual offences, and what reduces it

What the research says about how often people convicted of trafficking, or of sexual offences including offences against children, offend again, and which responses lower that rate, which do not, and which are unproven.

### Source rules

> **How to read this part.** Evidence is peer-reviewed research. Laws and court rulings are cited as documents. Government statistics carry [GOV: possible reporting incentives]. Most base rates cover all people convicted of sexual offences; child-offender-specific rates are reported where available. Several of the largest sex-offender meta-analyses pool samples from many countries, including Canada; they are labelled in the reference list. These multi-country syntheses are led by researchers at Canadian institutions, and are used here because they are the field's core pooled evidence, not as Canadian data. Canada note: seven sources in this Part, [1], [2], [3], [5], [9], [10] and [23], have Canadian-institution authors and are labelled in the reference list; no Canadian government report is used, and no conclusion here is rated on a Canadian government source. Non-Canadian sources give a similar picture: official rates of 5-15% after 5 years [24], long-term risk in a US high-risk sample [4], German and British-led treatment meta-analysis [8], and US prison data [G1]. Recidivism here means a new official charge or conviction, which undercounts actual reoffending, because most sexual offences are never reported.

> **The honest reading.** Most people convicted of a sexual offence are not convicted of another within 5 years, but official rates undercount, risk persists for decades for a high-risk minority, and the risk falls sharply for those who stay offence-free [1][3][4]. People convicted of trafficking are different: they look like versatile career criminals, with high general reoffending and a persistent core [6][G2]. The Dutch study of 630 traffickers [6] is the best trafficking evidence found. A small US descriptive study of 28 men with street-level sex-trafficking histories reached the opposite view, that they appear to be a distinct group needing specialised management and treatment [26]; the two differ in size and method. Research on traffickers is otherwise scant [7], so neither 'they never change' nor 'most do not reoffend' is supported for trafficking specifically.

### 4. What the evidence supports

- In crime generally, certainty of apprehension deters more consistently than longer sentences [21]; this has not been separately shown for sexual or trafficking offences in the sources cited here.
- Match supervision and treatment intensity to assessed risk, using programmes built on risk-need-responsivity principles, mainly in the community [8][9].
- Keep the most intensive supervision for the first years after release and for the high-risk minority, and reduce it for those who stay offence-free [3].
- Fund structured re-entry support such as Circles of Support and Accountability, with larger trials [12].
- Treat convicted traffickers as versatile offenders: target their general criminal careers, finances and networks, not only the trafficking offence [6][G2].
- Evaluate before expanding registries or public notification, which have not been shown to reduce reoffending by registrants [16] (Part A notes one study of fewer first-time offences). The opposing view is that registries serve other purposes, such as victims' interests and general deterrence; a South Carolina study found an 11% drop in first-time sex-crime arrests after the law (Part A, reference 24).

### 5. Rule-of-law limits

> **No extrajudicial detention.** Indefinite detention without trial, or sending convicted or suspected offenders somewhere to disappear, is not a lawful or evidence-based option. In the US, detention after a sentence is lawful only through civil commitment with due-process safeguards [L1], and even Guantanamo detainees hold the right to habeas corpus [L2]. Enforced disappearance and arbitrary detention also breach the international human-rights law on which global cooperation against trafficking depends (see Part F). The evidence in this part points to certainty of detection, risk-based supervision and effective treatment instead.

### 6. Where the evidence is weak

- Official records miss sexual offences that are never reported, so recorded rates are incomplete [4][24]; how much they undercount is not settled [28].
- No peer-reviewed study of reoffending by ring or co-offending groups was found.
- Almost no outcome research exists on convicted traffickers outside the Netherlands [6][7].
- Few treatment evaluations are randomised, and effects vary widely [8][11].
- The largest base-rate studies pool samples from several countries, including Canada [1][2][3].

An organization is named only where a court record, regulator order or audit exists; otherwise the text says no such finding was located. Canadian sources appear only in section 7.

### 1. How much money, and how it is handled

- FATF and the Asia/Pacific Group cite an ILO estimate that forced labour generates USD 150.2 billion a year, and find that proceeds from forced labour, sexual exploitation and organ removal are made and laundered in different ways [G1].
- The ILO's 2024 update puts illegal profits from forced labour at USD 236 billion a year, close to USD 10,000 per victim, with forced commercial sexual exploitation accounting for 73% of the total [GOV/IGO: ILO modelled estimate; possible reporting incentives] [G8].
- The same report finds that national risk assessments do not treat trafficking-related laundering with the gravity its estimated proceeds imply, that much of the money is cash, and that assets from forced labour are hard to identify [G1].
- An analysis of 378 police case files [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; agency records] found that most known trafficking cases were not substantially profitable, that they involved varied operating and infrastructure costs, and that many operated partly outside banks [1].
- Interviews with 152 sex workers, traffickers, police and prosecutors traced bookkeeping, internal money movement and laundering in Bulgarian groups exploiting women in Western Europe [2].

> **Honest scale.** The headline global figure is an estimate of forced-labour profits, not of money laundered. Most detected cases are small and cash-based [1][G1]. Sophisticated laundering exists, but the typical documented trafficker uses simple methods.

Over-broad rules against NPOs cause de-risking and financial exclusion [13]. No peer-reviewed or court-recorded case of trafficking proceeds laundered through an NPO was located. | Beneficial-ownership registers | In November 2022 the EU Court of Justice struck down the rule giving any member of the public access to company ownership registers, as a disproportionate interference with privacy; authorities and those with a legitimate interest keep access [L6]. No peer-reviewed study was located showing that ownership registers reduce laundering of crime proceeds | [L6] |

### 3. Tax and secrecy jurisdictions, including Ireland and Thailand

When G20 countries made tax havens sign bank-information treaties, deposits moved to havens not covered by a treaty with the depositor's home country, rather than going home [14]. Secrecy shifts; it does not disappear.

> **What the ratings mean.** These are peer assessments of whole systems, not measures of trafficking money. Thailand's report names human trafficking, with corruption and drug trafficking, among its main sources of criminal proceeds, and Thailand was later re-rated upward on two technical recommendations [G3][G4]. On professional enablers specifically, the 2017 Thailand report found that lawyers, notaries and accountants were not covered by its Anti-Money Laundering Act [G3]; by the 2023 follow-up Thailand had 33 of 40 recommendations rated compliant or largely compliant and remained in enhanced follow-up [G4]. Ratings rely on limited data and can be impressionistic [15], so they should not be read as rankings of how much dirty money a country holds.

### 4. Does anti-money laundering work?

- There are no credible estimates of the total laundered, so reductions cannot be measured, and FATF evaluations use data little [15].
- One analysis estimates the policy affects less than 0.1% of criminal finances while compliance costs exceed recovered funds more than a hundred times; the author notes the data are poorly validated [16].
- EU rules miss some common laundering methods in trafficking cases and work less well when proceeds stay inside the EU [3].
- A Dutch public-private partnership showed that proactive financial investigation can spot possible victims in bank records and follow money to networks [4].

### 6. What the evidence supports

- In a Dutch public-private partnership, proactive financial investigation identified possible victims in bank records and followed money toward networks; the official typology report also treats parallel financial investigation as good practice, though that is not evidence it should be required in every case [4][G1].
- A global incorporation experiment and a Paris property study give reasons to look closely at beneficial ownership, but they do not show that verifying ownership, as opposed to collecting it, reduces laundering of trafficking proceeds [7][8].
- Supervise law and accounting firms that handle company formation and client money [12][3].
- Use risk-based, proportionate rules for non-profits to avoid harming legitimate charities [13].
- Methods exist to link some sex ads to Bitcoin wallets and may help an investigator, but they have not been evaluated as an operational requirement for trafficking investigations [11].
- Measure outcomes such as confiscation and convictions, not only formal compliance [15][16].

None of these sources links casino laundering to trafficking proceeds, apart from FINTRAC's Project PROTECT indicators above, which list online and land-based casinos among the channels seen in trafficking-related transactions [C1].

### 8. Where the evidence is weak

- Most channel evidence comes from government-submitted case studies, not systematic samples [G1].
- No trafficking-specific peer-reviewed measure was found for trade-based laundering, real estate or NPO misuse.
- The crypto estimate covers bitcoin's early years and all illegal activity, not trafficking [10].
- Ratings describe systems, not dirty-money volumes [15].
- The Canadian casino record documents laundering through the channel but not trafficking proceeds specifically (section 7).

## Part F: Global cooperation against trafficking, within the rule of law

The treaties, courts, police and financial cooperation, supply-chain laws, technology, survivor-centred practice and citizen reporting that countries use against trafficking; how Israel's law compares; what the best laws share; and why detected cases are not the same as prevalence.

### Source rules

> **How to read this part.** Evidence is peer-reviewed research. Treaties, statutes and judgments are cited as documents. US TIP Reports, UN and ILO figures and government evaluations carry [GOV: possible reporting incentives]; the TIP Report is also an instrument of US foreign policy. No Canadian data is used.

### 1. The legal backbone

| Instrument | What it does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Palermo Protocol (2000) | Defines trafficking as recruiting, moving, harbouring or receiving people by force, coercion, fraud, deception, abuse of power or vulnerability, or payments, for exploitation (art 3). Its saving clause keeps states bound by human-rights and refugee law, including non-refoulement (art 14). Victim-support duties are mostly 'shall consider' | [L1][L2] |
| UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime | Extradition (art 16), mutual legal assistance that cannot be refused on bank-secrecy grounds (art 18), and joint investigations (art 19) | [L1] |
| Rome Statute | Enslavement is a crime against humanity when part of a widespread or systematic attack on civilians, and expressly includes ownership powers exercised in the course of trafficking in persons | [L3] |
| Universal jurisdiction | Lets any state try core international crimes without a link to the crime. In practice it is decentralised and shaped by political branches, so prosecutions are few and selective | [1] |
| Regional human-rights courts | Courts treat trafficking and slavery as breaches of state duty, not only crimes by individuals: the ECOWAS Court held Niger responsible for failing to protect a woman held as a slave (Koraou, 2008); the European Court held that trafficking falls under the ban on slavery and forced labour and found both Cyprus and Russia in breach (Rantsev, 2010); the Inter-American Court held Brazil responsible over 85 workers rescued from a ranch (Hacienda Brasil Verde, 2016) | [C1][C2][C3] |
| ASEAN Convention against Trafficking in Persons (2015) | A regional treaty that mixes hard and soft obligations to attract ratification; a peer-reviewed assessment says it needs a stronger compliance mechanism | [39] |

> **Critiques of the legal framing** One legal scholar argues the European Court adopted the trafficking concept in Rantsev without analysing its elements [37]. Another warns of 'exploitation creep': recasting all forced labour as trafficking, and all trafficking as slavery, can pull policy toward punishing individual wrongdoers and away from the labour and migration structures that make exploitation possible [38].

Across up to 180 countries from 2000 to 2010, governments complied most with prosecution duties and least with victim protection; compliance fell with corruption and rose where women's rights were respected [18].

### 2. Police, judicial and financial cooperation

- Joint investigation teams let police and prosecutors from several countries work one case under one agreement; the EU legal basis is Framework Decision 2002/465/JHA [L4], and UNTOC art 19 provides the global one [L1].
- Interpol notices and Europol analysis support these teams, but no peer-reviewed evaluation of their effect on trafficking outcomes was located.
- Financial intelligence and FATF standards are covered in Part E, including evidence that anti-money-laundering systems are rarely evaluated against outcomes.
- US federal and state laws produced few cases identified by local police and frequent use of lesser charges; little is known about the laws' effectiveness [17].

### 3. Supply-chain accountability laws

| Law | Main duty | Evidence on effect | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| UK Modern Slavery Act 2015, s54 | Large firms must publish an annual slavery and trafficking statement | Statements are poor or uneven; no fall in trafficking shown; in UK construction, the Act left anti-trafficking initiatives dependent on corporate will and funding, limiting change | [L5][8][9] |
| Australia Modern Slavery Act 2018 (comparison) | Reporting duty | Early statements varied widely in how they addressed effectiveness | [10] |
| France, duty of vigilance law 2017 | Firms with at least 5,000 staff in France or 10,000 worldwide must adopt, apply and publish a vigilance plan covering subsidiaries and established suppliers; courts can order compliance | No peer-reviewed outcome study located | [L10] |
| EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive 2024, as amended by Directive (EU) 2026/470 (24 Feb 2026) | Human-rights and environmental due diligence for companies with more than 5,000 employees and more than EUR 1.5 billion net turnover; the EU-level civil-liability regime was removed; transposition deadline 26 July 2028 | Not yet in application | [L6][L6a] |
| EU Forced Labour Regulation 2024 | Bans products made with forced labour from the EU market and from export, from 14 December 2027 | Too new to evaluate | [L7] |
| US Tariff Act s307 | Bars imports of goods made wholly or partly by forced labour | No peer-reviewed outcome study located | [L9] |
| US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act 2021 | Presumes goods from Xinjiang are made with forced labour unless shown otherwise | No peer-reviewed outcome study located | [L8] |

Business lobbying shaped the UK Act, partly by championing weak initiatives [7].

### 4. Do audits work?

| Study | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Nike's own supplier-audit data, 800+ suppliers in 51 countries | Monitoring alone produced limited improvement; it worked better combined with help on scheduling, quality and efficiency | [2] |
| Nearly 17,000 supplier audits | Auditors found fewer violations when they had audited the factory before, were less trained, were all-male teams, or were paid by the supplier | [3] |
| Nike lean manufacturing, 300+ factories | Lean adoption cut labour-standard noncompliance by 15 percentage points, mainly wages and hours, with no effect on health and safety | [4] |

> **Bottom line.** Audits alone are weak. Audits not paid for by suppliers, carried out by trained, mixed teams, and combined with supplier capability-building have better support [2][3][4]. Worker-informed monitoring and enforceable legal duties are plausible but were not tested in these studies.

### 5. Technology, survivors and citizen reporting

| Topic | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Ad-analysis tools | Semi-supervised learning flagged ads of high interest to police [11]; a text-and-image model reached 0.82 accuracy and high precision on its most confident flags [12]; near-duplicate clustering found trafficking ads with 84% precision [13]. All are lab results; none measured victims found or prosecutions | [11][12][13] |
| Survivor health | Reviews of 19 and then 31 studies found high levels of violence and mental distress among trafficked people, mostly women and girls in sexual exploitation, with emerging evidence on men and labour trafficking | [14][15] |
| Fair identification | In about 55,000 UK National Referral Mechanism cases [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; agency records], conclusive decisions differed by nationality and by which official referred the person | [16] |
| Citizen reporting | No peer-reviewed evaluation of hotlines or public-reporting campaigns was located. In UN court-case analysis, 41% of cases began with victims escaping and reporting themselves, against 28% found by police and 11% by community members or civil society [GOV]; systems that wait for self-rescue miss people who cannot leave. An exploratory study of 3,613 possible cases reported to a UK helpline found only about 1 in 10 came from people reporting their own exploitation, and that few involved sexual exploitation; it described contacts but did not measure outcomes | [G11][45] |
| Asia: external pressure and online child exploitation | After the EU's 2015 'yellow card' to Thailand over illegal fishing, labour rights were folded into the bilateral dialogue and shaped Thai fisheries reform [46]. A scoping review of online sexual exploitation of children in the Philippines found 19 eligible studies, identified poverty, weak social protection and payment and social-media systems as risk factors, and judged the evidence on prevalence and on what works insufficient [47] | [46][47] |
| Platform shutdowns (FOSTA and Backpage, 2018) | A difference-in-differences study found no measurable effect of the 2018 Craigslist and Backpage shutdowns on prostitution arrests or violence against women, because advertisers moved quickly to other, often offshore, sites [42]. US auditors found the market moved overseas and fragmented, making evidence harder to gather, and that by March 2021 prosecutors had used the new FOSTA offence only once [GOV: possible reporting incentives]. Other studies point in different directions: Craigslist's erotic-services section likely reduced female homicides by 12-18% [40]; its arrival raised prostitution by about 17%, with organised 'commercial vice groups' growing fastest [41]; sex workers report lost income and less ability to screen clients after FOSTA [43][44] | [40]-[44][G12] |

### 6. Israel's law compared with other countries

| Feature | Israel | Elsewhere | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core offence | Penal Law s377A (2006): a 'transaction in a person' for organ removal, giving birth to a child and taking the child away, slavery, forced labour, prostitution, pornography or sexual offences; up to 16 years, 20 if the victim is a minor | Palermo definition based on act, means and purpose [L1]; Israel's offence needs no proof of means [G3] | [L13][L1][G3] |
| Sex-purchase law | 2019 law penalises buying sex, with a client education alternative. The government-commissioned evaluation [GOV: possible reporting incentives] found thin, uneven enforcement (97% of fines from three police stations); self-reported buying among Jewish men aged 18-70 was higher in 2024 than in 2021 (five-year rate 12.2% against 9.2%), while about 20% of buyers said they had stopped or reduced buying since the law; searches stayed below pre-law levels. The evaluators caution that COVID-19 and the Israel-Hamas war confound these results and that three years is a short period. Sex workers interviewed in a qualitative study said the legislative process excluded them from the debate [35] | The Nordic model is not applied as written in the Nordic countries [24]; Dutch licensing did not make trafficking visible [23] | [L14][G4][35][23][24] |
| Migrant workers | The High Court struck down the 'binding' permit system in 2006; researchers argue that state migration schemes and private brokers drive labour trafficking | In the UK, identification outcomes differ by nationality and referring official [16] | [L15][31][16] |
| Victim status | Recognised victims receive visas and services not conditional on testifying, though the bar for formal recognition is high (US reports) [GOV: possible reporting incentives] | Protocol leaves this to states ('shall consider') | [G1][G3][L1] |
| Results | Israeli researchers describe a coordinated multi-agency response to sex trafficking from 2005 onward [34] and analyse the later End Demand policy [30]. The Egypt border fence stopped unauthorised crossings [33], but it was built after the main anti-trafficking reforms. No study located dates or measures a fall in trafficking itself | No peer-reviewed study located isolates the effect of a single legal change on trafficking prevalence in any country; causal studies of the 2018 US platform shutdowns measure arrests and violence, not trafficking [42] | [30][34][33][42] |
| Labour trafficking gap | The 2026 TIP report records zero forced-labour convictions despite an upgrade to Tier 1 [GOV: possible reporting incentives] | Worldwide, forced-labour detections rose 47% from 2019 to 2022 [GOV: possible reporting incentives] [G6] | [G3][G6] |
| Sinai trafficking | A distinct form, from about 2009, of abduction, sale, ransom and torture, mostly of Eritreans | - | [32] |

> **Reading the US tiers.** Israel was rated Tier 1 from 2012, Tier 2 from 2021 to 2025, and Tier 1 again in 2026 [GOV: possible reporting incentives] [G1][G2][G3]. Inclusion in the report makes countries more likely to criminalise trafficking [26], but rankings show political bias [27] and sanctions are applied strategically [28][29][L16]. Tiers grade government effort against US criteria, not prevalence.

### 7. What the best laws have in common

Synthesis, labelled as judgement. No legal feature has strong causal evidence of lowering actual trafficking [19][20][21][22]. These are the features with the best support for better detection, fairer treatment or accountability.

- A clear offence that covers labour as well as sexual exploitation and does not depend on crossing a border [L1][L13].
- Victim protection that does not depend on testifying, with fair, independent identification [16][G1].
- Enforceable corporate duties with remedies, rather than statements alone: statement-only regimes are shown to be weak [8][9]; whether enforceable duties such as France's do better has not yet been measured [L10].
- Monitoring not paid for by the supplier, by trained, mixed teams, plus supplier support, rather than paid audits alone [2][3][4].
- Low corruption and strong women's rights, which go with better compliance [18].
- Parallel financial investigation and asset recovery (see Part E).
- Built-in, external evaluation, as Israel's 2019 law required [G4].

Prostitution-law choices remain contested: cross-country studies link legal prostitution with higher reported trafficking inflows [19][20], but they use reported or proxied flows and cannot show cause [21][22], and criminalising sex work is associated with worse health for sex workers [25].

### 8. Cross-country data: detection is not prevalence

> **The core rule.** UNODC states that its victim counts cover only people officially identified and reported, and 'shall not be considered representative of the prevalence of the crime'; they show detection capacity [G5]. A country that looks harder finds more victims and can look worse.

| Measure | Figure | What it measures | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detected victims worldwide, 2020 [GOV] | Down 11% from 2019 during the pandemic, while vulnerability rose | Detection fell, not necessarily trafficking | [G11] |
| Detected victims worldwide, 2022 against 2019 [GOV] | Up 25%; detected children up 31%; forced-labour detections up 47% | Detection effort and capacity | [G6] |
| Modern slavery, 2021 [GOV/IGO and NGO: modelled estimate; possible reporting incentives] | 50 million people on any given day (forced labour and forced marriage) | Modelled survey-based estimate | [G7] |

This table uses only figures opened directly from their sources. Multiple Systems Estimation can estimate national hidden numbers where several lists exist, but not a global total [G10]. Global indices with precise national rates have drawn serious peer-reviewed criticism [36].

### 9. Rule-of-law limits

> **No extrajudicial detention.** Sending suspected or convicted traffickers somewhere to 'disappear', including Guantanamo, would be arbitrary detention [L12] and possibly enforced disappearance [L17], both barred by human-rights law that the Palermo Protocol expressly preserves [L1, art 14]. US courts have held that even Guantanamo detainees have habeas corpus rights [L11]. It would also undermine extradition and legal assistance, which depend on fair-trial guarantees. Cooperation works through courts, evidence and lawful sentences.

### 10. Where the evidence is weak

- No peer-reviewed study shows any legal model lowering actual trafficking [19][20][21][22].
- Supply-chain laws are studied for statement quality, not outcomes [8][9][10].
- Detection tools are tested in the lab, not on rescues or convictions [11][12][13].
- Most figures are official and subject to reporting incentives [G5][G6].

## Part G: Risk settings and tools: foster care, crises, online exploitation, organ removal and surrogacy, detection technology, corruption, labour trafficking, demand reduction, hotlines, survivor support

Ten settings and tools that bear on how people come to be trafficked and how trafficking is found or reduced: the child-welfare system, crises and displacement, online exploitation of children, trafficking for organ removal and exploitative surrogacy, detection technology, corruption, labour trafficking in four sectors, efforts to reduce demand, hotlines and the help that follows a call, and support for survivors' recovery. Each section states what has been measured and what has not.

### Source rules

> **How to read this part.** Evidence is peer-reviewed research; statutes, treaties and court records are cited as documents. Government, intergovernmental and hotline figures carry [GOV] or [NGO] flags for possible reporting incentives: they count what was detected, recorded or reported, not how much exploitation exists. Anything marked UNVERIFIED was not confirmed in an opened source and is reported only as labelled. Sections 7 and 8 also use official evaluations, one think-tank report and one preprint, each labelled as not peer-reviewed. No Canadian data or Canadian-institution research is used. Organisations are named as wrongdoers only where a court record exists; no individual is named.

### 1. Foster care and child welfare (United States)

| Question | What the evidence shows | Strength | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Are youth in care at higher risk of identified trafficking? | As a risk marker, yes. A systematic review of 15 US studies lists maltreatment, running away, child-protective involvement, juvenile-justice involvement and prior sexual victimisation among recurring risk factors [1]. In 273 people in the commercial sex industry in one state, having ever run away remained the distinguishing factor for entry before 18 in multivariate models [2]. In Florida agency data, children with trafficking allegations were more than five times as likely to have been in congregate care [3], and 7% of foster-care runaways placed at 10 or older had a trafficking allegation while on runaway status [4] [GOV FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; allegation data] | Moderate; detection bias cannot be separated | [1][2][3][4] |
| Does care itself cause the risk? | Not established. Placement instability predicted behaviour problems after adjustment [5], and group-home placement carried about 2.5 times the delinquency risk of foster homes in one county [6], but causal studies of removal give mixed results that depend on the child and the measure [11][12]. None measures trafficking | Gap for trafficking | [5][6][11][12] |
| Maltreatment inside care | Allegations reach up to about 2% of children in foster care a year in US, UK and Australian data [7]; self-report surveys find far higher rates, highest in residential care [8]. Kinship care's safety advantage is small or absent once investigations are separated from substantiations [9], though it is linked to better stability [10] | Moderate | [7][8][9][10] |
| Is missing-from-care reporting done as the law requires? | Federal law requires states to report missing children to law enforcement and the national clearinghouse within 24 hours [L1][L2]. A federal audit estimated about 69% of missing episodes of two days or more, mid-2018 to 2020, were not reported as required [G1] [GOV: audit finding of non-compliance] | Strong as an audit finding | [L1][L2][G1] |
| Did limits on group care work? | Congregate care fell, mostly before 2018; after 2018 the fall came mainly from fewer children in care [13]. Twenty-six of 49 states reported no decrease [G2] [GOV: possible reporting incentives] | Moderate | [13][G2] |
| Do screening tools and carer checks work? | Few trafficking screening tools are validated, and there is no gold standard [14]. Fingerprint and registry checks of carers are required by law, but no evaluation of their preventive effect was found; carer training improves parenting skills, with a small effect on child behaviour [15] | Limited; gap for checks | [14][15] |

Bottom line: running from placement and instability within care are high-priority risk markers for trafficking, but the evidence does not show that care is the main cause or that most victims come from care. The measurable failure points are unreported missing episodes, placement instability and group settings with high abuse rates.

### 2. Crises: war, disasters and displacement

| Setting | What was measured | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Ukraine, 2022 onward | Early warnings and an official model predicted a very large rise in victims. Peer-reviewed studies and later official data do not show a mass influx of trafficked people on that scale [16][17]. Identified Ukrainian victims in the EU rose from 65 in 2021 to 402 in 2022 while the Ukrainian population there more than tripled, and at least 25% of the 402 had been trafficked before February 2022; 5% of Ukrainians and 6% of non-Ukrainians surveyed said they paid for irregular border crossing [G3] [GOV/IGO: possible reporting incentives]. Researchers attribute the absence of a large wave mainly to visa-free entry and immediate temporary protection with rights to work, housing, health care and schooling [16][17][L3], while warning that under-paid and informal work persisted and that the case may not apply where people lack legal routes [16][17][26] | [16][17][26][G3][L3] |
| Armed conflict generally | A systematic review of 29 papers in 12 countries found sexual exploitation in conflict settings but limited, non-generalisable evidence and no study designed to measure prevalence | [18] |
| Disasters | A 158-country study and a scoping review report an association between disasters and more trafficking [21][22]; both were read as abstracts, which do not say whether the outcome is actual or detected trafficking. After the 2015 Nepal earthquakes, peer-reviewed journals had not confirmed a rise in sex trafficking [19]. A Haiti analysis from 2010 documents pre-existing child domestic exploitation but does not measure a post-earthquake rise [20] | [19][20][21][22] |
| Climate and drought | Climate change is reviewed as a possible multiplier of known risk factors, not a shown direct cause [24]. In drought-affected northern Ethiopia, community surveillance found child separations that would otherwise have gone unrecognised; it did not measure trafficking [27] | [24][27] |
| Front-line screening | Interviews with front-line workers at Italian borders describe gaps in identification and short-term permits [25]; this shows implementation problems, not whether screening works | [25] |

- Rapid legal status with the right to work, housing, health care and schooling has the strongest support in the best-measured recent case, though the design is observational and the outcome is identified harm, not prevalence [16][17].
- Cash assistance: in the largest refugee cash programme, a regression-discontinuity evaluation found the share of children working fell from 14.0% to 1.6% and the share out of school from 36.2% to 13.7% [23]. That is a child-labour and schooling result, not a trafficking result.
- Registration of unaccompanied children is widely judged necessary; studies show it detects separation, but no trafficking-reduction effect has been measured [27].
- Awareness campaigns: in the Ukraine case, researchers judged them to have had little measurable impact compared with legal rights [16].

### 3. Online exploitation of children

| Form | What is known | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Financially motivated sextortion | US surveys put childhood sextortion at about 3.5% to 5%, with ages 13 to 17 the peak and boys and non-heterosexual youth more often targeted [28][29]; a ten-country adult survey also found men and LGBTQ+ people more often targeted [31]; most perpetrators of online abuse in the US survey were dating partners, friends and acquaintances [29]. | Protective measure | What has been measured | Effect on exploitation |
|---|---|---|
| School sexual-abuse prevention education | Better knowledge (SMD 0.61) and protective behaviour (2 small trials), no added fear [38] | Not measured |
| Stand-alone internet-safety education | Content often mismatches the evidence; folding online safety into dating-abuse and abuse-prevention programmes is recommended [39] | Not measured |

| Question | What the evidence shows | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Law | The Palermo Protocol lists removal of organs as a form of exploitation; consent is irrelevant once a listed means such as abuse of vulnerability is used [L10]. ### 5. Detection technology

> **The evidence is thin.** The tools detect proxies, not confirmed trafficking. No study opened links flagged results to identified victims or convictions.

**Shown, against proxy labels**
- Grouping escort ads by likely author or shared identifiers, sometimes with high same-author accuracy against phone or email labels [58][60].
- Scoring ads for similarity to annotators' or investigators' 'suspicion' labels, with lab accuracy of roughly 60-80% against those labels [59][60].
- Exposing bias in how training labels are collected, and proposing fixes [61].
- Counting brick kilns from satellite images, and flagging fishing vessels whose tracks resemble those linked to forced labour, as risk scores [62][65].

**Not shown**
- Identifying a trafficked person from an online ad, with a validated rate against confirmed cases [58][59][60][61].
- Separating voluntary sex-work ads from trafficking ads in a way that survives the authors' own caveats [58][63][64].
- A published, peer-reviewed accuracy figure for commercial ad-screening products.
- Turning a financial model's output into confirmed trafficking payments, victims or convictions [58].
- Seeing forced labour itself from a satellite image or vessel track [62][65].

False positives and privacy: none of the ad-classifier papers estimates how common true trafficking ads are in their data, so the real false-positive burden cannot be calculated. Qualitative research argues that this kind of surveillance falls on voluntary sex workers as well as trafficked people, and that false positives carry civil-liberties costs [63][64]; the technical papers mostly do not measure those costs. US auditors found that by 2021 the new federal online-trafficking offence had been used rarely [G10] [GOV: possible reporting incentives].

### 6. Corruption and official complicity

| Question | What the evidence shows | Source |
|---|---|---|

Gaps: no peer-reviewed study measures how common official complicity in trafficking is in any country, and no evaluation was found of anti-corruption measures in consular services, courts in trafficking cases, digital visa systems or specialised anti-trafficking integrity units [G11].

### 7. Labour trafficking: fishing, agriculture, domestic work and employer-tied visas

> **Almost nothing here measures forced labour as an outcome.** Most studies measure wages, legal change, statement quality or proxies. No study found identifies a causal effect of any law or programme on the amount of forced labour. Rates from different studies use different definitions and are not comparable.

Scale: the ILO, Walk Free and IOM estimate 28 million people in forced labour in 2021 [G12] [GOV/IGO: modelled estimates; possible reporting incentives]. These are modelled, not counted, and the related Global Slavery Index has been criticised for its methods [83]. A systematic review of European labour-trafficking research screened 6,106 records and could synthesise only eight studies; few met minimum scientific standards and almost none evaluated interventions [82].

| Setting | How it happens | What has been measured to reduce it | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employer-tied visas and recruitment debt | Tying legal status to one employer gives the employer power over wages and exit: kafala sponsorship in Lebanon produces 'illegal' workers when they leave [97]; concentrated US guest-worker labour markets lower wages, by about 10% for H-1B in a move to a two-employer market [102] [GOV FIGURE: visa programme data]. In IOM's database of nearly 50,000 assisted victims, 94% had been recruited in a broad sense, and the authors call the link complex [108] [GOV/IGO FIGURE: possible reporting incentives; assisted cases only]. ### 8. Demand reduction

> **No demand-reduction measure has strong causal evidence.** Evaluations of laws against buying sex conflict. John schools change attitudes more than behaviour. Procurement and disclosure laws show weak compliance (section 7). Arrest, rearrest, fine and detention counts measure enforcement as much as behaviour.

### 9. Hotlines and the help that reaches victims afterwards

> **Hotline counts are outputs, not prevalence, and outcomes are not tracked.** Hotline figures count contacts and cases that an operator recorded; they move with publicity, outreach and reporting incentives. No system examined records what happens to each referral, and no study links a hotline contact or NGO service to a later outcome such as safety, exit, prosecution or wellbeing with a comparison group (GAP).

> **The samples come only from people already reached by services, and no study measures re-trafficking after a programme.** Symptom rates describe people who reached a shelter, clinic or assistance programme, not all survivors. The treatment studies are before-and-after comparisons without control groups, and none reports re-trafficking as an outcome. Men, boys and labour-trafficking survivors appear in the need studies but are largely absent from the evidence on what helps (GAP).

### 11. Where the evidence is weak

- Almost no study in this Part measures actual trafficking; outcomes are allegations, identifications, reports, risk scores or proxies.
- Foster care: no causal study measures trafficking, and no evaluation of carer background checks was found.
- Crises: the Ukraine result may not apply where people lack legal routes; disaster studies were read as abstracts.
- Online: no measured effect on exploitation for any protective measure; hotline and clearinghouse counts move with law and detection.
- Organs and surrogacy: the only prevalence figure dates from 2007; no study estimates how common coercion in surrogacy is.
- Detection technology and corruption measures: none has been linked to identified victims, convictions or less trafficking.
- Labour trafficking: no study measures forced labour before and after a visa reform, fee ban, ILO convention, port inspection, import ban or worker-driven programme; the null results are for pre-departure training and Italy's anti-caporalato laws.
- Demand reduction: purchase-ban evaluations conflict and rest on before-and-after comparisons of hidden markets; almost none separates buyers of minors; rearrest and detention counts track enforcement.
- Hotlines: referral outcomes are not tracked, contact counts are operator outputs, and survivor-service evaluations are few and mostly poor quality.
- Survivor support: need figures come from people already in services; therapy studies are uncontrolled before-and-after comparisons; no study tests whether housing, legal status, record relief or income support reduces re-trafficking.
