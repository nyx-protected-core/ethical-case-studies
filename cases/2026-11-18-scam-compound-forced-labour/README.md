# Forced Labour in Southeast Asia's Online-Scam Compounds: Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos and the Philippines

*Standalone scholarly brief. Every source below was checked live in November 2026. "200" means the page opened; "302" means a DOI resolved through doi.org. Where a page blocked automated requests (HTTP 401 or 403), this is noted and an open copy is given where one exists. Each figure is attributed to the source it came from.*

> **A note on the evidence base.** Peer-reviewed research on this topic is much thinner than for older public-health problems. The industry grew to scale only after 2020, and access to compounds is dangerous. This brief therefore relies mainly on UN bodies (OHCHR, UNODC, the ILO legal texts), INTERPOL, think tanks (USIP and GI-TOC), human-rights investigators (Amnesty International), blockchain-analytics firms (Chainalysis and Elliptic) and government enforcement documents. Peer-reviewed or academic work is used where it exists [10–14]. Journalism is used only for recent events and is labelled as journalism; it is not peer-reviewed evidence [20–22]. Commercial analytics firms have their own commercial interests. Government enforcement documents set out allegations, not proven facts.

## Key findings

### 1. Scale: large, and uncertain

- **How many people are trapped.** OHCHR says "credible sources" indicate *at least* 120,000 people in Myanmar and about 100,000 in Cambodia may be held and forced to run scams. Laos, the Philippines and Thailand are named as destination or transit countries for "at least tens of thousands" more [1]. USIP adds an estimate of 85,000 in Laos and about 500,000 region-wide. Its US$63.9 billion global figure was built by multiplying headcounts by an assumed US$350 per worker per day [3]. INTERPOL reports that by March 2025 victims from 66 countries had been trafficked into scam centres, about 74% of them to Southeast Asia [4]. Not everyone in a compound is a trafficking victim: INTERPOL notes that "not every person committing fraud in a scam centre is a victim" [4], and USIP refers to "a small number of workers who willingly participate" [3].
- **How much money is stolen.** UNODC estimates that East and Southeast Asian countries lost up to US$37 billion to cyber-enabled fraud in 2023, and that the industry's hundreds of large scam centres make just under US$40 billion a year in profit [2]. USIP's conservative estimate was about US$64 billion stolen worldwide in 2023, of which US$43.8 billion came from syndicates based in Mekong countries [3]. Chainalysis counted at least US$9.9 billion in on-chain crypto scam revenue in 2024. It calls this a lower bound, and pig-butchering revenue grew nearly 40% [16]. GI-TOC warns that these figures "are not comparable" because they define scams differently and use different methods [7].

### 2. Recruitment routes: deception, sale and debt

- **Luring.** Victims are typically recruited through fake job adverts on mainstream social media promising high pay, bonuses and free board, often for jobs said to be in Bangkok [1]. Others are recruited through friends and relatives, or kidnapped outright [11]. Women are mostly recruited through someone they know [14]. AI is now being used to write convincing fake job adverts [4].
- **Who the victims are.** Early victims were mostly Chinese-speaking Asians. Since then, people have been trafficked from South America, Western Europe and Eastern Africa [4]. Many are well educated, multilingual and computer-literate [1].
  - Of 7,141 workers removed from Myawaddy compounds by 26 February 2025, 4,860 were Chinese, 572 Vietnamese, 526 Indian and 430 Ethiopian, among other nationalities listed in the same table [2].
  - Ethiopian, Kenyan and other African nationals were among those handed to Thailand [2].
- **How they are held.** Traffickers confiscate passports and phones and confine victims. Victims who resist or miss targets face beatings, electrocution and sexual violence. Traffickers build up a "debt" that grows each time a victim is *sold* to another compound, and it is often demanded from families as ransom [1]. Ransom and debt bondage are revenue streams in their own right [8].

### 3. How the money flows

- **The fraud.** "Pig butchering" (shā zhū pán) combines romance and investment fraud. Scammers build trust over weeks, then steer the target into fake cryptocurrency investments [1, 7]. Scammers follow standardised grooming manuals [7].
- **Laundering.** Losses usually start as bank transfers from victims' own accounts. They are converted into cryptocurrency, layered, and moved back into cash through licensed fintech and crypto firms. Some of those firms have close links to political elites [8]. Griffin and Mei trace funds leaving mostly as Tether through large exchanges, with at least US$75.3 billion moved into suspicious deposit accounts [15].
- **Huione Guarantee.** This Telegram-based "guarantee" marketplace was used by Southeast Asian scam operators. Elliptic records at least US$11 billion in transactions on it [17], and UNODC cites estimates of at least US$24 billion flowing into wallets used by the marketplace and its vendors [2]. In a Section 311 final rule (a regulatory measure, not a criminal judgment), FinCEN found that Huione Group had received at least US$4 billion worth of illicit proceeds between August 2021 and January 2025, and cut it off from the US financial system [18].
- **The casino and SEZ nexus.**
  - **Origins:** the industry grew out of loosely regulated casinos and online gambling aimed largely at Chinese customers [3]. It clusters in areas controlled by non-state armed groups, special economic zones (SEZs) and border areas, including Myawaddy/KK Park, Laos's Golden Triangle SEZ and Cambodia's Sihanoukville [2, 7].
  - **Who profits:** USIP reports that a significant share of the profits flows to the Myanmar military and to ruling elites in Cambodia and Laos [3]. GI-TOC documents local elites who hold state, business and criminal roles at the same time [7]. The documented operators and their protectors are multinational: transnational syndicates, Myanmar armed groups and border guard forces, and local elites in Cambodia and Laos [2, 3, 7]. No nationality is responsible for the industry as a group.
  - **Cambodia case:** US authorities allege that Cambodia's Prince Group ran forced-labour compounds and bribed officials [18].

### 4. Why victims are treated as offenders

- **The law.** The Palermo Protocol defines trafficking but has no explicit non-punishment clause [5]. OHCHR describes the non-punishment principle as first set out in its own Principles and Guidelines, "understood to be encompassed" in the Protocol's aim of protecting victims, and included in the ASEAN Convention Against Trafficking in Persons [1]. The ILO's 2014 Forced Labour Protocol, Article 4(2), requires states to allow authorities not to prosecute victims for acts they were compelled to commit [6]. Seven ASEAN states have non-punishment provisions, but these mostly cover only a select list of offences [1].
- **How identification fails.** People are "erroneously identified as criminals" because of the scam work itself or for immigration offences. Many countries in the region do not recognise trafficking into forced criminality as trafficking at all [1].
  - *Large losses:* the scale of the fraud leads authorities to exclude these offences from non-punishment, so returnees are prosecuted for fraud and money laundering [12].
  - *Digital evidence:* the digital record of offending can bury the evidence of coercion [13].
  - *Cambodia:* authorities have treated escapees as irregular migrants. None of the 73 survivors Amnesty interviewed had been recognised as victims, although all met the legal definition [10].

### 5. Recent crackdowns and what they achieved (2023–2026)

- **China-led operations.** China's Ministry of Public Security claims that over 53,000 suspects were apprehended jointly with Myanmar and that large-scale operations in northern Myanmar were eradicated [19]. This is an official claim that has not been independently verified. Note that the people handed over were classed as *suspects*, not screened as possible victims [19].
- **Thailand–Myanmar border, early 2025.** Thailand cut internet, electricity and fuel supplies to five Myanmar border areas [20]. More than 7,000 people were then held at Myawaddy awaiting repatriation [2, 20]. UNODC records the strain this put on humanitarian capacity, and notes that during earlier closures many operators "simply relocated to more remote areas" [2].
- **KK Park, October 2025.** The Myanmar military's raid on KK Park was reported locally as "widely dismissed as a PR stunt" [21]. Operations reportedly moved to Shwe Kokko, Kyauk Khet and Payathonzu with help from junta-aligned militias. Scam centres elsewhere in Myawaddy kept running on alternative internet services [21].
- **Cambodia, July 2025 to 2026.** The government claimed more than 250 interventions. Amnesty found evidence of intervention at only 24 of 86 compounds, and survivors said they were moved around the country during the crackdown to evade police [10]. By August 2026 the government said no compounds remained. Its own statement acknowledged that groups had moved into guesthouses, condominiums, rented houses and vehicles [22].
- **Philippines.** A ban on Philippine offshore gaming operators (POGOs) took effect in November 2024, and more than 20,000 POGO workers left the country. Raids continued in 2025, including one that detained about 400 foreigners [2]. Observers report that operations are breaking up into smaller units in nondescript buildings [7].
- **Overall verdict.** Crackdowns have **disrupted** the large, visible compounds and freed or removed tens of thousands of people. The weight of evidence shows **displacement and fragmentation, not dismantling** [2, 7, 9, 10, 21, 22]. UNODC also documents spillover into other regions [2], and INTERPOL documents expansion into West Africa, the Middle East and Central America [4].

## Contested / uncertain points

- **The headcount figures are rough and widely repeated.** OHCHR's figures are attributed to unnamed "credible sources" [1]. USIP's total is a modelled multiplication [3]. GI-TOC's 2025 report even swaps OHCHR's country figures, giving 100,000 for Myanmar and 120,000 for Cambodia instead of the original 120,000 and 100,000 [7, 1], which shows how numbers degrade as they are repeated. Regional governments dispute the estimates but have offered no comprehensive alternative data [7].
- **How many workers were coerced versus willing.** INTERPOL and USIP both state that some workers are not trafficked [3, 4]. Victims may also become recruiters or supervisors, a pattern GI-TOC says challenges the usual distinction between victims and perpetrators [7]. Survivor samples are small or not random: 3 survivor interviews in [14], a survey of Chinese victims in [11], and 73 interviewees in [10].
- **The loss estimates measure different things.** They mix stolen funds, profits and on-chain inflows. Some cover the whole world, others only the region [2, 3, 7, 16]. Chainalysis figures are lower bounds that are revised upward over time [16].
- **Enforcement figures come from parties with a stake in them.** The Chinese, Myanmar military and Cambodian government figures [19, 22] are not independently verified. The US figures are allegations in an indictment and a civil forfeiture complaint [18]. The DOJ release described the Prince Group chairman as "at large" at the time of that release. Cambodia's Interior Ministry later said he was arrested and extradited to China on 6 January 2026 [23–25]. No public conviction, and no final US forfeiture order, was found as of November 2026. The status of each named entity is set out in the table above.
- **Whether financial actions reduce harm.** Platform takedowns and sanctions are well documented, but there is no published evidence linking them to fewer trafficking victims or less fraud. Elliptic documents users migrating to a successor marketplace [17].

## Solutions, ranked by strength of evidence

1. **Financial disruption: crypto tracing, exchange cooperation and sanctions. Evidence of disruption is the best available; displacement is documented.**
   - *Evidence:* actions are measurable and traceable. Telegram removed Huione and Xinbi Guarantee after Elliptic's analysis [17]. FinCEN cut Huione off from the US financial system, OFAC sanctioned 146 Prince Group targets, and DOJ is pursuing forfeiture of about 127,271 BTC [18]. Tracing shows that funds leave through a small number of large exchanges, which makes exchange cooperation and Know-Your-Customer enforcement a real chokepoint [15]. GI-TOC points to weak KYC rules and high suspicious-transaction reporting thresholds in the banking sector [8].
   - *Not yet shown:* that disruption cuts victimisation. Elliptic reports that users moved to a successor marketplace (Tudou Guarantee, whose user numbers rose by nearly 30%) and that Xinbi announced a relaunch [17].
2. **Cross-border law-enforcement cooperation (ASEAN, China, Thailand and origin countries). The disruption is real; durable reduction is unproven.**
   - *Evidence:* joint operations produced the largest documented removals: over 53,000 suspects according to China [19], and more than 7,000 people in a single month at Myawaddy [2, 20]. GI-TOC recommends cooperation even between geopolitical rivals, and closer coordination with countries vulnerable to displaced scam centres [9].
   - *Weaknesses:* relocation followed each wave [2, 21, 22]. Mass removals overwhelmed screening and humanitarian capacity [2]. Handovers have sometimes treated everyone as a suspect [19].
3. **Victim-centred identification and non-punishment. The legal and normative case is strong; evidence of effect is thin.**
   - *Basis:* international standards support it [1, 5, 6]. Failures are well documented in Cambodia [10], in prosecutions after return [12] and in Indonesia [13]. OHCHR and GI-TOC argue that proper identification protects victims and keeps them out of fraud [1, 9].
   - *Not yet shown:* no study found for this brief evaluates whether better screening increases prosecutions or reduces re-trafficking.
4. **Platform-side fraud and recruitment prevention. Plausible, but largely unevaluated.**
   - *Rationale:* recruitment runs through mainstream social media and job sites [1], and AI-written adverts make detection harder [4]. Telegram's removal of the guarantee markets [17] and the reported disabling of more than 2,500 Starlink terminals [21] show that platforms can act.
   - *Not yet shown:* any published evaluation of whether platform measures reduce recruitment or fraud losses. Compounds kept operating on other internet providers [21].
5. **Addressing Myanmar's governance vacuum and armed-group complicity. The most fundamental cause, and the least tractable.**
   - *Diagnosis:* USIP reports that a significant share of profits flows to the Myanmar military and to ruling elites in Cambodia and Laos, and that compounds operate under armed protection from border guard forces and proxies [3]. GI-TOC documents local elites who hold state, business and criminal roles at the same time [7]. UNODC shows how operations concentrate in areas controlled by non-state armed groups, special economic zones and border areas [2]. The KK Park episode shows how a raid by an actor alleged to profit from the industry can relocate operations rather than end them [3, 21].
   - *Evidence:* there is no tested intervention. GI-TOC proposes diplomatic and economic pressure on jurisdictions that offer political protection [9]. That depends on politics far outside the counter-trafficking field, and its effect is unproven.

**Cross-cutting caution.** Every enforcement-led approach has so far moved the problem around: from large compounds to small sites, from Myawaddy to neighbouring towns, and from Southeast Asia to other regions [2, 4, 7, 9]. GI-TOC also recommends economic reintegration for returnees, so that trafficked workers' scam skills do not spread further [9]. This makes sense but has not been evaluated.
