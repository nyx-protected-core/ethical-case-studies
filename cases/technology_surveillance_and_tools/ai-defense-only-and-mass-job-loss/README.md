# AI Only for Defense: Mass Job Loss and Civil Unrest

Date: 20 November 2026
Author: anonymous

## Thesis

This paper argues that general-purpose artificial intelligence should not be sold into the consumer and ordinary commercial market, and that its lawful use
should be confined to the defense industry. The claim is not that defense use is harmless. It is that market-scale deployment would be a labor-replacing shock
of a breadth and speed that earlier automation did not produce; that the best empirical work already finds job loss, wage damage and political backlash from
narrower shocks; and that a much larger, more simultaneous loss of work could, on the historical record of Western democracies, feed civil unrest and a turn
toward extremist politics.

Three kinds of claim are kept apart. **Evidence** is what published studies have measured. **Argument** is the inference drawn from that evidence.
**Projection** is a labelled scenario, not a forecast that has already come true.

**Overall evidence strength.** Strong for the local labor-market effects of industrial robots, for technical task-exposure estimates, and for the link from
earlier economic shocks to unrest and extremist voting in Western democracies. Moderate for generative AI's measured productivity effects, which come from a few
workplace studies and do not yet show large employment losses. Weak for any precise worldwide job-loss total: no such total has been observed, and exposure is
not the same as unemployment.

## 1. How consumer AI displaces work worldwide

**Evidence.** Occupations are bundles of tasks. Computers have long substituted for routine, codifiable work and complemented non-routine abstract and in-person
work, which is why middle-skill routine jobs shrank while employment grew at the high and low ends of the wage distribution [1]. Industrial robots go further:
they are multipurpose, reprogrammable machines that can take over manual tasks previously done by people [2]. Large language models go further again. Eloundou,
Manning, Mishkin and Rock assess occupations by how far model capabilities, and software built on them, overlap with the tasks workers perform. In the
peer-reviewed *Science* version of that study, roughly 1.8 percent of jobs could have over half their tasks affected by language models used through simple
interfaces with general training; when current and likely future complementary software is included, that share rises to just over 46 percent of jobs [3]. The
authors treat this as a measure of technical relevance, not as a prediction of hiring or firing, and they argue that these models have the marks of a
general-purpose technology.

Frey and Osborne, using a different method and an earlier generation of machine learning and mobile robotics, estimated that 47 percent of US employment was in
occupations with a high probability of computerization (above 0.7). They placed transport and logistics, office and administrative support, and much of
production in the first wave, and found high probabilities for substantial shares of services, sales and construction as well. They were explicit that this was
technological possibility over an unspecified period, "perhaps a decade or two," not a forecast of how many jobs would actually disappear [4].

**Argument.** Consumer-market AI is the channel through which that task-level overlap becomes a labor-market event. A model sold to any firm, or bundled into
office, customer-service, coding and back-office software, can be applied at once to clerical work, writing, translation, basic analysis, customer support and
parts of professional practice. Unlike a robot fixed to a factory floor, the same system can be copied across occupations and countries at low marginal cost.

The worldwide character of the shock matters. Trade and offshoring moved some routine cognitive tasks from rich countries to service-export economies. The same
task classes now sit inside the exposure measures for language models [3]. **Argument, not a measured global layoff count:** if firms can obtain that work from
software, the commercial logic that sent it abroad weakens, and workers in both the originating and the receiving economies are exposed at the same time. A
trade shock was staggered and sectoral. A general-purpose software shock need not be.

## 2. Evidence so far

What has been measured is already enough to take displacement seriously, and not enough to treat mass unemployment as a completed fact.

Acemoglu and Restrepo study industrial robots in US commuting zones, using industry-level robot advances and local industry mix. Their preferred aggregate
estimate is that each additional robot per thousand workers reduces the employment-to-population ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by about 0.42
percent. They also report that one robot replaces about 3.3 workers. Between 1993 and 2007 the robot stock rose by about one robot per thousand workers in the
United States and by about 1.6 per thousand workers in Western Europe [2]. These are equilibrium estimates: they already net out offsetting job gains in other
local tasks and industries. The residual is still negative.

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond study a generative-AI assistant rolled out to 5,172 customer-support agents. Access raised issues resolved per hour by 15 percent
on average. Less experienced and lower-skilled agents improved in both speed and quality, including a 30 percent rise in issues resolved per hour; the most
experienced agents saw only small speed gains and small quality declines [5]. This is evidence of productivity and of a compressed return to experience. It is
not, by itself, evidence of layoffs. **Argument:** if a workplace can close 15 percent more cases per person, and if demand for the service does not rise by as
much, the firm can serve the same load with fewer people. The same study also implies that junior hiring is at risk: tools that give new staff the performance
of veterans reduce the need for large entry-level benches. That last step is an inference, not a result the authors report.

Two limits belong here, not only at the end. Robot studies describe a physical technology concentrated in manufacturing, not a general software tool. The
customer-support study describes augmentation inside an existing job, not the deletion of the job. The gap between those results and a claim of worldwide mass
unemployment is filled, in section 3, by labelled projections, not by additional facts.

## 3. Scenarios of worldwide mass job loss (projections)

The following three paths are **projections**. They apply published exposure and productivity estimates to different assumptions about substitution, demand and
the creation of new tasks. They are not forecasts, and they do not assign a date.

**Projection A — augmentation with limited net job loss.** Firms adopt tools of the kind studied by Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond [5]. Output per worker rises;
some tasks are reassigned rather than deleted; new work appears in supervision and complementary services, as Autor documents for earlier computerization [1].
Employment holds up in the aggregate, but routine cognitive work shrinks, pay gaps shift, and entry-level hiring weakens. This path is closest to the historical
average. It still redistributes work and income, and it is not a reason for complacency, because the next two paths remain open.

**Projection B — partial substitution, concentrated damage.** Complementary software makes a large minority of occupations majority-exposed, in the sense of the
"just over 46 percent" figure in Eloundou et al. [3], but only some of those tasks are in fact automated. Clerical work, customer support, translation, basic
document review, parts of sales and some mid-level analysis shrink over a decade. Factory robotics continues the employment and wage losses already estimated by
Acemoglu and Restrepo [2]. Service-export regions that specialized in offshored back-office and support work lose a development ladder at the same time that
rich-country white-collar work is cut. Official unemployment need not reach Depression levels for the shock to be socially large: it is enough that large
cohorts lose career paths in the same window, and that new hiring fails to absorb them.

**Projection C — rapid, broad substitution.** Firms treat the Frey and Osborne high-risk set, or the Eloundou complementary-software exposure set, as a
practical menu rather than a technical upper bound [3][4]. Adoption is fast because the tool is software. New-task creation, the historical safety valve in
Autor's account [1], is weaker because the same models can perform many of the "new" cognitive tasks. Labor demand falls in many countries at once. Tax bases
shrink just as spending on unemployment support rises. This is the path on which the unrest mechanism in section 4 is most dangerous. Nothing in the present
evidence shows that the world is already on this path. Nothing in the evidence rules it out once the tools are sold to every firm that wants them.

What massive worldwide job loss would look like, if Projection C or a hard form of B arrived, is therefore this: not a single occupation vanishing, but
simultaneous cuts in office work, customer service, media production, parts of professional services and remaining routine production; a freeze in junior
hiring; a hit to service-export employment in developing economies; regional pockets of long-term joblessness where alternative employers are thin; and fiscal
stress that invites the spending cuts studied in the next section. That picture is a projection built from the exposure literature. It should not be read as a
count of jobs already lost.

## 4. From job loss to unrest in Western democracies

The case for civil unrest does not rest on a modern study of "AI riots." No such study exists, because a consumer-scale general AI shock has not yet run its
course. The case rests on a mechanism measured for earlier labor-saving technology, for fiscal contraction after economic damage, and for labor-market shocks in
Western democracies today.

**Evidence, historical technology and unrest.** Caprettini and Voth study the Captain Swing unrest in England in 1830–32, a wave of machine-breaking, arson and
riots aimed at the threshing machine. Parishes that had adopted the machine were far more likely to see unrest: the probability of a riot was 13.6 percent
without the machines and 26.1 percent with them [6]. This is the cleanest available demonstration that labor-saving technology, adopted into ordinary economic
life, can produce civil unrest in a country that was already a leading polity of the West.

**Evidence, fiscal damage and unrest.** Mass job loss becomes a public-finance event if it shrinks revenue and raises the cost of support. Ponticelli and Voth
study Europe from 1919 to 2008 and find that expenditure cuts, more than tax rises, are associated with more unrest (demonstrations, riots, strikes and related
disturbances). Countries with a one standard deviation larger cut in government expenditure (2.5 percentage points of GDP) saw an increase of 0.16 unrest
episodes per 10 million inhabitants. In their estimated relationship, moving from a 1 percentage-point cut to a 10 percentage-point cut raises predicted unrest
from 0.1 to 1 episode per 10 million people; an equally large fall in growth, holding cuts fixed, has a milder effect [7]. **Argument:** a large AI labor shock
could create the fiscal conditions in which governments cut spending, and those cuts have a documented unrest effect in European history. That fiscal step is a
projection; the cut-unrest link is evidence.

**Evidence, crises and extremist voting.** Funke, Schularick and Trebesch study financial crises from 1870 to 2014 and find that far-right vote shares rise by
about 30 percent relative to their pre-crisis level over the following five years. They do not find a comparable far-right rise after ordinary recessions [8].
This is not a study of street violence, and it is not a study of AI. It shows that severe economic ruptures in Western and other electoral systems have a
regular political aftermath on the extreme right.

**Evidence, modern labor-market shocks and Western voting.** Several papers find that shocks which destroy or threaten work shift politics in Western
democracies toward polarization and the radical right, even when they do not produce a general collapse in employment. Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Majlesi find that
US commuting zones more exposed to Chinese import competition became more politically polarized [9]. Colantone and Stanig, using 76 legislative elections in 15
Western European countries, find that regions more exposed to the same import shock showed higher support for nationalist, isolationist and radical-right
parties and a general shift to the right [10]. Frey, Berger and Chen find that US counties more exposed to robots were more likely to swing toward the
Republican presidential candidate in 2016: comparing counties at the 75th and 25th percentiles of robot exposure, they estimate an additional 1.33
percentage-point rise in the Republican two-party vote share between 2012 and 2016 [11]. Anelli, Colantone and Stanig find that, in 13 Western European
countries between 1999 and 2015, people more exposed to industrial robot adoption were more likely to support radical-right parties, a result that remains after
accounting for nativism, status threat, cultural traditionalism and globalization [12].

**Argument.** The modern papers mostly measure votes, not riots. That is a real limit, and it is stated again in section 7. The reason they still support a
civil-unrest claim is the combination, not any one paper: labor-saving machines have already caused riots in a Western society [6]; economic ruptures and the
austerity that often follows them have already raised unrest and extreme-right voting in European and other democracies [7][8]; and the labor-market shocks most
like a first wave of automation — robots and import competition — have already moved Western electorates toward the radical right [9][10][11][12]. A consumer-AI
shock that was larger, faster and more widely felt would, on this record, put more strain on the same joints: household income, regional job prospects, trust in
government and the willingness to accept ordinary party competition. Civil unrest in this paper means demonstrations, strikes, riots and a wider breakdown of
public order. Extremist electoral waves are a closely related risk, not a synonym for unrest, and they can also precede it.

## 5. The case for confining AI to defense

**Argument.** If the danger comes from market-scale deployment of a labor-replacing general-purpose technology, the direct response is to keep that technology
out of the consumer and ordinary commercial market. Defense use would remain lawful under licence. Research, testing and production for military and closely
related official purposes would continue. What would not be permitted is the sale of frontier general-purpose models, or close substitutes, into offices, shops,
platforms and homes.

Democracies have used this structure before for technologies whose civilian diffusion was judged too dangerous, even when the underlying science had wide uses.

Nuclear technology is the leading case. Civilian nuclear power was not left to an ordinary consumer market. It is licensed, inspected and, for weapons-usable
materials and equipment, export-controlled under the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and the Nuclear Suppliers Group guidelines [13][14]. The
state retained a monopoly on weapons use. The precedent is not "ban physics." It is: confine the dangerous application, licence the narrow civilian exception,
and control exports of the critical inputs.

Cryptography is the closer precedent for software, and the more cautionary one. For much of the late twentieth century the United States treated strong
encryption as a munition under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, then as a dual-use item under export rules later coordinated in the Wassenaar
Arrangement [15][16]. The policy goal was to keep a general-purpose information technology from uncontrolled civilian and foreign circulation. Court challenges
and commercial pressure later loosened the US rules. The lesson cuts both ways. It shows that democracies have already tried to confine a general-purpose
information technology by licensing and export control. It also shows that software controls leak and that firms will fight them.

**Argument, why the nuclear analogy is still useful.** Today's frontier models depend on specialized chips, large training runs and a small number of
laboratories. Those inputs look more like enrichment equipment than like a pamphlet or a desktop cipher. Chip and model controls already exist in export-control
practice [16]. That industrial fact may not last; if it does not, enforcement gets harder, which is a limit, not a reason to release the technology first.

Confining AI to defense is not a licence for unconstrained military systems. Autonomous weapons, reduced human control over force, accidental escalation and the
use of military AI against a state's own population are serious risks. They need their own legal limits. The labor-market thesis does not cancel those risks,
and it does not justify them.

## 6. Strongest counterarguments and replies

**Past automation created new jobs.** Autor's history is the strongest version of this point: previous waves of mechanization and computerization destroyed
tasks and occupations, yet aggregate employment did not collapse, because new work appeared and because people moved into tasks machines did poorly [1].
Task-based studies also produce much lower "high risk" shares than occupation-based ones; Arntz, Gregory and Zierahn, using a task approach, estimate that only
about 9 percent of US jobs are automatable [17]. **Reply.** The historical average is real, and it is why Projection A cannot be dismissed. It does not bind a
technology that can perform a wide range of the cognitive tasks that used to be the refuge. Eloundou et al. is precisely an estimate that the refuge has shrunk
[3]. Even if net employment recovered after twenty or thirty years, Caprettini and Voth show that the transition itself can produce unrest [6]. A 9 percent
high-risk share is also not a small social fact if it is concentrated in time and place.

**Productivity gains raise demand for labor.** The 15 percent rise in issues resolved per hour is a real gain [5]. In standard theory, cheaper production can
lower prices, raise demand and create work elsewhere. **Reply.** That offset is already built into Acemoglu and Restrepo's framework, and their aggregate robot
estimates are still negative [2]. Productivity in a task is consistent with less hiring in that task. Gains that fall mainly to firms, or that shrink the junior
pipeline, can raise output and still leave a mass of people without a stable wage.

**Defense AI will leak into civilian use.** The internet and satellite navigation began as defense projects and became consumer infrastructure. Export controls
on cryptography were worn down. A defense-only rule could delay diffusion without preventing it. **Reply.** This objection is partly right, and a serious policy
must assume leakage. Delay still matters if the social risk is a fast, simultaneous shock. A licensing regime can also distinguish labor-replacing
general-purpose systems from narrow official tools. The failure of a past control to be permanent is not an argument for abandoning control at the start, any
more than leakage in nuclear smuggling is an argument for free trade in weapons-usable material.

**Concentrating AI in defense raises military and autonomous-weapons risks.** A state-centered AI sector could accelerate lethal autonomy, reduce public
scrutiny and invite an arms race. **Reply.** Those risks are real and should be constrained by law, treaties and rules on human control of force. They are not a
reason to add a worldwide labor shock to the list of dangers. The alternative on offer from the consumer market is not "no military AI." It is military AI plus
unrestricted civilian deployment.

**A defense-only rule cannot be enforced.** Models can be copied, trained in more than one country and reached through a network. **Reply.** Enforcement would
be imperfect, as it is for export-controlled goods and for nuclear materials. It is more feasible while training still needs scarce chips and large, visible
facilities. A coalition of governments can condition market access, chip sales and public contracts on the rule. None of that yields a hermetic seal. The
standard is whether circulation is reduced enough to prevent a rapid, economy-wide substitution shock, not whether every illicit copy is prevented.

**Countries that commercialize AI will outcompete those that do not.** If one bloc sells general-purpose AI into every industry and another confines it to
defense, the first bloc may grow faster and fund a larger military. **Reply.** This is the hardest strategic objection. Coordinated controls, on the model of
the Nuclear Suppliers Group, are the only complete answer [14]. Absent coordination, a state still has to weigh growth against a documented path from
labor-market damage to unrest and extremist politics at home [6][7][8][11][12]. A society that undergoes that path may not keep the productivity advantage it
was trying to protect. Defense-only development also still allows the frontier to move, just not through a mass consumer market.

## 7. Limits of the evidence

Exposure is not unemployment. Frey and Osborne and Eloundou et al. measure technical possibility, and both sets of authors warn against reading their figures as
job-loss forecasts [3][4]. The robot results are the best causal evidence of displacement, but they concern industrial robots, mostly in manufacturing, in a
period before large language models [2]. The best generative-AI workplace study measures productivity inside a job, not the destruction of that job, and it
covers one firm and one occupation [5].

The unrest and political papers are about other shocks: threshing machines in the 1830s, budget cuts, financial crises, Chinese import competition and
industrial robots [6][7][8][9][10][11][12]. None of them is a study of consumer AI and civil unrest in a Western democracy today. The modern evidence is
stronger for voting than for riots. Mechanisms can travel across shocks and still fail in a new one, especially if new work, shorter hours or public insurance
absorb the blow.

The defense-only recommendation is a policy judgment, not an empirical result. Historical controls on nuclear technology and cryptography are imperfect
analogies. Encryption policy was partly reversed. Defense research has repeatedly seeded civilian technology. The paper does not present evidence that a
defense-only rule would be easy to police, or that it would leave military risk unchanged.

No worldwide count of jobs already lost to generative AI is used here, because a reliable peer-reviewed total of that kind was not established for this version.

## 8. Policy options short of a full ban

A complete consumer-market ban is the thesis, not the only instrument. If governments reject a ban, or can enforce only part of one, the same evidence still
supports slower and narrower civilian deployment.

- **Licensing above a compute or capability threshold.** Frontier general-purpose models would need a licence; narrow official and defense systems would be the default permitted class.
- **Sectoral delays or moratoria.** Independent of a full ban, governments could postpone unrestricted use in large-scale customer service, clerical work and other occupations where substitution risk is already visible [3][5].
- **Export controls on chips, trained models and training services.** These already exist in outline in dual-use regimes [16]. They can be aimed at labor-replacing general-purpose systems, not at all software.
- **Mandatory human responsibility.** Consumer-facing decisions about hiring, firing, credit, housing and policing would not be delegated to a model, even if some civilian use is allowed.
- **Taxes on substitution, and wage insurance.** If civilian use continues, a tax on labor-replacing deployments, and insurance for displaced workers, would not prevent a shock but could blunt the income collapse that feeds unrest [7].
- **Public creation of new tasks.** Autor's account of why jobs survived earlier automation depends on new work appearing [1]. That process can be left to firms, or it can be treated as a public obligation if firms are deploying the technology that erases the old tasks.
- **Military constraints in parallel.** Human control over the use of force, and limits on autonomous weapons, should accompany any defense-centered regime so that the labor-market policy does not become a weapons policy by default.

These options are second-best relative to the thesis. They are listed because a defense-only rule may be only partly adopted, and because a partial rule is
still better than unregulated consumer deployment if the unrest mechanism in section 4 is taken seriously.
