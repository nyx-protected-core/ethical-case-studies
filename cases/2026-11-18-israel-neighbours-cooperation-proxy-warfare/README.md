# Israel and its neighbours: the case for cooperation, and the role of proxy warfare

A sourced brief for a public ethics case study. It sets out (1) the strategic, economic and security case for cooperation between Israel and neighbouring states, (2) the evidence on Iran's support for armed groups, (3) two analyst hypotheses tested against public evidence, and (4) counterarguments, Palestinian perspectives and humanitarian-law obligations. The brief does not take a side. Each claim is tagged **[well-supported]**, **[contested]** or **[unsupported]**, and each factual statement points to a numbered source. Bracketed numbers such as [6a] refer to the Sources list.

**Verification note.** Every link below was checked on 18 Nov 2026 (UTC+7). Each DOI was resolved through `https://doi.org/` and returned HTTP 302 to the publisher. Author, year and venue for DOI items come from the DOI registry record. Each URL returned HTTP 200 unless marked otherwise. Items marked **live-blocked** returned 401, 403 or 202 to an automated request (a paywall or bot-check). Those pages are known to exist from search-engine indexing, and where possible an open copy or a secondary report is listed alongside. Some numbered entries group two or three documents from the same issuer or on the same narrow point, so 22 entries cover 29 primary documents (open mirrors and secondary copies are listed beside them and not counted). Figures are quoted only where they appear in the cited document.

## Part 1: The case for cooperation

**1.1 The two peace treaties have lasted for decades. [well-supported]** The Egypt–Israel treaty (1979) [1] and the Israel–Jordan treaty (1994) [2] have stayed in force through several regional wars. The Jordan treaty went beyond security: Annex II sets water allocations and a Joint Water Committee [2], so water became a matter of treaty cooperation and not only of dispute. Both treaties are often described as a "cold peace": cooperation between governments, including on security, while the public stays hostile. In 2021–22, only 5% of Egyptians and 5% of Jordanians favoured normalisation [8].

**1.2 The Abraham Accords had a security logic and an economic logic. [well-supported]** The 2020 Accords with the UAE, Bahrain, Morocco and Sudan [3] built on years of quiet ties, driven by shared concern about Iran and about how long the US would stay engaged in the region [4][5]. Results followed. Israel's first free-trade agreement with an Arab state (the UAE) was concluded in 2022, and security agreements were signed with Morocco (2021) and Bahrain (2022) [5]. None of the Accord states cut ties after October 2023 [5].

**1.3 Water, energy and gas show that interdependence is possible. [well-supported as design; stalled in practice]** Project Prosperity was designed as two halves that depend on each other: Jordanian solar power for Israel, and Israeli desalinated water for Jordan [6a]. Each side would hold a stake in the other's supply, which is a standard argument for interdependence as a stabiliser. In November 2023, however, Jordan said it would not sign, citing the Gaza war [6b]. The EMGF brings Israel, Egypt, Jordan and Palestine together with European states in one gas body [7]. That is notable because it puts Israel and Palestine in the same intergovernmental forum.

**1.4 Saudi normalisation was moving before October 2023 and has stalled since. [well-supported]** Before the war, Riyadh pursued "creeping normalization", including airspace access for Israeli flights and quiet security contacts. It was assessed as willing to move without the immediate creation of a Palestinian state [9]. Since then, Saudi Arabia has made a credible path to Palestinian statehood a precondition [5][9], and INSS assessed in February 2026 that normalisation is "currently off the table" [9]. Several sources report that disrupting Saudi–Israeli normalisation was one of Hamas's aims [5][9]. That point is **[contested]** as to motive, but widely reported.

**1.5 What made agreements last or stall.**
- *Durable:* state-to-state security interests, written treaty commitments, and limits on how much the public has to accept. The treaties survived while publics stayed opposed [1][2][8]. **[well-supported]**
- *Fragile:* public opinion and the Palestinian question. Moroccan support for normalisation fell from 31% to 13% after October 2023, and Arab Barometer ties the fall to perceptions of the Gaza war [8]. Projects that needed visible joint signings (Prosperity) were the first to stop [6b]. **[well-supported]**
- *Leadership and domestic politics:* Saudi policy shifted with the crown prince's domestic legitimacy needs [9]. Sudan's civil war left its accession unratified [5]. Egypt's president called Israel the "enemy" in September 2025 [5]. **[well-supported]**

## Part 2: Iran and armed groups

**2.1 Documented support.**
- **Hezbollah. [well-supported]** The US government says Iran has supplied thousands of rockets, missiles and small arms since 2006, has provided "hundreds of millions of dollars", and has trained thousands of fighters [10]. Independent scholarship treats Hezbollah as the closest of Iran's partners [12b][14].
- **Hamas and PIJ. [well-supported as to material support]** The US government reports Iranian weapons systems and "long-standing material, financial, and training support" to Hamas, and support to PIJ [10]. The US national security adviser said in October 2023 that Iran provided "the lion's share" of funding for Hamas's military wing [16a]. That is an official assertion; the underlying figures are not public.
- **Houthis. [well-supported]** The UN Panel of Experts inspected intercepted components and missile debris bearing makes, markings and serial numbers like those produced in Iran. It concluded that without foreign support the Houthis could not build complex systems such as ballistic and cruise missiles [11]. It reports technical assistance, training, weapons and money from Iran, Iraqi armed groups and Hezbollah, and joint operational centres in Iraq and Lebanon [11].

**2.2 Theory: delegation and deniability.** Principal–agent theory holds that states delegate violence to non-state groups to cut costs and risk, at the price of agency loss: the agent may follow its own aims [13]. Ostovar treats militant clients as central to Iran's way of war, citing extended reach and lower political cost alongside escalation risk [14]. The US government states that Iran "uses regional militant and proxy groups to facilitate deniability" [10]. That is an official interpretation, consistent with the theory but not a neutral finding.

**2.3 How much control Iran has. [contested]** Sources differ on how far Iran *directs* each group, as opposed to *supplying* it.
- Katz describes a spectrum "from ally to proxy" and an "Iranian-led alliance", not simple command [12b].
- The Yemen panel says the Houthis "exploited the regional situation" to deepen cooperation with the axis [11]. That reads as a partner acting on its own interests, not one carrying out orders.
- The seized Hamas minutes, as reported, show Hamas *trying to persuade* Iran and Hezbollah to join, and going ahead without a firm commitment [17]. That suggests Hamas did not need Iran's permission on timing.

*Documented vs asserted:* the transfers of arms, money and training are documented by UN inspection and official reports [10][11][19]. Operational command over specific attacks is mostly asserted, and varies by group.

## Part 3: Two hypotheses tested

### Hypothesis 1: that the 7 October 2023 Hamas attack functioned as a distraction sought by Iran

*Note on framing.* The hypothesis does not say what the attack would distract from (for example Saudi–Israeli normalisation, or attention on Iran's nuclear programme). The evidence below bears on whether Iran **sought, directed or timed** the attack.

| Evidence | Direction | Source quality |
|---|---|---|
| Iran's long-term arms, money and training "enabled Hamas to execute the attack" | Supports Iranian *enablement*, not timing | US official report [10] |
| IRGC helped plan from August and gave a "green light" in Beirut on 2 October 2023 | Supports | Anonymous Hamas/Hezbollah sources; denied on the record by a Hamas official in the same article [15] |
| Early US intelligence: key Iranian officials appeared surprised | Against | Anonymous US officials, explicitly preliminary [16a] |
| Supreme leader reportedly told Hamas "You gave us no warning" | Against | Three anonymous senior officials; self-serving for Iran [16b] |
| US government: "no evidence that Iran knew… in advance" | Against | Official, later than the October 2023 reporting [10] |
| Seized minutes: Hamas delayed the plan to court Iran; in July 2023 an IRGC officer reportedly welcomed it "in principle" but asked for time | Mixed: suggests general awareness of a plan, but Hamas setting the timing, possibly ahead of Iran's preference | Documents seized by the Israeli military and reviewed by a newspaper; Iran calls them fabricated [17] |
| Disrupting Saudi normalisation was reportedly a goal | Consistent with a shared Iran–Hamas interest; does not show Iranian direction | Analyst and parliamentary sources [5][9] |

**Verdict: CONTESTED overall. The strong form is UNSUPPORTED on public evidence.**
- *Supported:* Iran's long-standing support made the attack possible [10][16a]. An attack that derailed Saudi normalisation served interests that Iran and Hamas shared [5][9].
- *Contested:* whether parts of the Iranian system had general foreknowledge of a plan. The reported seized minutes point that way [17]; US intelligence and the US government's later report point the other way [10][16a].
- *Unsupported:* that Iran sought, ordered or timed the attack as a distraction. No public source shows Iranian direction of the timing. The most detailed documentary evidence, if authentic, shows Hamas deciding on timing after failing to secure a commitment from Iran [17].

### Hypothesis 2: that Russia's war in Ukraine functioned as a strategic distraction or supply-chain strain that benefited Iran while its nuclear programme advanced

**Timeline (from listed sources):**
- **17 Apr 2021:** IAEA verifies that Iran has begun producing 60%-enriched uranium [18a].
- **Feb 2022:** Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine (a matter of public record; the US sanctions documents describe the invasion [19a]).
- **Sep–Nov 2022:** US designations over Iranian Shahed UAVs used by Russia in Ukraine [19a].
- **Sep 2024:** US says Iran transferred ballistic missiles to Russia [19b]. Press reports name the Fath-360 system; that model name is **UNVERIFIED** here, because the cited official text does not name it.
- **13 Jun 2025:** IAEA estimate of 440.9 kg of uranium enriched up to 60% [18c].
- **13–24 Jun 2025:** Israeli strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities; **22 Jun 2025:** US strikes on three facilities [18b][18c].
- **2 Jul 2025:** Iran suspends cooperation with the IAEA by law [18c].
- **28 Sep 2025:** Earlier UN Security Council resolutions on Iran apply again [18b].
- **Jun 2026:** IAEA still cannot verify the stockpile or whether enrichment has stopped [18c].

**What is supported. [well-supported]**
- Russia–Iran arms cooperation is documented: Iranian-designed UAVs used by Russia in Ukraine [19a], and later ballistic-missile and munitions transfers [19b].
- Iran's enrichment advanced sharply: from 60% production beginning in April 2021 [18a] to 440.9 kg at 60% by June 2025 [18c]. Verification gaps are severe, and the IAEA reports loss of continuity of knowledge [18c].

**What is contested. [contested]**
- Whether the war *benefited* Iran overall. Arms sales deepened ties with Russia [19a][19b]. But Iran also drew new US sanctions for those same transfers [19a][19b], its nuclear sites were struck in 2025 [18b], and UN measures were reapplied [18b]. The listed sources do not settle the net effect.

**What is unsupported. [unsupported]**
- That the war was a *deliberate distraction*, engineered by or for Iran. No listed source shows such intent.
- That the war *caused* the nuclear advance. The 60% step began about ten months **before** the invasion [18a], so the war cannot explain its start. Other explanations are not tested here, such as Iran's long-running escalation after the 2015 nuclear deal broke down.
- That a "supply-chain strain" benefited Iran. The listed sources show Iran using procurement networks for Western-origin parts, some for UAVs later provided to Russia [19b]. They do not show a strain that advantaged Iran's nuclear programme.

**Verdict: PARTLY SUPPORTED on the components (the arms cooperation and the enrichment advance are documented). UNSUPPORTED on the causal "distraction" claim.**

## Key findings

1. State-to-state peace with Egypt and Jordan has lasted for decades despite hostile public opinion. Security interests and treaty structure appear to carry these agreements more than popular support [1][2][8]. **[well-supported]**
2. The Abraham Accords produced trade and security ties that survived the Gaza war at government level [5], but public support for normalisation fell sharply, most clearly in Morocco [8]. **[well-supported]**
3. Cooperation projects that need visible joint action (Prosperity) stalled first after October 2023 [6b]. Multilateral frameworks (EMGF) include Palestinians alongside Israel [7]. **[well-supported]**
4. Saudi normalisation now depends on a credible path to Palestinian statehood [5][9]. **[well-supported]**
5. Iranian material support to Hezbollah, Hamas, PIJ and the Houthis is documented by official and UN sources [10][11]. **[well-supported]** The degree of operational command varies by group and is disputed [11][12b][17]. **[contested]**
6. On public evidence, Iran enabled but did not demonstrably time or direct the 7 October attack [10][16][17].
7. Russia–Iran arms cooperation and Iran's enrichment advance are both documented [18][19], but nothing in the public record shows the war was a deliberate distraction that caused the nuclear advance.

## Contested and uncertain points

- **Provenance of the seized Hamas minutes.** They were seized by one party to the war and reported by a newspaper whose page could not be fetched for this brief. Iran calls them fabricated [17]. Treat them as significant but not conclusive.
- **Anonymous sourcing on both sides of Hypothesis 1.** The WSJ claim of Iranian involvement [15] and the US and Iranian accounts of surprise [16] all rest on unnamed officials, and each party has reasons to shape the story.
- **Funding figures.** "Hundreds of millions of dollars" for Hezbollah [10] and "the lion's share" of Hamas military-wing funding [16a] are official assertions without public underlying data.
- **IAEA picture after June 2025.** The Agency cannot verify the stockpile's location or whether enrichment continues [18c], so current status is unknown, not merely unreported.
- **Cooperation and normalisation figures after 2025.** Trade volumes and project status after October 2023 are not quantified in the listed sources.
- **Missile model naming (Fath-360)** is UNVERIFIED against an official text in this brief.

## Counterarguments and balance

**4.1 Palestinian grievances predate and are independent of Iran. [well-supported]** The ICJ advised in July 2024 that Israel's continued presence in the Occupied Palestinian Territory is unlawful, that new settlement activity should cease, and that settlers should be evacuated [20a]. Baconi traces Hamas's trajectory through Palestinian politics, the 2007 split and the blockade of Gaza [22]. The occupation of the West Bank and Gaza dates from 1967, more than a decade before the 1979 Iranian revolution (a matter of public record). Any account that starts from Tehran leaves out the occupation, the settlements and the blockade.

**4.2 "Proxy" framing can erase agency. [contested, with strong support for the critique]** Treating Palestinian armed groups as Iranian instruments assumes a control that the evidence does not show. The seized minutes, as reported, depict Hamas trying to recruit Iran, not taking orders from it [17]. Katz describes alliance dynamics rather than command [12b]. Principal–agent theory itself predicts that agents act on their own aims [13]. The critique does not deny Iranian support [10]. It warns that "proxy" can stand in for an analysis of motive.

**4.3 Humanitarian-law obligations bind all parties. [well-supported as legal findings; Israel disputes them]**
- The UN Commission of Inquiry found that Hamas and other armed groups deliberately killed civilians, took hostages and committed sexual violence on 7 October. These are war crimes [21].
- It found that Israel committed war crimes and crimes against humanity in Gaza, disregarded distinction, proportionality and precautions, and inflicted collective punishment [21].
- The ICJ ordered provisional measures under the Genocide Convention in January 2024. The merits are undecided [20b].
- Israel rejected the Commission's findings as biased [21].

The principles of distinction, proportionality and the ban on collective punishment apply whatever side or sponsor is involved [21].

**4.4 The strongest objection to the cooperation thesis: normalisation "over the heads" of Palestinians is unstable. [well-supported by recent evidence]** The Palestinian Authority criticised the Accords for neglecting a two-state solution [5]. When the Palestinian question flared, the most visible cooperation stalled: Jordan withdrew from Prosperity [6b], Saudi talks froze [9], and Arab publics turned further against normalisation [8]. Arab Barometer concludes that a "warm peace" would only have a chance after an independent Palestinian state is established [8]. Defenders of the thesis reply that state ties held even under that strain: no Accord state cut relations [5], and both treaties survived [1][2]. On this evidence, cooperation can last as a cold peace, but deepening it appears to depend on progress for Palestinians.

**4.5 A fair conclusion.** The evidence supports both of the following: cooperation between states brings real security and economic gains, and Iran arms groups that attack Israel. It does not support reducing the conflict to Iranian design, and it does not support a cooperation model that leaves the Palestinian question aside. A credible case for cooperation also has to take humanitarian-law obligations and Palestinian political agency seriously.
