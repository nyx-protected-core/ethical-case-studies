# Child and Forced Marriage: Scale, What Works, Law, Economic Shocks, and Links to Trafficking

*Ethics case-study source brief. Anonymous author. The brief covers policy and prevention only and contains no individual case narratives. Every factual claim is attributed to a numbered source. Items that could not be verified are marked UNVERIFIED. All URLs and DOIs were checked live in November 2026 (all DOIs resolve with 302). Live-blocks (403) are noted, with an open copy given where one exists.*

*Revisions (18 November 2026).* Revised after an independent review. The changes correct facts, fill gaps and tighten wording. The review is not named here.

**Evidence labels:**
- RCT = randomised controlled trial
- QE = quasi-experimental (difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, matched comparison)
- OBS = observational or cross-sectional
- MOD = model or projection (not a measured outcome)
- SR = systematic review
- REP = institutional report
- WP = working paper (not peer-reviewed)

**Terms (kept distinct throughout):**
- **Child marriage:** a formal marriage or informal union in which at least one party is under 18. It is measured by SDG indicator 5.3.1: the share of women aged 20–24 first married or in union before 18 [1, 3].
- **Forced marriage:** a marriage entered without free and full consent, or one the person cannot leave. UN treaty bodies treat child marriage as a form of forced marriage because a child cannot give full consent [18].
- **Trafficking for forced marriage / "bride trafficking":** a marriage that also meets all three elements of the Trafficking in Persons Protocol (act, means, purpose of exploitation). Not every forced or child marriage is trafficking [20].

### B. Causal evidence on what reduces child marriage

5. **Baird S, McIntosh C, Özler B, 2011.** "Cash or Condition? Evidence from a Cash Transfer Experiment." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 126(4):1709–1753. DOI 10.1093/qje/qjr032.

   *Note:* Zomba (Malawi) cash-transfer RCT for adolescent girls, with arms for transfers conditional on schooling (CCT) and unconditional transfers (UCT).
   - Schooling: CCTs cut dropout more; the UCT effect on dropout was only 43% as large.
   - Marriage and teen pregnancy: rates were substantially lower in the UCT arm than in the CCT arm. This came entirely from girls who dropped out of school.

   [RCT; peer-reviewed]

6. **Baird S, McIntosh C, Özler B, 2019.** "When the money runs out: Do cash transfers have sustained effects on human capital accumulation?" *Journal of Development Economics* 140:169–185.    *Note:* Follow-up two years after the Zomba transfers ended.
   - **UCT arm:** the reductions in teen marriage and pregnancy "evaporated", with a spike in marriages and pregnancies right after transfers stopped. By Round 4 UCTs had reduced neither child marriage nor teen pregnancy.
   - **CCT arm:** among girls out of school at baseline, effects persisted. They were 14.0, 15.7 and 10.7 percentage points less likely to have ever married at Rounds 2–4.
   - **CCT arm, baseline schoolgirls:** no effect on marriage at any point.

   [RCT; peer-reviewed]

7. **Amin S, Saha JS, Ahmed JA, 2018.** "Skills-Building Programs to Reduce Child Marriage in Bangladesh: A Randomized Controlled Trial." *Journal of Adolescent Health* 63(3):293–300. DOI 10.1016/j.jadohealth.2018.05.013. Full Population Council report (BALIKA): Amin S, Ahmed J, Saha J, Hossain M, Haque E, 2016, DOI 10.31899/pgy9.1009 [REP].

   *Note:* Four-arm cluster RCT.
   - Arms: education support, gender-rights awareness, livelihoods training, and control. Each offered 144 hours of training over 18 months.

   [RCT; peer-reviewed]

   *Note:* Quasi-experimental (intervention vs control area, baseline/endline) evaluation of a 2004–06 pilot. Components: girls' groups, school support, community awareness, and a conditional goat.
   - Girls aged 10–14: much less likely to have ever married (odds ratio 0.1) and more likely to be in school (OR 3.0).
   - Girls aged 15–19: *more* likely to have married by endline (OR 2.4).

### C. Minimum-age-of-marriage laws

12. **Maswikwa B, Richter L, Kaufman J, Nandi A, 2015.** "Minimum Marriage Age Laws and the Prevalence of Child Marriage and Adolescent Birth: Evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa." *International Perspectives on Sexual and Reproductive Health* 41(2):58 ff. DOI 10.1363/4105815.

    *Note:* Cross-sectional DHS analysis of 12 countries. Countries with *consistent* laws (18+ for marriage, for marriage with parental consent, and for the age of sexual consent) had 40% lower child-marriage prevalence (prevalence ratio 0.6) and 25% lower teen childbearing. The design is associational, not causal.

    [OBS; peer-reviewed]

13. **Collin M, Talbot T, 2023.** "Are age-of-marriage laws enforced? Evidence from developing countries." *Journal of Development Economics* 160:102950. DOI 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2022.102950. Abstract: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/deveco/v160y2023ics0304387822000967.html

    *Note:* Uses regression-discontinuity density tests on DHS/MICS data from more than 100 countries to look for bunching of marriages at the legal age. Many countries are "not enforcing the laws on their books", and enforcement may not be improving. Legal change needs enforcement and monitoring to work.

    [QE; peer-reviewed]

14. **McGavock T, 2021.** "Here waits the bride? The effect of Ethiopia's child marriage law." *Journal of Development Economics* 149:102580. DOI 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2020.102580. Abstract: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/deveco/v149y2021ics0304387820301553.html

    *Note:* Difference-in-differences and event-study analysis of Ethiopia's staggered regional roll-out (from 2000) raising the minimum age from 15 to 18.
    - Marriages of girls under 16 fell by about 17% (6.8 percentage points) where early marriage was common.
    - The effect was larger but insignificant in ethnic groups with the strongest early-marriage norms.

    [QE; peer-reviewed]

15. **Batyra E, Pesando LM, 2021.** "Trends in child marriage and new evidence on the selective impact of changes in age-at-marriage laws on early marriage." *SSM – Population Health* 14:100811. DOI 10.1016/j.ssmph.2021.100811.

    *Note:* Regression-discontinuity analysis of law changes in six countries.
    - No effect in Benin, Mauritania, Kazakhstan and Bhutan.
    - Reductions in Tajikistan and Nepal depended on model specification.
    - Laws "rarely achieve the desired outcome" without enforcement and monitoring.

    [QE; peer-reviewed, open access]

### D. Economic shocks, conflict and crises

16. **Corno L, Hildebrandt N, Voena A, 2020.** "Age of Marriage, Weather Shocks, and the Direction of Marriage Payments." *Econometrica* 88(3):879–915. DOI 10.3982/ECTA15505.

    *Note:* Covers about 400,000 women. Droughts cut crop yields by 10–15% and income by 4–5%, and their effect depends on the direction of marriage payments:

## KEY FINDINGS

1. **Scale and trend.**
   - Scale: UNICEF's 2026 estimates put the number of women and girls alive today who were married in childhood at about 650 million, or about one in five girls married before 18 [23]. The earlier 2023 estimate was about 640 million, with about 12 million girls marrying each year [1].
   - Trend: prevalence is falling (21% to 19% of women aged 20–24), mainly because of South Asia and India [1, 2]. Progress is about 20 times too slow for the 2030 target [1].
   - Stalls: sub-Saharan Africa is more than 200 years from elimination. Middle East and North Africa, and Eastern Europe and Central Asia, have stagnated [1].
   - Inequality: prevalence among the poorest girls rose or stagnated in most regions [2]. Parts of the Sahel reach about 90% [2].
   - Forced marriage "occur[s] in every region of the world and cut[s] across ethnic, cultural, and religious lines" [18]. Regional and corridor figures in this brief are about measured prevalence or about particular case samples, not about any one culture or nationality.

2. **Measurement.**
   - All headline figures rest on retrospective, self-reported age at first union from DHS/MICS, using women aged 20–24 [1, 3]. That indicator lags current practice by several years.
   - Trend breaks can partly reflect data-collection processes, as in Mali [4].
   - Global "number per year" figures are proxies (girls reaching 18 who married before 18), not direct counts [3].

3. **Cash and schooling support have the strongest causal evidence, but whether effects persist depends on design.**
   - **During the programme:** Malawi's unconditional transfers cut marriage among girls who had already left school [5].
   - **After transfers stopped:** that effect vanished, with a marriage spike once payments ended [6]. Schooling-conditional transfers produced *lasting* delays for baseline dropouts, but did nothing for girls already in school [6].
   - **Systematic review:** schooling-support transfers succeed most often (8 of 10 quality studies), while poverty-relief unconditional cash showed no effect (3 studies) [10].

4. **Girls' skills and empowerment programmes can work at scale-relevant cost.**
   - BALIKA (RCT): education, gender-rights and livelihoods arms each cut child marriage by about 25–30% [7].
   - Berhane Hewan (quasi-experimental): only the youngest girls (10–14) benefited; marriage *rose* among 15–19-year-olds [8].
   - Low-cost conditional asset transfers helped older girls in Ethiopia and Tanzania, but some comparisons were against baseline only [9].
   - Multicomponent packages have a poor success record [10].

5. **Laws alone change little.**
   - Cross-sectional associations are large: 40% lower prevalence where laws are consistent [12].
   - Causal and enforcement-focused studies find weaker effects:
     - Many countries show no bunching at the legal age [13].
     - Six-country regression-discontinuity results are null or fragile [15].
     - Ethiopia's reform delayed the youngest marriages but not where norms were strongest [14].
   - Mali's 2011 Family Code set the minimum age at 16 for girls and 18 for boys, with exceptions from 15, after a 2009 text setting 18 was not enacted [24]. Batyra and Pesando describe this as lowering the general minimum from 18 to 16. The change coincided with rises among the poorest [4].
   - Only 35 countries, in the ILO report's account, set 18 as the minimum age with no exceptions; in many others, 16- and 17-year-olds may marry with a court ruling or parental consent [18]. The problem is not confined to the countries named in this brief.

6. **Economic shocks move marriage timing through marriage payments.**
   - Drought *raises* child-marriage hazard where bride price is paid (sub-Saharan Africa, +3%) and *lowers* it where dowry is paid (India, −4%) [16].
   - Conflict effects are mixed in direction, and the evidence is thin [17].
   - COVID-19 figures (10–13 million additional marriages) are projections [3]. No measured global increase attributable to COVID-19 was found among these sources.

7. **Trafficking is an overlapping but much smaller category.**
   - About 22 million people live in forced marriage [18], yet forced-marriage trafficking is only about 1% of *detected* trafficking victims [19]. The gap reflects definitions and detection, not necessarily rarity.
   - UNODC stresses that forced or child marriage is trafficking only when act, means and exploitative purpose all coexist [20].
   - Documented cross-border bride trafficking from Myanmar into China is real but estimated in the thousands, and wider extrapolations are speculative [21].
   - India's cross-region marriages tied to skewed sex ratios include trafficking, but research warns against labelling them all as such [22].

## SURVIVOR-CENTRED PRINCIPLES

Prevention is not enough. Girls and women already married, or trying to leave a forced marriage, need support in their own right. This section is about policy, not individual stories.

- The person's safety, consent and choices come first.
- No action should expose her to retaliation from a spouse, family or community.
- Support services should come before, or at least alongside, prosecution: safe exit routes, shelter, legal aid, health care, and a way to keep or return to school.
- Girls already married should not be penalised, for example by being excluded from school or treated as offenders.
- Responses should not criminalise the girl herself, or forcibly separate her in a way that leaves her without shelter or support.

These principles apply as much to anti-trafficking work as to child-marriage prevention. Section 7 of the solutions list should not be read as a prosecution-first approach.

## CONTESTED / UNCERTAIN POINTS

- **Unconditional vs conditional cash.**
  - In Malawi, unconditional transfers outperformed conditional ones on marriage during the programme [5], but the effect did not last [6].
  - The review finds no effect for poverty-relief unconditional cash [10].
  - Whether to condition on schooling, on marriage, or on nothing remains context-dependent.
- **Persistence.** Most programme evaluations follow girls for only 1–3 years. The only long-run follow-up here [6] shows a rebound after transfers end. Durable effects of skills programmes beyond endline are **UNVERIFIED** in these sources [7].
- **Design quality.**
  - Berhane Hewan 2009 is quasi-experimental [8].
  - The 2017 Ethiopia arm used baseline, not a control, as the comparison [9].
  - The Malhotra–Elnakib review weights by quality but includes grey literature [10].
- **Law effects.**
  - The cross-sectional association [12] may reflect reverse causation: countries with less child marriage find it easier to legislate.
  - Quasi-experimental studies [13, 14, 15] find weaker or null effects.
  - Enforcement capacity and norms appear decisive, but which enforcement tools work is not established here.
- **Measurement.** Self-reported ages, informal unions, and recall bias in DHS/MICS can shift estimates [1, 4]. Sub-national figures such as the roughly 90% for parts of the Sahel [2] carry wide uncertainty.
- **COVID-19.** The 10–13 million figures are model outputs with strong assumptions: a one-year programme delay, a 10% GDP shock, and doubling five-country results [3]. They should not be cited as observed increases.
- **Conflict.** The direction of the effect varies by setting [17]. Global claims that conflict uniformly raises child marriage are not supported by this review.
- **Trafficking figures.** Detected-victim data [19] capture only cases that reach the authorities. The modelled forced-marriage estimate [18] is broader and survey-based. Bride-trafficking estimates for the Myanmar–China corridor [21b] rest on extrapolation.
  - A widely repeated figure of "7,500 women trafficked 2013–2017" did not match the report's own tables in this check. The report gives about 4,997 returnees in forced marriage (mid-range) and about 3,889 trafficked into it, so the 7,500 figure is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **India cross-region marriages.** Activists frame these as widespread trafficking; ethnographic work disputes this [22]. The share that is trafficking is **UNVERIFIED** in these sources.

## SOLUTIONS RANKED BY STRENGTH OF EVIDENCE

1. **Keep girls in school through cash or in-kind schooling support (CCTs, fee waivers, school supplies).**
   - *Evidence: strong.* Multiple RCTs and the clearest pattern in a systematic review [5, 6, 10].
   - Schooling-conditional transfers produced lasting marriage delays among out-of-school girls [6].
   - Caveat: no effect on girls already enrolled [6].

2. **Girls' skills-building programmes (education support, rights awareness, livelihoods), delivered at community level.**
   - *Evidence: moderate to strong.* One large cluster RCT with similar effects across arms [7], and consistent positive findings in the review [10].
   - Long-run persistence is not established [7].

3. **Unconditional cash during economic stress.**
   - *Evidence: mixed.* It reduced marriage while paid but rebounded once transfers ended [5, 6]. No effect when designed purely for poverty relief [10].
   - Shock evidence [16] suggests timing transfers to droughts in bride-price settings is promising, but not tested here.

4. **Small conditional asset transfers tied to staying unmarried and in school.**
   - *Evidence: moderate but uneven.* Positive for older girls in two of three countries [9]; 2 of 4 studies positive in the review [10].
   - Low cost (about US$30 per girl per year in Ethiopia) [9].

5. **Community dialogue and norm-change components.**
   - *Evidence: weak to mixed.* Some age-specific reductions [9]. Multicomponent packages succeed rarely [10].
   - Berhane Hewan's combined package helped only the youngest girls [8].

6. **Raising and harmonising the minimum marriage age to 18 with no exceptions.**
   - *Evidence: weak alone; moderate with enforcement and favourable norms.*
   - Associational benefits [12]. Null or fragile causal effects in many settings [13, 15]. Partial effects in Ethiopia [14].
   - Repealing protections, or lowering a stated general age, may harm the poorest. Mali's 2011 Family Code set 16 for girls and 18 for boys, with exceptions from 15, after a 2009 text setting 18 was not enacted [4, 24].
   - Only 35 countries, in the ILO report's account, already follow the 18-with-no-exceptions recommendation [18].
   - Pair laws with enforcement, birth and marriage registration, and monitoring of age-at-marriage distributions [13, 15].

7. **Anti-trafficking responses to forced and bride marriage (victim identification, cross-border police cooperation, prosecution of brokers).**
   - *Evidence: descriptive only.* Case and court analyses [19, 21a] and expert interviews [20]; no evaluations found.
   - Applying trafficking law should follow the three-element test [20], so that ordinary or ambiguous marriage migration is not criminalised [22].

8. **Services for those already married: safe exit, shelter, legal aid, health care, and schooling and support for married girls.**
   - *Evidence: thin in these sources.* No evaluation of these services was found among the studies reviewed here. They are included because the duty of care to girls already married does not depend on a trial result, and because prevention-only lists leave them out.
   - Responses should follow the survivor-centred principles above: safety and consent first, no retaliation, and no penalising or criminalising the girl herself.

9. **Awareness campaigns on their own.**
   - *Evidence: weak.* No stand-alone awareness intervention among these sources shows a rigorous effect. Awareness appears only inside multicomponent packages, which perform poorly [10].
