# Pay structures that can close the gender pay gap

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. It looks past awareness training and negotiation workshops, and asks what a business could change in its pay and incentive system. Every cost figure is an estimate, in 2026 US dollars, for a mid-size firm of about 1,000 to 5,000 employees. Cost figures are not quotes from vendors or counsel. This is not legal advice.*

## The ethical question

Training and culture work can help, but they leave the machinery of pay in place. The question is what a business could change in that machinery — how offers are set, how bonuses are awarded, how jobs are priced, how promotions are decided, and how managers themselves are paid — so that fair pay is the normal result.

The aim is a system that produces fair pay even when people do not all bargain the same way, and even when individual managers have biases they do not see. The test for every idea below is the same: would this still move pay if nobody attended another workshop?

## Findings

The gender pay gap is not only a story about attitudes. A large share of it lives in ordinary pay rules: starting offers that can be bargained, wide and secret salary bands, subjective ratings, discretionary bonuses, uneven access to the work that leads to promotion, the way jobs held mostly by women are priced, and pay that rises disproportionately for long or irregular hours. Those are design choices. They can be redesigned.

The evidence on transparency laws is encouraging but incomplete. Public reporting has narrowed gaps in Denmark and the United Kingdom, often by slowing men’s pay growth rather than raising women’s. Austria’s quieter, internal-only reports did not work. Iceland’s equal-pay certification improved pay-setting practice, but researchers could not show a direct effect on the gap. Salary-history bans in the United States have raised women’s pay among people who change jobs. The lesson is not “publish a number and stop.” It is to change the rules that create the number, then hold leaders to the result.

### What pay systems can fix

These are mechanisms inside the pay system, not a complete account of every cause of the gap. Figures are from the sources listed at the end, checked in November 2026.

A useful starting point is the research review by Francine Blau and Lawrence Kahn in the *Journal of Economic Literature* (2017). After accounting for education, experience, occupation, industry and union status, a large unexplained gap still remained: 0.088 log points in 2010, or about 38 percent of the overall gap in their full decomposition. That residual is exactly where pay rules, discretion and biased valuation do their work.

#### Negotiation-based pay and lowball offers

When a company lets people bargain, it is not a neutral contest. In a large field experiment by Andreas Leibbrandt and John List, men were more likely to negotiate when a job ad left negotiability unstated. When the ad said wages were negotiable, that gap disappeared, and the mix of applicants changed. A 2012 University of Chicago summary of the study reported that 8 percent of women and 11 percent of men initiated talks when the wage was presented as a set figure, compared with 24 percent of women and 22 percent of men when it was described as negotiable.

Bargaining is not the only problem at hire. Shiya Wang and Adina Sterling studied more than 700,000 initial salary offers in the United States from 2017 to 2020 and found a 5.5 percent gender offer gap after detailed controls for job, employer, occupation, industry, location and human capital. Women were being offered less before anyone sat down to negotiate. A workshop that tells women to “ask for more” cannot fix an offer that was already set too low.

#### Opaque bands and secret pay

If salary ranges are secret, or so wide that a posted band is meaningless, people cannot tell whether they are underpaid. Interviews in UK finance, reported in the open-access book *Architectures of Inequality*, describe staff who could not find the minimum for their grade, and bonus decisions that felt hidden and depended on who their manager was. Opacity also lets past underpayment travel with a person from job to job, which is the problem salary-history bans are meant to stop.

#### Subjective ratings, bonuses and the work that gets assigned

Performance ratings look objective and often are not. Alan Benson, Danielle Li and Kelly Shue studied 29,809 management-track employees at a large North American retail firm. Women received higher performance ratings — they were 7.4 percent more likely to get the top score — but much lower “potential” ratings: they were 12 percent more likely to get the lowest potential score. Those potential scores explained about half of the promotion gap. The forecasts were wrong: women later outperformed men who had the same potential rating, and were less likely to leave, yet their later potential ratings stayed low.

Discretionary bonuses and overtime can widen the gap even when base pay looks closer. Australia’s Workplace Gender Equality Agency, in its 2024–25 employer report, found that the mid-point of employer gender pay gaps on discretionary pay — bonuses, overtime and other additional payments, after an adjustment that sets aside mandated superannuation — was 29.7 percent. That means half of employers paid men more than 29.7 percent more than women in those payments. The agency also notes that these payments are often decided by line managers and are rarely fully objective. A 2024 European Banking Authority review made a related point: even inside formal pay ranges, a margin of negotiation and discretionary bonus allocation can still produce differences that are not based on objective criteria.

#### Undervalued work and hour premiums

Two structural features of pay design also matter. First, jobs held mostly by women often pay less than the skill, effort, responsibility and conditions would suggest. Asaf Levanon, Paula England and Paul Allison, using US Census data from 1950 to 2000, found substantial evidence for this devaluation: as occupations became more female, their pay fell, even after accounting for stable features of the occupation. A pay system that simply “matches the market” will copy that undervaluation.

Second, some jobs pay a steep premium for long, irregular, or always-available hours, so earnings rise more than proportionally as hours rise. Claudia Goldin’s 2014 *American Economic Review* lecture called this pattern out as a central remaining source of the gap, and contrasted it with pharmacy, where workers are more substitutable and pay is closer to linear in hours. That premium is a design choice. A firm can reward coverage with scheduled, shareable shifts instead of paying the highest rate to whoever is always on call.

### What existing policy has done

Transparency helps when numbers are public, comparisons are possible, and someone has to act. It helps much less when reports stay inside the firm and nothing has to change.

- **About 2 percentage points** in Denmark after the 2006 reporting law: about a 13 percent reduction relative to the pre-law gap, mainly from slower growth in men’s pay.
- **1.6 percentage points** in the United Kingdom among firms with more than 250 staff: women’s hourly pay rose by 1.6 percentage points relative to men’s, about 19 percent of the gap in the study sample.
- **No clear effect** from Austria’s internal reports. Researchers could rule out a reduction larger than about 0.3 to 0.5 percentage points.
- **6.2 percent** higher pay for women who changed jobs after US salary-history bans, in the 2024 published study by Bessen, Denk and Meng.

In both Denmark and the UK, the gap narrowed mainly because men’s pay grew more slowly, not because women’s pay jumped. That still reduces inequality, but a good firm-level design should aim to lift underpaid people onto the right rate, not just compress everyone downward. Watch average pay, hiring, quits and output, not only the headline gap.

## Proposals

Each idea below can be adopted on its own. They are stronger as a set, which the next section puts together. These are system designs, not training programmes.

### 1. Formula pay with no negotiation

**How it works.** Every role has a published range and a written formula for where a person lands in that range, using a short list of job-related factors such as level, location, scarce skills, and relevant experience. Offers are generated by the formula. Hiring managers cannot bargain. Candidates are told the range before interview, and nobody is asked what they used to earn. Out-of-cycle raises and counteroffers are either banned or converted into a market adjustment that is applied to everyone in the same role, not just the person who asked. Exceptions go through a central compensation review and are reported each quarter.

**Why this beats socialization.** Negotiation workshops try to change who asks. This changes the rule so asking no longer sets the wage. It also blocks the 5.5 percent offer gap that appears before bargaining starts, and it stops an old, possibly unfair salary from becoming the new one.

- **How to measure it:** Share of hires and transfers paid exactly by the formula; residual gender gap in starting pay within role; spread of offers inside each band; number and gender mix of exceptions; whether candidates still try to bargain.
- **Gaming and side effects:** Managers may inflate titles, over-credit favoured experience, or route people into higher-priced roles. A tight formula can lose scarce talent in a hot market if it is not updated. If the formula is built on biased market surveys, it will freeze those biases in place. Review the factors and the market data at least once a year.
- **Legal considerations:** Posted ranges and a salary-history ban already match EU Directive 2023/970 and many US state rules. The formula should rest on job-related, gender-neutral criteria and be applied consistently. This is not legal advice; employment counsel should review the factors, especially experience definitions and any exception process.
- **Cost (estimate):** About $150,000 to $500,000 in the first year for job architecture, human-resources system changes, market data and legal review, plus roughly 0.5 to 1 full-time compensation role to keep the formula current. Ongoing vendor and survey costs may run $20,000 to $80,000 a year.

### 2. Equity-weighted leadership bonuses

**How it works.** A meaningful slice of annual bonuses for executives, and for managers who set pay, depends on independently audited pay-equity outcomes. A workable starting split, as an estimate, is 15 to 25 percent of variable pay. The scorecard should use a few hard measures: the unexplained pay gap inside comparable roles (base pay and total pay), placement inside the range, bonus-gap residuals, and promotion rates after accounting for actual performance. A modest gap inside a pre-set tolerance — many employers treat something like plus or minus 5 percent as a planning band, which is also the EU action threshold — can be treated as “on track.” Larger unexplained gaps reduce the bonus. An outside auditor, not the manager’s own team, signs the figures. Leaders should not be able to win this portion of pay by shrinking their team or by moving people out of the comparison group.

**Why this beats socialization.** Awareness training asks managers to be fair in their spare attention. This makes fairness part of how they themselves are paid. People manage what they are measured and rewarded for.

- **How to measure it:** Year-on-year change in residual gaps by unit; share of leaders hitting the tolerance band; audit adjustments versus self-reported figures; whether any unit improved its score by reclassifying jobs rather than fixing pay.
- **Gaming and side effects:** The Denmark and UK pattern is the main risk: managers hold down men’s raises instead of correcting underpayment. Other tactics include pushing people into non-comparable titles, delaying women’s promotions to “wait for a better mix,” or hiring to dress up a ratio. Use residual within-role gaps, not raw headcount mix, and add a rule that the women’s rate in a role may not be held flat as the only way to close the gap.
- **Legal considerations:** Do not write this as a quota that pays people more, or promotes them, because of sex. In the United States, the Equal Pay Act and Title VII require equal pay for substantially equal work and bar sex discrimination, including against men. The safer design rewards the closing of unexplained differences after job-related factors, plus fair process. In the EU, this sits well with the Directive’s joint pay assessment. Have counsel and, where relevant, works councils review the scorecard before it affects anyone’s bonus.
- **Cost (estimate):** If a bonus plan already exists, the design cost is mostly legal and data work: roughly $50,000 to $200,000 a year for an independent audit in a mid-size firm, plus internal analytics time. The cash cost of the incentive is a reallocation of bonus money, not new payroll, unless the firm adds a new pot.

### 3. A correction pool with use-it-or-explain

**How it works.** Each year the firm sets aside a ring-fenced budget to correct unexplained within-role pay differences. A reasonable planning range, as an estimate, is 0.5 to 2 percent of payroll for two or three years, sized to the residual gap the audit finds. Awards follow a written method: compare people in the same or equal-value roles, adjust those below the formula rate or below similarly qualified peers, and document the reason. Unused funds do not flow back into ordinary raises or discretionary bonuses. Leaders must either use the pool or explain, in writing, why the audit found no residual gap. A summary of how the pool was used — amounts, number of people, and remaining residual — goes to the executive team and, where law allows, to worker representatives.

**Why this beats socialization.** A one-off “equity review” is easy to defer when budgets are tight. A standing pool makes correction a normal budget line, like benefits or taxes, rather than a favour that has to be championed every year.

- **How to measure it:** Share of the pool used; residual within-role gap before and after awards; repeat corrections for the same people or managers; time from audit to payment.
- **Gaming and side effects:** Managers may wait for the pool instead of paying fairly at hire, which would turn the pool into a subsidy for sloppy offers. Block that by charging the hiring manager’s budget for first-year corrections. There is also a morale risk if adjustments are secret and peers cannot tell why someone received one. A clear method, applied to underpayment relative to the role, is easier to defend than informal “catch-up” deals.
- **Legal considerations:** Frame awards as bringing people to the rate for the work, not as a sex-based bonus. Apply the same method whenever anyone, of any sex, is below the formula or below comparable peers. Keep a record of the criteria. Privacy and, in union settings, bargaining rules may limit how much detail can be shared.
- **Cost (estimate):** The pool itself is the main cost: 0.5 percent of a $100 million payroll is $500,000 a year; 2 percent is $2 million. Administration is smaller, perhaps $25,000 to $75,000 a year once the audit process exists. This is a transfer to underpaid staff, not overhead.

### 4. Published role-value scores

**How it works.** Every job is scored on a small, published set of factors. EU Directive 2023/970 names a sound core: skills, effort, responsibility and working conditions, plus other job-relevant criteria, applied in an objective, gender-neutral way. Market pay is a check, not the only input. Roles with similar scores sit in the same band, even if one role is male-dominated and the other is not. The scoring guide is visible to staff. A joint panel — compensation, operations, and worker representatives where they exist — reviews edge cases and re-scores when jobs change. This is the firm-level version of “equal pay for work of equal value,” which is already the legal standard in the EU and the point of Iceland’s certification model.

**Why this beats socialization.** You cannot train your way out of a job ladder that prices one kind of work as “support” and another as “strategic” when the underlying demands are similar. Re-scoring the work changes the price list.

- **How to measure it:** Distribution of women and men across score bands; pay differences between equally scored roles; number of roles re-banded in the first two years; staff ability to look up their role score and band.
- **Gaming and side effects:** Influential departments will lobby for higher scores. If “responsibility” is coded as budget size or profit and loss, male-dominated line jobs will keep winning. Define responsibility and effort with examples from every function. A re-score can also raise costs in undervalued roles; budget for that, or the exercise will stall.
- **Legal considerations:** In the EU this is close to what the law already asks for. Elsewhere, a consistent, job-content method is still the most defensible way to compare work. Do not hide a market-only system behind a scorecard. If collective agreements set some rates, the architecture has to be bargained, not imposed.
- **Cost (estimate):** A first build, with outside job-evaluation help, may run $200,000 to $800,000 depending on the number of unique roles, then $50,000 to $150,000 a year to maintain. The larger cost is any upward re-pricing of undervalued work, which belongs in the correction-pool budget.

### 5. Closed-form bonuses and commissions

**How it works.** Variable pay is written as a formula: what counts, how it is measured, how credit is split, and how payouts are capped. Manager discretion over the size of a bonus is removed, or limited to a small, audited exception pot (for example, no more than 5 percent of the unit’s bonus pool). High-value accounts, territories, shifts and projects are assigned by a published rule — rotation, capacity, seniority in role, or a documented skill match — not by informal favour. Calibration meetings, if they remain, can only check that the formula was applied, not rewrite outcomes to fit a pre-set bell curve.

**Why this beats socialization.** The Workplace Gender Equality Agency’s 29.7 percent mid-point gap on discretionary pay is a reminder that the bonus layer is where a lot of inequality still sits. Teaching people to “put themselves forward for the big account” will not fix a system that hands out those accounts quietly.

- **How to measure it:** Gender gap in bonuses and commissions within role, before and after the formula; share of high-value assignments by gender; share of payouts coming from exceptions; complaint rate about credit.
- **Gaming and side effects:** People will aim at whatever the formula counts, and ignore what it does not. If revenue is easy to measure and client-development work is not, the formula will underpay the second kind of work. Build the measures carefully, and review them. Sales leaders may also try to keep a shadow discretion through account assignment; audit the assignment rule as hard as the payout rule.
- **Legal considerations:** Incentive plans are contracts. Changing them mid-cycle can create claims, and in some places they must be consulted or bargained. The criteria should be job-related and consistent. Keep the exception log. In financial services, regulators already expect gender-neutral remuneration policies; a formula makes that easier to show.
- **Cost (estimate):** Design and system work of about $75,000 to $250,000 in year one for a mid-size firm, more in complex sales organisations. Ongoing cost is analytics and audit time rather than a new pot of money, unless the firm is also topping up historically under-credited people through the correction pool.

### 6. Promotion on proven performance, not potential

**How it works.** Promotion shortlists are built from demonstrated performance and a small set of role requirements, not from a manager’s sense of “potential.” If a potential or “readiness” score is kept at all, it must be calibrated against later outcomes, and a pattern like the one Benson, Li and Shue found — higher performance, lower potential, then better later results than the potential score predicted — should automatically trigger a correction. Stretch assignments, acting roles, client introductions and training slots are logged and reviewed the same way pay is, because those are the inputs to promotion. Managers are scored on time-to-promotion and promotion rates after performance is taken into account, and on whether high-value assignments are distributed fairly. A person who meets the performance bar should not need a sponsor to be seen.

**Why this beats socialization.** Mentoring and “lean in” advice try to help people navigate a biased gate. This changes the gate. It directly targets a measured mechanism that accounted for about half of the promotion gap in the best large-firm evidence we have.

- **How to measure it:** Promotion rates and time-to-promotion by gender, after controlling for performance; residual in potential scores versus later performance; share of stretch assignments by gender; conversion rate from acting roles to permanent ones.
- **Gaming and side effects:** Managers may rename “potential” as “readiness,” “gravitas,” or “culture fit.” Ban unmeasured criteria, or define them in behavioural terms that can be audited. A pure performance rule can also favour people already in high-visibility seats; that is why assignment logs have to sit beside the promotion rule. Do not turn this into a promotion quota, which is legally and practically shakier than a fair process.
- **Legal considerations:** Promotion is a term and condition of employment. A consistent, job-related process is the defensible path under US, UK and EU discrimination law. Outcome quotas are more legally exposed, especially in the United States. Retain records of criteria, shortlists and decisions.
- **Cost (estimate):** Mostly process and data, not cash: perhaps $50,000 to $150,000 to rebuild the promotion cycle and dashboards, plus manager time. The larger cost is cultural resistance from managers who lose informal discretion.

### 7. Linear hours and shared coverage

**How it works.** Where pay currently rises more than proportionally with long or irregular hours, the firm redesigns the work so coverage is a scheduled, shareable duty. Premiums attach to the shift, the on-call rota, or the team that provides coverage, not to a culture of being always available. Output or client outcomes, where they can be measured fairly, carry more weight than face-time. Staffing is set so one person’s absence can be covered without a heroic overtime spike. This follows Goldin’s core point: the nonlinear hour premium is a property of how the job is organised, and some occupations have already shown that more substitutable work can support more linear pay.

**Why this beats socialization.** Telling people to set boundaries does not change a pay scale that rewards the person who never logs off. Changing the pay scale, and the staffing model underneath it, does.

- **How to measure it:** Elasticity of earnings with respect to hours in the redesigned roles (the target is close to one, not well above it); gender gaps in hours, overtime and total pay within role; coverage failures and client-outcome measures, so quality is not quietly traded away.
- **Gaming and side effects:** If the premium is simply cut and staff are not added, service quality falls and informal after-hours work continues unpaid. Budget the coverage. In some roles, true emergencies still need rapid response; pay those events at a clear rate and review who receives them. Do not use this redesign as a way to cut total pay.
- **Legal considerations:** Overtime, working-time and minimum-wage rules still apply. In some jurisdictions, changing schedules or premiums is a mandatory bargaining subject. Be careful that a new “coverage shift” is not a back-door cut for one group of jobs.
- **Cost (estimate):** This is the most operationally expensive idea. A pilot in one function might cost $100,000 to $400,000 in redesign and systems, plus ongoing staffing if headcount has to rise to make coverage shareable. Against that, firms should count overtime bills, quits driven by overwork, and errors from very long hours — all of which are often under-measured.

### 8. Certification with buyer pressure

**How it works.** The firm adopts an Iceland-style equal-pay management system: a documented, auditable method for equal pay for the same work and for work of equal value, checked by an independent body on a set cycle (every three years is the Icelandic rhythm). That is the internal half. The external half is incentive from outside the firm. A government can offer a modest tax credit or a plus-factor in public procurement for certified employers. A large private buyer can do the same in its supplier scorecards: certified pay systems earn points, just as safety or environmental standards already do. A firm that is not waiting for legislation can still require certification from its own significant suppliers, and publish its own certificate.

**Why this beats socialization.** Certification, done properly, forces a system, an audit trail and a recurring test. Buyer pressure then gives that certificate a cash value. Iceland’s experience is a warning as well as a model: a badge with loose methods may not move the gap, and may even excuse it. The standard has to be tight, and it has to sit on top of the structures above, not in place of them.

- **How to measure it:** Certification status and audit findings; residual gaps in certified versus uncertified units or suppliers; share of procurement spend going to certified firms; whether certified firms actually close gaps, not just pass the audit.
- **Gaming and side effects:** The main risk, named by Icelandic researchers, is legitimacy: a firm can pass the audit and treat the remaining gap as settled. Another is a paper process that never touches actual rates. Require public residual-gap metrics alongside the certificate. Small suppliers may find audit costs heavy; a buyer programme should offer a lighter, supported path for them rather than shutting them out.
- **Legal considerations:** Public tax or procurement incentives need legislative or regulatory authority, and must fit trade and equal-treatment rules. Private buyers have more room in supplier scorecards, but should not impose conditions that conflict with local labour or competition law. Certification reports will contain personal pay data and need a privacy design, especially under the GDPR.
- **Cost (estimate):** For a mid-size firm, an external equal-pay certification cycle might cost $15,000 to $80,000 in auditor fees, plus substantial internal time in the first round. A procurement plus-factor costs the buyer little in cash, but staff time to verify certificates. A tax credit’s cost sits with the government, not the firm.

## A combined model and pilot

The recommended model is a stack, not a single trick. Call it Equity by Design. The base is a published job architecture and role-value scores. On that base sit formula offers, posted ranges, a salary-history ban, and no individual bargaining. Variable pay and high-value assignments follow written formulas. An annual correction pool clears what the audit still finds. A slice of leadership bonuses depends on those audited results. In roles where long or irregular hours currently carry a steep extra premium, coverage is redesigned so pay is closer to linear. Certification, and a supplier preference once the firm has passed its own audit, lock the system in place and extend it outward.

The stack, in order:

1. **Job architecture** — published role-value scores and bands.
2. **Formula pay** — posted ranges, no bargaining, no salary history.
3. **Formula bonuses** — closed-form incentives and assignment rules.
4. **Correction pool** — use-it-or-explain residual adjustments.
5. **Leadership scorecards** — a share of bonus tied to the audit.
6. **Linear hours where needed** — shared coverage instead of always-on premiums.
7. **Certification and buyers** — independent audit, then supplier pressure.

Do not roll this out everywhere on day one. Pilot it. The point of the pilot is to learn whether the stack closes residual gaps without harming output, hiring or lawful fairness to everyone in the system.

### Pilot plan (about 12 to 18 months)

1. **Months 0 to 2 — Diagnose.** Run an independent pay audit on base pay, bonuses and total pay, within role and for work of equal value. Measure range placement, promotion rates against performance, assignment of high-value work, offer variance, and the relationship between hours and earnings in the relevant jobs. Pre-register the success metrics so nobody moves the goalposts later.
2. **Months 2 to 4 — Design and legal review.** Build the role-value scores, the offer and bonus formulas, the correction-pool rules, and the leadership scorecard. Review them with counsel, and with worker representatives or unions where they exist. Choose two (or more) business units to treat, and two similar units to keep as a comparison. They should be close in size, role mix and starting gap.
3. **Months 4 to 16 — Run the pilot.** Turn on formula offers, formula bonuses, the correction pool and the manager scorecard in the treated units. If a treated unit has a steep hour premium, start the hours redesign there too. Do not quietly introduce pieces of the new system in the comparison units. Publish the rules inside the treated units so staff can see how pay is set.
4. **Months 16 to 18 — Evaluate, then decide.** Compare treated and comparison units on the metrics below. If residual gaps fall and output, hiring and quits are healthy, scale. If gaps fall only because pay was compressed downward, or if quality dropped, fix the design before any wider rollout. Certification comes after the system has actually worked, not as a first step.

### Pilot metrics

Track at least these, all split by sex and by role or equal-value group, in treated and comparison units:

- The residual (unexplained) gap in base pay and in total pay, within comparable roles
- Where people sit inside the pay range (a simple compa-ratio: pay divided by the midpoint for the role)
- The bonus and commission gap within role, and the share of high-value assignments
- Promotion rates and time-to-promotion, after accounting for actual performance ratings
- Share of offers generated by the formula, and the number of bargaining exceptions
- In redesigned roles, how steeply earnings rise with hours, plus coverage and quality measures
- Hiring, quits and regretted losses, so a narrowing gap is not just a change in who works there
- Output or revenue per person, so the Denmark productivity warning is watched in real time
- A short staff survey on whether pay rules feel understandable and consistently applied
- Grievances and legal claims, which should be reviewed with counsel

## Risks

The main risks are not theoretical. Managers can hold down men’s raises instead of lifting underpaid people to the right rate, which is how gaps narrowed in Denmark and the UK. They can inflate titles, reclassify jobs, or push people out of comparison groups to dress up a scorecard. A tight formula can freeze biased market data in place, or lose scarce talent if it is not updated. A correction pool can become a subsidy for sloppy offers if hiring managers are not charged for first-year fixes. Bonus formulas will underpay work they fail to count. Promotion rules can be relabelled as “readiness” or “culture fit.” Cutting hour premiums without adding staff will push informal after-hours work underground. A loose equal-pay certificate can excuse a remaining gap instead of closing it.

A poor result is a prettier headline gap achieved by holding pay down, relabeling jobs, or shrinking one group’s opportunities. The audit should be able to tell the difference. Cost figures in this study are estimates, and the legal notes are orientation, not advice. A firm should have employment counsel, and where relevant its worker representatives, in the room before anyone’s pay changes.

## Recommendation

The strongest package is Equity by Design: a published job architecture, formula pay with no individual bargaining, formula bonuses, an annual pot to correct leftover gaps, and a slice of leadership pay that depends on an independent audit. In roles with a steep hour premium, redesign coverage so pay is closer to linear in hours. Seek certification, and then a supplier preference, only after the system has actually worked.

A firm can pilot that stack in a few units, compare them with similar units that keep the old system, and scale only what the metrics support. A good result is a clear drop in the residual within-role gap, more even placement inside pay ranges, fairer bonuses and promotions, and stable or better output — achieved by lifting underpaid people to the right rate, not by squeezing pay together.
