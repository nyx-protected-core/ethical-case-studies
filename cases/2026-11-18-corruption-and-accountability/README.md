# Corruption and accountability worldwide: who pays, what the numbers really say, and what has worked

*An ethics research case study. Research and writing only. Every DOI below was checked against the Crossref registry, and every web link was opened, on 18 November 2026. Where a figure is weak or contested, the note says so. The companion case on global tax cooperation and illicit financial flows covers profit shifting, the FATF standards and the 2022 EU court ruling on public beneficial-ownership registers; this case refers to it rather than repeating it.*

---

## The ethical question

Corruption is usually defined as the abuse of entrusted public power for private gain. It is often discussed as a problem of rich elites and big numbers. The evidence in this note points somewhere more uncomfortable: the people who lose most are those who depend on public services they cannot replace with private ones, such as a village school, a clinic, a subsidised food ration or a public works job. The ethical question is therefore not only "how do we punish the corrupt?" but "how do we make sure money meant for the poor reaches them, in a way that does not hand governments a new weapon against their critics, and that puts real duties on the rich financial centres where stolen money ends up?"

Who it hurts, in measured terms:

- **Schools.** A survey of Ugandan primary schools found that in 1991–1995 schools received on average only 13 percent of a central government capitation grant. Most schools received nothing, the bulk was captured by local officials and politicians, and schools in better-off communities claimed a larger share, so actual spending was regressive even though the budget was not [1].
- **Food for the poor.** In a large Indonesian programme that distributed subsidised rice to poor households, comparing official distribution records with household surveys showed that at least 18 percent of the rice disappeared on average. The author estimates the welfare losses may have been large enough to offset the gains the programme was meant to bring [2].
- **Public works and welfare payments.** In India's rural employment scheme, an experiment that replaced advance payments with "just-in-time" payments triggered by electronic invoices cut programme spending by 24 percent while employment rose slightly, there were fewer fake households in the database, and officials' personal wealth fell by 10 percent. The money that disappeared had been leakage, not wages that workers needed [3].
- **Health.** A cross-country study of 178 countries found that perceived corruption was significantly associated with under-five mortality after adjusting for sanitation, health spending and other factors, and the authors "roughly hypothesized" that more than 140,000 child deaths a year could be indirectly attributed to corruption [4]. This is a correlation from a single cross-sectional regression, not a causal estimate; a later review graded it as close to credible but not credible [5].
- **Inequality.** Cross-country evidence associates higher corruption with higher income inequality (about 11 Gini points for a one-standard-deviation rise in corruption) and slower income growth for the poor [6]. Like all cross-country regressions on perception indices, this is suggestive rather than proof.

## Scale (real figures)

Global corruption numbers are widely quoted and poorly founded. A 2021 review for the U4 Anti-Corruption Resource Centre by Wathne and Stephenson traced ten of the most cited global corruption statistics to their origins. None was rated credible, only two came close (an estimate of illicit financial outflows and the child-mortality estimate above), six were "problematic" and four "appear to be entirely unfounded" [5]. Their main findings on the headline figures:

- **"Corruption costs US$2.6 trillion, or 5 percent of global GDP, each year."** Rated *unfounded*. The review found no traceable analysis behind it and notes that 5 percent of global GDP has not equalled US$2.6 trillion since 2006. It concludes that no organisation should cite it [5].
- **"US$2.6 trillion in public funds is stolen or embezzled each year."** Also *unfounded*. Its most prominent appearance was a 2018 speech by the UN Secretary-General; the review's best guess is that the cost-of-corruption figure was misread as an amount stolen [5].
- **"US$1 trillion in bribes is paid every year."** Rated *problematic*. It traces to World Bank Institute work from the early 2000s that produced a range of US$604 billion to US$1.76 trillion, with US$1 trillion as an approximate midpoint, and a promised methods paper was never published. The most the review would support is that "some estimates in the early 2000s found suggestive evidence" of bribes in that range [5]. An IMF staff discussion note cites an unpublished 2015 update of about US$1.5–2 trillion (roughly 2 percent of world GDP) [7]; the same concerns apply to it [5].
- **Procurement.** The claim that 10–25 percent of procurement spending is lost to corruption was rated *problematic*, being based on perceptions and unexplained extrapolations [5].

This case therefore uses no global dollar total. The better evidence is local and measured: missing rice, missing school grants, missing road materials, and cash that moves offshore when aid arrives. On the last point, Andersen, Johannesen and Rijkers find that aid disbursements to highly aid-dependent countries coincide with sharp increases in deposits in offshore financial centres known for bank secrecy, but not in other financial centres, implying a leakage rate of around 7.5 percent at the sample mean that rises with aid dependence [8].

**Asset recovery is small relative to any estimate of theft.** A joint World Bank–UNODC (StAR) and OECD survey found that OECD members returned US$423.5 million in stolen assets between 2006 and June 2012, against US$2.623 billion frozen, and set this against the StAR estimate of US$20–40 billion stolen each year from developing and transition countries [9]. That US$20–40 billion figure is itself an approximation from 2007 and should be treated as an order of magnitude.

## Root causes

- **Discretion without information.** Theft is easiest where officials control money that citizens cannot see. The Ugandan and Indonesian results above both came from comparing what the centre sent with what arrived [1][2]. A review of the newer micro-evidence finds robust evidence that corruption responds to standard economic incentives, but also that the effects of anti-corruption policies often weaken as officials find other ways to pursue rents [11]. Svensson's survey of the field asks, among other things, why so few recent attempts to fight corruption have succeeded [10].
- **Collective action, not just bad individuals.** Interviews in Kenya and Uganda led Persson, Rothstein and Teorell to argue that where corruption is systemic it behaves like a collective action problem: if everyone expects others to be corrupt, no one gains by being honest, and reforms that assume an honest superior who will punish dishonest subordinates break down [12]. Mungiu-Pippidi asks how societies reach the point where integrity becomes the norm, and notes that although few states now lack an anti-corruption agency or ombudsman, no statistical evidence shows that these bodies by themselves induce progress [13].
- **Weak electoral and judicial accountability.** Using Brazil's random municipal audits, Ferraz and Finan find that mayors who can stand for re-election misappropriate 27 percent fewer resources than mayors in their final term, with larger effects where access to information is lower and judicial punishment less likely [14].
- **Supply from abroad.** Bribes need payers and stolen money needs somewhere to go. In a global field experiment with company formation services, providers in tax havens were nearly three times more likely than providers in OECD countries to comply fully with identity rules (36.5 percent against 13.3 percent; 9.6 percent in developing countries) [15][16]. A World Bank–UNODC review of about 150 grand corruption cases found that corporate vehicles were routinely used to hide the owners of illicit assets, and that three-quarters of the misused vehicles were ordinary companies [17]. Responsibility therefore sits with the large financial and incorporation centres, not only with the countries where the theft happens.

## What has worked

The strongest evidence comes from Brazil, Indonesia, Uganda and India, and from institutions built in Asia, Africa and the Caucasus. That is where the most important lessons have been learned.

**Top-down audits (Indonesia).** In a randomised experiment in more than 600 Indonesian village road projects, raising the probability of a government audit from 4 percent to 100 percent reduced "missing expenditures" (the gap between official costs and an independent engineers' estimate) by eight percentage points. Increasing grassroots participation in monitoring had little average impact, except where free-riding and elite capture were limited [18].

**Random audits with public results (Brazil).** Since 2003 Brazil's federal government has selected municipalities at random to audit their use of federal transfers and published the results. Releasing audit findings before the 2004 elections significantly hurt incumbents' electoral performance, more so where local radio spread the news [19]. Being audited in the past reduced future corruption by about 8 percent and raised the likelihood of later legal action by about 20 percent, with most of the effect coming from higher expected legal and other non-electoral costs [20].

**Public expenditure tracking and publication (Uganda).** After the tracking survey revealed the capture of school grants, information on monthly grant transfers was published in newspapers so that schools and parents could monitor local officials. Reinikka and Svensson find that the newspaper campaign reduced capture and that this raised enrolment and learning [21]. Hubbard cautions that the drop in leakage was real but that attributing it solely to the publication of grant data ignores the wider funding and reforms in which the campaign was embedded [22]. Both readings support the same conclusion: information works best when it reaches people who can act on it, inside a broader reform.

**Community monitoring of health services (Uganda).** A randomised experiment in which local NGOs helped communities monitor their primary health providers led, a year later, to more effort by health workers, large increases in use of services, reduced child mortality and higher child weight [23]. This contrasts with the weak effect of community monitoring of roads in Indonesia [18], which suggests that local monitoring works where the service is visible to users and the community can act together.

**Payment systems that cut out intermediaries (India).** Biometric "Smartcard" payments in Andhra Pradesh, rolled out at random across 157 subdistricts and 19 million people, gave employment-scheme and pension beneficiaries faster, more predictable and less corrupt payments, reduced leakage, and were cost-effective [24]. Electronic invoicing in the national employment scheme cut leakage, but payment delays increased, a real cost to poor workers [3].

**E-procurement and open contracting.** Across India and Indonesia, e-procurement did not reduce prices but improved quality: better roads in India, fewer delays in Indonesia, and more winners from outside the region [25]. Ukraine's ProZorro system moved all public procurement online from 2016, made auctions the default and published contract data in the Open Contracting Data Standard. The most careful evaluation available, a Center for Global Development working paper, finds among already-competitive large contracts "some evidence" of more bidders and higher savings and "strong evidence" of reduced procurement times, alongside a large rise in the share of procurement that was competitive at all [26]. It is a working paper using before-and-after comparisons, and its "savings" are measured against estimated prices that buyers set themselves, so the size of the effect is uncertain. This case found no peer-reviewed causal estimate of ProZorro's effect on corruption as such.

**Rebuilding a police force (Georgia).** Before reform, a 2000 survey estimated that motorists stopped by Georgia's traffic police were asked for bribes in 7 of 10 contacts. In 2004 the government dismissed the traffic police (about 16,000 officers) and created a new patrol police with competitive hiring and higher pay; a World Bank account reports that a 2010 survey found only 1 percent of the population reported paying a bribe to road police [27]. Light describes this as the transformation of a criminalised force into the most service-oriented in the post-Soviet region, but also notes that it relied on a revolutionary moment and that the reformed police became closely associated with the governing leadership of the day [28]. The Transparency International Helpdesk reached similar conclusions about Georgia and Rwanda: progress followed regime change or post-conflict reconstruction, strong political will and broad public demand [29].

**Independent anti-corruption commissions (Hong Kong and Botswana).** Hong Kong's Independent Commission Against Corruption was set up in February 1974, separate from the police, with a three-pronged approach of law enforcement, prevention and community education [30]. Manion's comparison of Hong Kong and mainland China credits the Commission's combination of enforcement, public education and institutional redesign, and its independence from the police, with signalling a credible commitment that changed public expectations [31]. Mungiu-Pippidi's caution that agencies alone do not induce progress [13] is the counterweight. Botswana set up its Directorate on Corruption and Economic Crime in August 1994 after a series of public scandals; an early evaluation credits it with a public campaign against corruption and a high conviction rate while noting many operating problems [32]. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson attribute Botswana's long-run success more broadly to secure, broadly accessible property rights and effective constraints on elites, rooted partly in pre-colonial institutions [33]. The "Hong Kong model" remains contested: its defenders argue donors dismissed specialised agencies without good evidence, while critics argue the context cannot be exported and that such a powerful body can become an instrument of political oppression [34].

**State capacity with concentrated power (Rwanda).** Rwanda is widely perceived as having reduced corruption by strengthening laws and institutions, public financial management and the civil service, and by prosecuting officials at all levels [29]. Booth and Golooba-Mutebi show that this has coexisted with the governing party's large business holdings, and describe Rwanda as a "developmental patrimonial" state in which economic rents are centrally managed rather than eliminated [35]. Rwanda's case shows that falling petty corruption and strong political accountability are not the same thing.

**What a systematic review says about health-sector corruption.** A Cochrane review found no studies that met its primary inclusion criteria and only nine for a secondary analysis. It concluded that there is "a paucity of evidence", with improved detection and punishment, especially when coordinated by an independent agency, among the more promising approaches [36]. Much of the anti-corruption toolkit remains untested.

## Global and regional cooperation

**UNCAC.** The UN Convention against Corruption was adopted in October 2003, entered into force in December 2005 and had 193 parties as of 22 September 2026 [37]. Chapter V makes the return of stolen assets "a fundamental principle" of the Convention (Article 51) [38]. Its Implementation Review Mechanism is a peer review: by 1 March 2026, 181 of 190 executive summaries had been adopted for the first cycle and 131 of 190 for the second [39]. At its eleventh session the Conference of the States Parties launched a second phase, with reviews to begin in 2027 [39]. Civil society groups have pressed for more transparency and participation in those reviews [40]. The mechanism is universal and treats every party as both reviewer and reviewed, which gives Global South states equal standing, but it does not sanction.

**The African Union Convention.** The African Union Convention on Preventing and Combating Corruption was adopted in July 2003 and entered into force in August 2006 [41]. In February 2020 the AU Assembly unanimously adopted a Common African Position on Asset Recovery, proposed by Nigeria, as the continent's framework for negotiating the return of assets held in foreign jurisdictions [42]. African states are therefore not only recipients of anti-corruption advice; they have a common negotiating position addressed to the jurisdictions that hold the money.

**The OECD Anti-Bribery Convention and its gaps.** The Convention obliges its parties to criminalise and enforce against bribery of foreign public officials. It has 46 parties: all 38 OECD members plus Argentina, Brazil, Bulgaria, Croatia, Peru, Romania, the Russian Federation (whose participation has been suspended since April 2022 except for monitoring) and South Africa [43]. Its peer review has measurable effects: using a survey method designed to reduce reporting bias, Jensen and Malesky find that after Phase 3 monitoring began in 2010, multinationals from signatory countries reduced their actual bribery relative to competitors from non-signatories [44]. Enforcement is thin, however. Transparency International's 2022 assessment of 47 leading exporters (an advocacy report, not peer-reviewed) classed only the United States and Switzerland, with 11.8 percent of world exports, as active enforcers, down from seven countries with 27 percent in 2018. Major non-Convention exporters, including China and India, were in the lowest category, and India still had no law criminalising foreign bribery [45]. Countries that export the most carry the largest share of the duty to prosecute the bribe-payers.

**Asset recovery and beneficial ownership.** The StAR figures above show how little is returned [9]. Beneficial-ownership registers aim to make the people behind companies visible. The companion tax-cooperation case discusses the FATF standards and the 2022 EU court ruling that unrestricted public access to such registers was disproportionate [46]. The shell-company evidence [15][16] shows that the weak link is often in OECD countries, not only in small offshore centres.

## Ethical tensions

**Anti-corruption as a political weapon.** Anti-corruption drives can be used to remove rivals. A study of provincial leadership turnover in China finds that intra-elite power competition raised investigations of senior officials by up to 20 percent and concludes that such drives "tend to be selective, arbitrary, and factionally biased" [47]. The same authors note that partisan competition politicises corruption in democracies too [47]. Manion, by contrast, argues that China's campaign since 2012 also changed bureaucratic incentives in ways that point toward institutionalisation [48]. Both can be true. The test is whether investigations follow transparent, evenly applied rules and whether the accused get fair trials, wherever in the world the campaign happens. The worry that a strong commission can become an instrument of political oppression is one of the standard objections to the Hong Kong model [34], and the Georgian police reform showed how quickly a reformed agency can become tied to one government [28].

**Donor conditionality.** Donors often make aid conditional on anti-corruption reforms. Svensson argues that ex ante conditionality has a poor record and proposes instead pooling aid for a group of countries and allocating it afterwards according to relative performance [49]. Conditions set from outside can also cut services to the poor when governments fail them, punishing citizens for their rulers' conduct, and they can sit uneasily with the fact that the money often leaves through financial centres in donor countries [8][15]. Aid leakage into offshore accounts [8] argues for donors to put their own banking systems in order alongside any conditions they set.

**Transparency versus privacy and surveillance.** Several of the tools that worked rely on more data: biometric payments [24], e-invoicing [3], public audit reports [19], beneficial-ownership registers [46]. The same systems can exclude people whose fingerprints fail or whose payments are delayed [3], expose ordinary people's data, or support state surveillance. The proportionality line drawn by the EU court for ownership registers [46] is a useful general rule: give access to those with a legitimate need (investigators, journalists, civil society), protect individuals' personal data, and keep information on public money (budgets, contracts, audit results) fully public.

**Measurement honesty.** Advocates have an incentive to quote large numbers. Repeating unfounded figures undermines the credibility of the cause [5]. An ethical approach states the uncertainty, prefers measured local leakage to invented global totals, and admits where interventions failed, such as community monitoring of roads in Indonesia [18].

## Original proposals (this study's own)

*The following are proposals of this study, not findings of the sources. They draw on the evidence above, but none has been evaluated as a package.*

1. **Random audits with published results as a default for intergovernmental transfers.** Brazil's design, with random selection, public reports and follow-up by prosecutors, has the strongest causal evidence [19][20]. Countries of any income level could adopt it for transfers to local governments, and regional bodies such as the African Union could support peer exchange between national audit institutions.
2. **"Follow the transfer" public dashboards.** Publish what each school, clinic and ration shop is due, and when it is sent, in a form that people there can check, through notices and radio as well as online, following the Ugandan and Brazilian lessons that information must reach those who can act on it [19][21][22].
3. **Leakage-cutting payments with a fair-delay guarantee.** Where digital or biometric payments are introduced, set a maximum payment delay and a non-digital fallback for those who fail authentication, so that the poor do not bear the cost of fighting leakage [3][24].
4. **Open contracting with independent evaluation built in.** Countries adopting e-procurement should publish contract data in an open standard and fund independent before-and-after evaluations with a comparison group, so that future studies can do better than the evidence on ProZorro [25][26].
5. **Financial-centre accountability.** Major financial and incorporation centres, whether in Europe, North America, the Gulf or Asia, should publish annual data on assets frozen and returned, make ID checks by company formation providers an enforced legal duty, and treat recovered assets under a negotiated framework such as the Common African Position on Asset Recovery [9][15][42]. Exporting countries that are not party to the OECD Convention should criminalise foreign bribery, as UNCAC already requires [45].
6. **Due-process safeguards for anti-corruption agencies.** Any agency with strong powers should publish case-selection criteria and statistics by political affiliation where lawful, be overseen by a body that includes opposition and civil society members, and face independent courts, so that it cannot easily become a tool against rivals [34][47].
7. **Performance-based pooled aid rather than unilateral conditions.** Donors could test Svensson's proposal of pooling aid and allocating it on measured service delivery, using audit-based measures of leakage rather than perception indices [49].
8. **Statistics discipline.** International organisations and advocates should stop citing the US$2.6 trillion and 5 percent of GDP figures and should label every global estimate with its source, year and range [5].

## Risks

- **Displacement.** Corruption can move to channels that are not audited or digitised. The effects of anti-corruption policies often weaken as officials find other strategies [11], and leakage pushed out of one stage of a programme may reappear elsewhere.
- **Exclusion.** Biometric and digital systems can deny benefits to legitimate recipients and add delays [3][24].
- **Political capture of the reforms.** Strong agencies and audits can be used selectively [34][47], and reformed bodies can become associated with one government [28].
- **Context dependence.** Hong Kong, Georgia, Botswana and Rwanda each reformed at a particular political moment [28][29][32][33]. Copying their institutions without the conditions that supported them may fail, as the collective-action view predicts [12].
- **Weak evidence.** Many tools, including ProZorro and most health-sector measures, lack rigorous causal evaluation [26][36]. Global cost figures are unreliable [5].
- **Sovereignty and fairness.** Conditions set by donors or financial centres can be seen as interference, especially when those same centres hold stolen assets [8][9].

## Recommendation

Treat corruption first as a problem of public money failing to reach the poor, and judge reforms by whether services improve. The best-evidenced tools are random audits with published results [18][19][20], tracking and publishing transfers to front-line services [1][21], community monitoring where users can see and act on the service [23], and payment and procurement systems that cut out intermediaries, with protections against exclusion and delay [3][24][25]. These are low-cost and have been proven in Brazil, Indonesia, Uganda and India. Global cooperation should rest on UNCAC's universal peer review and the African Union's common positions, with Global South states as equal partners, and should place the heaviest duties on the exporting countries that fail to prosecute foreign bribery and the financial centres that hold stolen assets [37][41][42][45]. Every anti-corruption power should come with due-process safeguards, and every public figure on the cost of corruption should be one that can be traced to its source.
