3. **IPC (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification) Global Partners.** "Famine facts" / IPC Famine Fact Sheet (updated 2025–26). https://www.ipcinfo.org/famine-facts/ ; archived https://web.archive.org/web/20250822043305/https://www.ipcinfo.org/famine-facts/. *Note:* Famine (IPC Phase 5) is an area classification that requires three conditions:
   - at least 20% of households face an extreme lack of food;
   - at least 30% of under-fives have acute malnutrition by weight-for-height and/or oedema, or about 15% by MUAC;
   - more than 2 non-trauma adult deaths per 10,000 per day, or 4 or more under-five deaths per 10,000 per day.

   The fact sheet states that these definitions "refer strictly to the observed conditions and do not imply causality, intent, or alignment with any legal definitions."

   Phase 5 is also used at the household level, where it is called "Catastrophe". Households can be in Phase 5 Catastrophe without the area being classified as Famine. Famine is applied only at the area level, when widespread starvation, widespread acute malnutrition and excess deaths occur together.

   A classification is "with solid evidence" when all three conditions are clearly met, or "with reasonable evidence" when two are met and the third is judged likely. "Famine Likely" and "Risk of Famine" are further, distinct labels. All Famine classifications go to the Famine Review Committee.

   In the last 15 years Famine was confirmed on six occasions, "overwhelmingly driven by armed conflict and restrictions on humanitarian access":
   - Somalia, July 2011, in parts of Southern Somalia, "due to conflict and drought";
   - South Sudan, February 2017, in parts of Unity State, after "three years of civil war";
   - South Sudan, November 2020, "Famine Likely" in four payams of Pibor county, driven by violence and flooding together with sharp price rises;
   - Sudan, December 2024, in at least five areas, "driven by the devastating conflict" and "poor humanitarian access";
   - Sudan, September 2025, Famine "with reasonable evidence" in El Fasher (North Darfur) and Kadugli (South Kordofan), "likely to persist through January 2026", in a civil war that began in April 2023;
   - Gaza Strip, August 2025, Famine in Gaza Governorate, "driven by relentless conflict", later updated (see [23]).

   The fact sheet's first recommended actions when Famine is detected are "Cessation of Conflicts" and "Humanitarian Access", then a multi-sectoral response. *Access:* the live site returns 403 to some automated requests but was opened and read; the archived copy is also cited. [REP]

7. **Pople A, Premand P, Dercon S, Vinez M, Brunelin S, 2025.** "The Earlier the Better? Cash Transfers for Drought Response in Niger." World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11138. DOI 10.1596/1813-9450-11138. *Note:* This is an RCT of drought response with satellite-based triggers.

## KEY FINDINGS

1. **Early-warning systems are layered.** They are interdependent and fragile.
   - IPC sets the common severity scale and the formal famine thresholds [3].
   - FEWS NET produces "IPC-compatible" analysis and 8-month projections [1].
   - WFP's HungerMap LIVE offers near-real-time monitoring and model-based nowcasts for more than 50 countries [4].

   In 2025 the main US-funded node went dark for about six months [1, 2]:
   - The fews.net archive record shows the site unavailable from late January 2025 [1].
   - FEWS NET came back in late June 2025 with an interim global update [1].
   - Coverage was restricted to 22 countries for the rest of 2025 [2].
   - Coverage was not fully restored, to 51 countries, until 2026 [2].

   The removal itself is documented in the US government's own report to Congress [2] and in contemporaneous reporting [1].

2. **Famine is a narrow technical classification, and it is not the same as household Phase 5.**
   - An area Famine classification requires simultaneous extreme food gaps, acute malnutrition and mortality thresholds, plus independent review [3]. The IPC states that these definitions describe observed conditions and "do not imply causality, intent, or alignment with any legal definitions" [3].
   - Phase 5 at the household level is called "Catastrophe". People can be in Catastrophe without an area being classified as Famine [3]. The *Global Report on Food Crises 2026* counted 1.4 million people in Catastrophe across six countries and territories in 2025 [25].
   - Famine has been confirmed only six times in 15 years. The IPC says these famines "were overwhelmingly driven by armed conflict and restrictions on humanitarian access" [3]. Its case notes also name secondary drivers: drought alongside conflict in Somalia in 2011, and flooding with sharp price rises alongside violence in South Sudan in 2020 (classified as "Famine Likely") [3].
   - The dates matter. Sudan's 2025 classification (El Fasher and Kadugli, "with reasonable evidence") was reached in September 2025 and was "likely to persist through January 2026" [3]. Gaza's Famine classification was dated August 2025 [3]. The IPC's 19 December 2025 update found that, after access improved following the 10 October 2025 ceasefire, no area of the Gaza Strip was classified in Famine; more than 100,000 people remained in Catastrophe and the territory was in Emergency (Phase 4) [23]. Israel rejected that assessment (see the political-causes section and [24]).
   - The US government's report notes that famine "often is only confirmed after people are already dying", which is why early warning matters [2].

2a. **Headline figures on need should be read with their dates and coverage.**
   - The *Global Report on Food Crises 2026* (released April 2026) counted 266 million people in 47 countries and territories in high acute food insecurity in 2025, 22.9% of the population analysed. The fall from 296 million in 2024 "mainly reflects a reduction in the number of countries covered" [25].
   - WFP's *2026 Global Outlook* (November 2025) estimated that 318 million people would face crisis-level hunger or worse in 2026 in countries where WFP operates and data are available. WFP aimed to reach 110 million people in 2026 at an estimated cost of US$13 billion [26]. These figures are not interchangeable with the GRFC count.
   - The US Department of State's June 2026 report to Congress states that, during calendar year 2025, FEWS NET's restored 22-country analysis projected that "between 125 and 135 million people were projected to need food assistance throughout the year" in that subset of countries [2].

3. **Anticipatory action has the best causal evidence of any intervention reviewed here, though the evidence is still limited.**
   - Bangladesh floods (two QE studies): cash before the flood peak reduced going without food and costly borrowing, with some benefits still present at three months [5, 6].
   - Niger drought (one RCT): early transfers beat traditional lean-season transfers in the short run, but the advantage faded by nine months [7].
   - Somalia 2021: evidence is limited to recipient surveys, and only 4% of recipients said earliness changed their decisions [8].

4. **Pooled and parametric risk-finance is not reliably fast.** The independent evaluation of African Risk Capacity found most responses were not faster than traditional aid, and payouts were generally untimely [10]. Model design matters: Malawi's 2015/16 policy initially failed to trigger [10].

5. **Export restrictions amplify price spikes; this is the best-supported finding in the trade literature reviewed here.**
   - Insulating policies explain roughly 45% of the 2006–08 rice price rise and 30% of the wheat rise [17].
   - Export surges and trade shocks preceded the price surges [18].
   - In 2022, restrictions again covered about 17% of traded calories at peak, more than the 2008 peak of 15.3%, led by wheat and Indonesian palm oil [19].
   - Restrictions are a collective-action trap: they are individually rational but collectively self-defeating [17].

6. **Reserves are not a free stabiliser.** Theory suggests storage alone is costly and partly benefits foreign consumers, and works best when combined with trade policy [16]. Evidence on how the actual reserves reviewed here have performed is thin:
   - APTERR is designed mainly as a contractual emergency mechanism, and no impact evaluation was found [12].
   - China holds an estimated half or more of world wheat, rice and corn stocks [20]. How that affects world market stability is not settled by any source here.

7. **India's PDS shows both the promise and the cost of a stock-backed safety net.**
   - Leakage fell from about 54% to 42% (NSS-based) between 2004–05 and 2011–12, with steep falls in reforming states [13].
   - Technocratic "fixes" can harm: randomised biometric verification cut corruption but excluded 1.5–2 million legitimate beneficiaries for a time [14].
   - At the WTO, India relies on the 2013 Bali peace clause to shield above-limit rice support [15].

8. **Cooperation mechanisms exist but under-deliver on stated goals.**
   - CAADP: no African Union member state was on track to meet its Malabo commitments [11].
   - Gulf states: overseas farmland strategies largely failed [21].
   - AMIS and the G20 2011 Action Plan improved market transparency and created a coordination forum, but the 2022 wave of restrictions [19] shows the limits of non-binding commitments [22].

## POLITICAL CAUSES OF FAMINE

The technical interventions reviewed below — cash, trade policy, reserves and early-warning finance — address how food is monitored, priced and delivered. They do not, on their own, explain why Famine has occurred. The IPC, which supplies this brief's definition of Famine, states that the six confirmed cases of the past 15 years "were overwhelmingly driven by armed conflict and restrictions on humanitarian access" [3]. Its first recommended actions when Famine is detected are the cessation of conflicts and the securing of humanitarian access [3]. The official United Nations meetings coverage of Security Council resolution 2417 (2018) records that the Council, in adopting the resolution, strongly condemned the starving of civilians and the unlawful denial of humanitarian access as tactics of warfare [27].

**What the IPC attributes to each confirmed case.** The following summaries follow the fact sheet's own wording [3], with the later Gaza update in [23].

- **Somalia, 2011.** Famine was classified in July 2011 in parts of Southern Somalia, including districts of Middle Shabelle and IDP settlements. About 490,000 people were in Catastrophe "due to conflict and drought" [3].
- **South Sudan, 2017.** Famine was declared in February 2017 in parts of Unity State, after "three years of civil war" had devastated livelihoods, together with a weak economy and high food prices. Unity State was "at the centre of some of the fiercest fighting" [3].
- **South Sudan, 2020.** In November 2020 four payams in Pibor county were classified as "Famine Likely". Pibor was "particularly affected by violence and flooding", which destroyed homes and livelihoods, cut off humanitarian services and, with sharp price rises, "drove tens of thousands of people into Famine" [3].
- **Sudan, 2024 and 2025.** The December 2024 classification was "driven by the devastating conflict", mass displacement, economic collapse and "poor humanitarian access" [3]. In September 2025 the Famine Review Committee concluded that Famine (with reasonable evidence) was ongoing in El Fasher and Kadugli and likely to persist through January 2026, in a civil war that began in April 2023 [3]. Official public statements by the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces on those classifications were not opened for this brief, so they are not quoted here.
- **Gaza Strip, 2025.** In August 2025 the IPC determined that Famine was occurring in Gaza Governorate, "driven by relentless conflict", and projected that it would expand to Deir al-Balah and Khan Younis [3]. In the 19 December 2025 update, after "improved access for both humanitarian and commercial food deliveries" following the 10 October 2025 ceasefire, no area of the Strip was classified in Famine. The territory remained in Emergency (Phase 4), and more than 100,000 people were still in Catastrophe [23].

**Attributed positions on the Gaza findings.** The IPC's December 2025 analysis is the classification itself [23]. Israel's Coordinator of Government Activities in the Territories (COGAT), which coordinates the entry of goods into Gaza, rejected that analysis. In a statement reported by the Times of Israel on 19 December 2025, COGAT said the report "portrays a distorted, biased and unfounded picture of the humanitarian situation in the Gaza Strip", alleged "severe gaps in data collection", and stated that 600 to 800 aid trucks had entered daily since late October, including more than 500,000 tons of food during the ceasefire period [24]. Reuters likewise reported that Israel rejected the assessment [24]. This brief records those positions; it does not adjudicate them. A statement by Hamas on the December 2025 classification was not found in the sources opened here.

**Access and the protection of civilians as an intervention.** *Evidence: observational and institutional, not experimentally identified.* The IPC puts cessation of conflict and humanitarian access first among its recommended actions [3], and the December 2025 Gaza update is a before-and-after observation: once a ceasefire was in place and access improved, the area Famine classification was lifted, while Emergency and household Catastrophe conditions remained and the classifying agencies warned that the gains were "extremely fragile" [23]. That sequence is consistent with the IPC's account of access as a prerequisite, but it is not a controlled estimate of lives saved, and the parties dispute the underlying data [24]. No randomised or quasi-experimental evaluation of ceasefires or access negotiations as famine-prevention instruments was found among these sources. The honest rating is therefore that access and civilian protection are necessary conditions in conflict-driven famines, on the IPC's own evidence, but that the causal magnitude of any given access improvement is not identified here.

## CONTESTED / UNCERTAIN POINTS

- **How strong the anticipatory-action evidence is.**
  - Only one study here is a true RCT [7], and its benefits were short-lived.
  - The Bangladesh results rest on quasi-experimental comparisons [5, 6].
  - Somalia has no counterfactual [8].
  - The marginal value of being *earlier* (rather than simply giving cash) is small or uncertain in [5], and only 4% of recipients reported it mattered in [8].
- **Whether the 2025 FEWS NET gap caused harm.**
  - The gap and the reduced coverage are documented [1, 2].
  - No source here measures any downstream effect on aid decisions or mortality, so any such claim is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **HungerMap LIVE accuracy.** Its model accuracy figures appear only in WFP-authored materials and secondary summaries. No independent validation was verified for this brief [4].
- **Do reserves stabilise prices or distort markets?** The model evidence [16] says storage alone is detrimental to consumers in a small open economy. Real-world effects of large reserves are not established by these sources:
  - China's stocks [20];
  - India's buffer stocks [13, 15].
- **China's stock data.** The shares are USDA estimates [20]; Chinese state reserve data are not public in any source here. The figures should be read as approximate.
- **WTO public stockholding.** One side treats the peace clause as legitimate food-security space; others treat above-limit support as trade-distorting. The cited sources report the dispute but do not resolve it [15]. The TWN account [15] is advocacy-adjacent trade journalism.
- **India PDS leakage numbers.** The figures vary by data source (NSS vs IHDS) and method. Drèze and Khera directly dispute the government committee's 47% estimate [13].
- **Gulf land investments.** Woertz [21] is a short commentary rather than a systematic review. Land-rights harms associated with the land-acquisition wave are not quantified in any source here (**UNVERIFIED** in this brief).
- **APTERR scale.** The earmarked 787,000 t figure could not be retrieved directly (**UNVERIFIED**) [12].
- **Pooled risk insurance.** One evaluation [10] is negative on timeliness, but it covers ARC only through Phase 1 case studies. Results may not generalise to other pools such as Start Ready [9].

## SOLUTIONS RANKED BY STRENGTH OF EVIDENCE

The list below ranks *technical* interventions by the strength of causal evidence reviewed in this brief. It is preceded by a political intervention that the IPC itself places first, but which cannot be ranked on the same experimental scale.

0. **Cessation of hostilities and reliable humanitarian access.** *Evidence: observational and institutional [3, 23]; not experimentally tested.* In conflict-driven famines this is a prerequisite rather than a substitute for food assistance. The December 2025 Gaza update shows an area Famine classification being lifted after a ceasefire and improved access, while Emergency and Catastrophe conditions persisted [23]. Parties may dispute the data [24]. See the political-causes section.

1. **Forecast-triggered cash before floods and droughts (anticipatory cash).** *Evidence: moderate. One RCT [7] plus two QE studies [5, 6]; the gains are mostly short-term.* Pre-agree the triggers, delivery channels (mobile money) and beneficiary lists so cash lands before the shock peaks. Expect short-run food and borrowing benefits rather than lasting livelihood gains [7].

2. **Restrain export restrictions; exempt humanitarian purchases.** *Evidence: strong that restrictions amplify spikes [17, 18, 19]. Weak or absent on whether coordination commitments change behaviour [22].* Possible policy levers:
   - binding limits or notification rules for restrictions;
   - keeping the G20 exemption for WFP purchases [22];
   - pairing domestic safety nets with open trade, rather than relying on insulation [16, 17].

3. **Sustained, diversified funding for early-warning systems (FEWS NET, IPC analyses, HungerMap).** *Evidence: descriptive. The systems' role is documented [1, 2, 3, 4], but no causal estimate of lives saved was found.* The 2025 outage [1, 2] shows the risk of depending on a single funder. Multi-donor or treaty-based financing is a design response, but its effectiveness is not evidenced here.

4. **Better targeting and delivery in existing safety nets (e.g. India's PDS).** *Evidence: mixed.*
   - Observational leakage falls after state reforms [13].
   - Experimental evidence warns that strict ID verification can exclude many legitimate beneficiaries [14].
   - Reforms should be piloted and phased in, with monitoring of exclusion [14].

5. **Market transparency (AMIS).** *Evidence: weak or indirect. The institutional design is documented [22], with no causal evaluation here.* It is cheap and low-risk, so it is a reasonable complement but not a stand-alone solution.

6. **Pooled parametric risk finance (ARC, Start Ready).** *Evidence: weak to negative on speed so far [10]; Start Ready has programme descriptions only [9].* Value depends on model quality, government delivery capacity and pre-agreed contingency plans [10].

7. **Strategic grain reserves (national or regional, e.g. APTERR, China, India).** *Evidence: contested. Models favour combining storage with trade policy and warn that storage alone hurts consumers [16]; no impact evaluation of APTERR was found [12].* Small, emergency-oriented, rules-based reserves are better defended than large price-support stocks. That is an inference from [16], not a tested result.

8. **Overseas farmland acquisition for national food security (Gulf model).** *Evidence: weak and negative [21].* It has delivered little food to investor states. Value-chain and trade diversification has replaced it in Gulf policy [21].

9. **Continental agricultural-investment frameworks (CAADP).** *Evidence: process monitoring only. Targets were missed by all reporting states [11].* It is useful as an accountability scorecard; no causal effect on resilience is shown here.
