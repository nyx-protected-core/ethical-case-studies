# Israel and its neighbours: the case for cooperation, and the role of proxy warfare

A sourced brief for a public ethics case study. It sets out (1) the strategic, economic and security case for cooperation between Israel and neighbouring states, (2) the evidence on Iran's support for armed groups, (3) two analyst hypotheses tested against public evidence, (4) counterarguments, civilian harm, Palestinian perspectives and humanitarian-law obligations, with the legal status of each finding stated, and (5) the arms-export and trade restrictions that the United Kingdom, Canada, Germany and other states placed on Israel over the conduct of the war in Gaza, with the evidence on what drove them. The brief does not take a side. Each claim is tagged **[well-supported]**, **[contested]** or **[unsupported]**, and each factual statement points to a numbered source. Bracketed numbers such as [6a] refer to the Sources list.

**Verification note.** Every link below was checked on 18 Nov 2026. Each DOI was resolved through `https://doi.org/` and returned HTTP 302 to the publisher. Author, year and venue for DOI items come from the DOI registry record. Each URL returned HTTP 200 unless marked otherwise. Items marked **live-blocked** returned 401, 403 or 202 to an automated request (a paywall or automated-access check). Those pages are known to exist from search-engine indexing, and where possible an open copy or a secondary report is listed alongside. Some numbered entries group two or three documents from the same issuer or on the same narrow point. Entries 34–65 (group F) were added and checked the same way on 19 Nov 2026. The list now runs to 65 numbered entries (open mirrors and secondary copies are listed beside them and not always counted separately). Figures are quoted only where they appear in the cited document.

### E. Law, humanitarian findings and Palestinian perspectives

20. **International Court of Justice.**
   (a) **Advisory Opinion, 19 July 2024.** *Legal Consequences arising from the Policies and Practices of Israel in the Occupied Palestinian Territory, including East Jerusalem.* Case page: https://www.icj-cij.org/case/186. UN copy of the operative paragraph: https://www.un.org/unispal/document/advisory-opinion-icj-19jul24/. This is an *advisory opinion*, not a judgment in a contentious case. In the operative paragraph the Court “Is of the opinion that”, by 11 votes to 4, Israel's continued presence in the Occupied Palestinian Territory is unlawful, and, also by 11 votes to 4, that Israel must bring that presence to an end “as rapidly as possible”. By 14 votes to 1, it is of the opinion that Israel must cease immediately all new settlement activities and evacuate all settlers, and that Israel has the obligation to “make reparation”. By 12 votes to 3, it is of the opinion that all States must “not … recognize as legal the situation arising from the unlawful presence”.
   (b) **Orders on provisional measures in *South Africa v. Israel*.** Case page: https://www.icj-cij.org/case/192. The merits of the Genocide Convention case are undecided, and these orders do not decide whether genocide occurred. The 26 January 2024 order required Israel to take measures to prevent acts under Article II of the Convention and to enable humanitarian aid. The 28 March 2024 order added further measures after the Court found that “famine is setting in”: https://www.un.org/unispal/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/192-20240328-pre-01-00-en.pdf. The 24 May 2024 order required Israel to “immediately halt its military offensive, and any other action in the Rafah Governorate, which may inflict on the Palestinian group in Gaza conditions of life that could bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part”: https://www.un.org/unispal/document/icj-order-24may24/.

## Part 1: The case for cooperation

**1.1 The two peace treaties have lasted for decades. [well-supported]** The Egypt–Israel treaty (1979) [1] and the Israel–Jordan treaty (1994) [2] have stayed in force through several regional wars. The Jordan treaty went beyond security: Annex II sets water allocations and a Joint Water Committee [2], so water became a matter of treaty cooperation and not only of dispute. Both treaties are often described as a "cold peace": cooperation between governments, including on security, while the public stays hostile. In 2021–22, only 5% of Egyptians and 5% of Jordanians favoured normalisation [8].

**1.2 The Abraham Accords had a security logic and an economic logic. [well-supported]** The 2020 Accords with the UAE, Bahrain, Morocco and Sudan [3] built on years of quiet ties, driven by shared concern about Iran and about how long the US would stay engaged in the region [4][5]. Results followed. Israel's first free-trade agreement with an Arab state (the UAE) was concluded in 2022, and security agreements were signed with Morocco (2021) and Bahrain (2022) [5]. None of the Accord states cut ties after October 2023 [5].

**1.3 Water, energy and gas show that interdependence is possible. [well-supported as design; stalled in practice]** Project Prosperity was designed as two halves that depend on each other: Jordanian solar power for Israel, and Israeli desalinated water for Jordan [6a]. Each side would hold a stake in the other's supply, which is a standard argument for interdependence as a stabiliser. In November 2023, however, Jordan said it would not sign, citing the Gaza war [6b]. The EMGF brings Israel, Egypt, Jordan and Palestine together with European states in one gas body [7]. That is notable because it puts Israel and Palestine in the same intergovernmental forum.

**1.4 Saudi normalisation was moving before October 2023 and has stalled since. [well-supported]** Before the war, Riyadh pursued "creeping normalization", including airspace access for Israeli flights and quiet security contacts. It was assessed as willing to move without the immediate creation of a Palestinian state [9]. Since then, Saudi Arabia has made a credible path to Palestinian statehood a precondition [5][9], and INSS assessed in February 2026 that normalisation is "currently off the table" [9]. Several sources report that disrupting Saudi–Israeli normalisation was one of Hamas's aims [5][9]. That point is **[contested]** as to motive, but widely reported.

**1.5 What made agreements last or stall.**
- *Durable:* state-to-state security interests, written treaty commitments, and limits on how much the public has to accept. The treaties survived while publics stayed opposed [1][2][8]. **[well-supported]**
- *Fragile:* public opinion and the Palestinian question. Moroccan support for normalisation fell from 31% to 13% after October 2023, and Arab Barometer ties the fall to perceptions of the Gaza war [8]. Projects that needed visible joint signings (Prosperity) were the first to stop [6b]. **[well-supported]**
- *Leadership and domestic politics:* Saudi policy shifted with the crown prince's domestic legitimacy needs [9]. Sudan's civil war left its accession unratified [5]. Egypt's president called Israel the "enemy" in September 2025 [5]. **[well-supported]**

**1.6 Official positions of Israel and of Accord and mediating states. [well-supported as official statements]** Israel's government states that it is acting in self-defence: its immediate aims are to secure the release of hostages and “to deny Hamas and other armed groups in Gaza the capacity to continue attacking its citizens and territory,” “pursuant to its right and obligation to protect itself and its citizens,” and that the threat includes “Iran and its proxies” [28]. The UAE, an Accord state, called in June 2024 for “an immediate and permanent ceasefire in Gaza” and a settlement “based on the Two-State Solution and the establishment of an independent Palestinian state,” and commended mediation by Qatar and Egypt [31]. None of the Accord states cut diplomatic ties [5]. A primary official statement from Bahrain was not opened for this revision; Qatar’s mediator role is recorded here as described by the UAE [31].

## Part 2: Iran and armed groups

**2.1 Documented support.**
- **Hezbollah. [well-supported]** The US government says Iran has supplied thousands of rockets, missiles and small arms since 2006, has provided "hundreds of millions of dollars", and has trained thousands of fighters [10]. Independent scholarship treats Hezbollah as the closest of Iran's partners [12b][14].
- **Hamas and PIJ. [well-supported as to material support]** The US government reports Iranian weapons systems and "long-standing material, financial, and training support" to Hamas, and support to PIJ [10]. The US national security adviser said in October 2023 that Iran provided "the lion's share" of funding for Hamas's military wing [16a]. That is an official assertion; the underlying figures are not public.
- **Houthis. [well-supported]** The UN Panel of Experts inspected intercepted components and missile debris bearing makes, markings and serial numbers like those produced in Iran. It concluded that without foreign support the Houthis could not build complex systems such as ballistic and cruise missiles [11]. It reports technical assistance, training, weapons and money from Iran, Iraqi armed groups and Hezbollah, and joint operational centres in Iraq and Lebanon [11]. The group also opened a campaign against shipping in the Red Sea after October 2023. The Panel records the Houthi military spokesperson, Yahya Saree, describing strikes on ships bound for “the port of Haifa in occupied Palestine”; recruitment rhetoric to “liberate Palestine from the enemies”; the hijacking of the *Galaxy Leader* on 19 November 2023, with 25 crew members still detained at the date of the report; and “at least 134 attacks” launched from Houthi-controlled areas [11]. The Panel’s own assessment is that “the Houthis exploited the regional situation to strengthen the stability of their regime,” and that a Yemeni peace road map “can only be signed when the regional situation is conducive and the Houthis cease attacking the vessels in the Red Sea” [11].

**2.2 Theory: delegation and deniability.** Principal–agent theory holds that states delegate violence to non-state groups to cut costs and risk, at the price of agency loss: the agent may follow its own aims [13]. Ostovar treats militant clients as central to Iran's way of war, citing extended reach and lower political cost alongside escalation risk [14]. The US government states that Iran "uses regional militant and proxy groups to facilitate deniability" [10]. That is an official interpretation, consistent with the theory but not a neutral finding.

**2.3 How much control Iran has. [contested]** Sources differ on how far Iran *directs* each group, as opposed to *supplying* it.
- Katz describes a spectrum "from ally to proxy" and an "Iranian-led alliance", not simple command [12b].
- The Yemen panel says the Houthis "exploited the regional situation" to deepen cooperation with the axis [11]. That reads as a partner acting on its own interests, not one carrying out orders.
- The seized Hamas minutes, as reported, show Hamas *trying to persuade* Iran and Hezbollah to join, and going ahead without a firm commitment [17]. That suggests Hamas did not need Iran's permission on timing.

*Documented vs asserted:* the transfers of arms, money and training are documented by UN inspection and official reports [10][11][19]. Operational command over specific attacks is mostly asserted, and varies by group.

**2.4 Hezbollah and the Lebanon front.** Fighting between Israel and Hezbollah opened on the Lebanon–Israel border on 8 October 2023, a matter of public record reflected in the later casualty figures in section 4.0 [30a]. A primary official statement of Hezbollah’s own stated reason for those attacks was not opened for this revision, so none is attributed here.

## Part 3: Two hypotheses tested

### Hypothesis 1: that the 7 October 2023 Hamas attack functioned as a distraction sought by Iran

*Note on framing.* The hypothesis does not say what the attack would distract from (for example Saudi–Israeli normalisation, or attention on Iran's nuclear programme). The evidence below bears on whether Iran **sought, directed or timed** the attack.

| Evidence | Direction | Source quality |
|---|---|---|
| Iran's long-term arms, money and training "enabled Hamas to execute the attack" | Supports Iranian *enablement*, not timing | US official report [10] |
| IRGC helped plan from August and gave a "green light" in Beirut on 2 October 2023 | Supports | Anonymous Hamas/Hezbollah sources; denied on the record by a Hamas official in the same article [15] |
| Early US intelligence: key Iranian officials appeared surprised | Against | Anonymous US officials, explicitly preliminary [16a] |
| Supreme leader reportedly told Hamas "You gave us no warning" | Against | Three anonymous senior officials; self-serving for Iran [16b] |
| US government: "no evidence that Iran knew… in advance" | Against | Official, later than the October 2023 reporting [10] |
| Seized minutes: Hamas delayed the plan to court Iran; in July 2023 an IRGC officer reportedly welcomed it "in principle" but asked for time | Mixed: suggests general awareness of a plan, but Hamas setting the timing, possibly ahead of Iran's preference | Documents seized by the Israeli military and reviewed by a newspaper; Iran calls them fabricated [17] |
| Disrupting Saudi normalisation was reportedly a goal | Consistent with a shared Iran–Hamas interest; does not show Iranian direction | Analyst and parliamentary sources [5][9] |

**Verdict: CONTESTED overall. The strong form is UNSUPPORTED on public evidence.**
- *Supported:* Iran's long-standing support made the attack possible [10][16a]. An attack that derailed Saudi normalisation served interests that Iran and Hamas shared [5][9].
- *Contested:* whether parts of the Iranian system had general foreknowledge of a plan. The reported seized minutes point that way [17]; US intelligence and the US government's later report point the other way [10][16a].
- *Unsupported:* that Iran sought, ordered or timed the attack as a distraction. No public source shows Iranian direction of the timing. The most detailed documentary evidence, if authentic, shows Hamas deciding on timing after failing to secure a commitment from Iran [17].

### Hypothesis 2: that Russia's war in Ukraine functioned as a strategic distraction or supply-chain strain that benefited Iran while its nuclear programme advanced

**Timeline (from listed sources):**
- **17 Apr 2021:** IAEA verifies that Iran has begun producing 60%-enriched uranium [18a].
- **Feb 2022:** Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine (a matter of public record; the US sanctions documents describe the invasion [19a]).
- **Sep–Nov 2022:** US designations over Iranian Shahed UAVs used by Russia in Ukraine [19a].
- **Sep 2024:** US says Iran transferred ballistic missiles to Russia [19b]. Press reports name the Fath-360 system; that model name is **UNVERIFIED** here, because the cited official text does not name it.
- **13 Jun 2025:** IAEA estimate of 440.9 kg of uranium enriched up to 60% [18c].
- **13–24 Jun 2025:** Israeli strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities; **22 Jun 2025:** US strikes on three facilities [18b][18c].
- **2 Jul 2025:** Iran suspends cooperation with the IAEA by law [18c].
- **28 Sep 2025:** The IAEA Board recorded that earlier UN Security Council resolutions on Iran “shall apply from 28 September 2025” [18b]. France, Germany and the United Kingdom treat this as a valid “snapback.” Russia and China contend that the mechanism lapsed after the United States withdrew from the 2015 nuclear deal, and as of October 2026 the Security Council had been “unable to agree on whether the 1737 Committee’s mandate is valid” [29]. The reapplication is therefore **[disputed]**.
- **Jun 2026:** IAEA still cannot verify the stockpile or whether enrichment has stopped [18c].

**What is supported. [well-supported]**
- Russia–Iran arms cooperation is documented: Iranian-designed UAVs used by Russia in Ukraine [19a], and later ballistic-missile and munitions transfers [19b].
- Iran's enrichment advanced sharply: from 60% production beginning in April 2021 [18a] to 440.9 kg at 60% by June 2025 [18c]. Verification gaps are severe, and the IAEA reports loss of continuity of knowledge [18c].

**What is contested. [contested]**
- Whether the war *benefited* Iran overall. Arms sales deepened ties with Russia [19a][19b]. But Iran also drew new US sanctions for those same transfers [19a][19b], its nuclear sites were struck in 2025 [18b], and UN measures were said by some Council members to have been reapplied, a claim Russia and China dispute [18b][29]. The listed sources do not settle the net effect.

**What is unsupported. [unsupported]**
- That the war was a *deliberate distraction*, engineered by or for Iran. No listed source shows such intent.
- That the war *caused* the nuclear advance. The 60% step began about ten months **before** the invasion [18a], so the war cannot explain its start. Other explanations are not tested here, such as Iran's long-running escalation after the 2015 nuclear deal broke down.
- That a "supply-chain strain" benefited Iran. The listed sources show Iran using procurement networks for Western-origin parts, some for UAVs later provided to Russia [19b]. They do not show a strain that advantaged Iran's nuclear programme.

**Verdict: PARTLY SUPPORTED on the components (the arms cooperation and the enrichment advance are documented). UNSUPPORTED on the causal "distraction" claim.**

## Key findings

1. State-to-state peace with Egypt and Jordan has lasted for decades despite hostile public opinion. Security interests and treaty structure appear to carry these agreements more than popular support [1][2][8]. **[well-supported]**
2. The Abraham Accords produced trade and security ties that survived the Gaza war at government level [5], but public support for normalisation fell sharply, most clearly in Morocco [8]. **[well-supported]**
3. Cooperation projects that need visible joint action (Prosperity) stalled first after October 2023 [6b]. Multilateral frameworks (EMGF) include Palestinians alongside Israel [7]. **[well-supported]**
4. Saudi normalisation now depends on a credible path to Palestinian statehood [5][9]. **[well-supported]**
5. Iranian material support to Hezbollah, Hamas, PIJ and the Houthis is documented by official and UN sources [10][11]. **[well-supported]** The degree of operational command varies by group and is disputed [11][12b][17]. **[contested]**
6. On public evidence, Iran enabled but did not demonstrably time or direct the 7 October attack [10][16][17].
7. Russia–Iran arms cooperation and Iran's enrichment advance are both documented [18][19], but nothing in the public record shows the war was a deliberate distraction that caused the nuclear advance.
8. Sourced casualty figures and legal findings are set out in section 4, each with its source and legal status. The main Gaza and 7 October totals come from OCHA, which attributes them to the Gaza Ministry of Health and, for Israeli fatalities, to official sources via Israeli media; the ministry’s Gaza totals are disputed by Israel and do not separate combatants from civilians [23][24][28].
9. The UK (September 2024), Canada (from January 2024) and Germany (August–November 2025) restricted military exports to Israel that could be used in Gaza. They cited humanitarian access, international humanitarian law and the conduct of the war [34][35][43][45]. The measures were partial, covered military rather than essential civilian goods, and in Germany's case were reversed after the October 2025 ceasefire [46]. **[well-supported]** That protest movements *caused* these decisions is not established by the evidence found; the link is correlational and contributory at most [55]–[61]. **[contested]**

## Contested and uncertain points

- **Provenance of the seized Hamas minutes.** They were seized by one party to the war and reported by a newspaper whose page could not be fetched for this brief. Iran calls them fabricated [17]. Treat them as significant but not conclusive.
- **Anonymous sourcing on both sides of Hypothesis 1.** The WSJ claim of Iranian involvement [15] and the US and Iranian accounts of surprise [16] all rest on unnamed officials, and each party has reasons to shape the story.
- **Funding figures.** "Hundreds of millions of dollars" for Hezbollah [10] and "the lion's share" of Hamas military-wing funding [16a] are official assertions without public underlying data.
- **IAEA picture after June 2025.** The Agency cannot verify the stockpile's location or whether enrichment continues [18c], so current status is unknown, not merely unreported.
- **Cooperation and normalisation figures after 2025.** Trade volumes and project status after October 2023 are not quantified in the listed sources.
- **Missile model naming (Fath-360)** is UNVERIFIED against an official text in this brief.
- **Casualty figures.** OCHA has not independently verified the Gaza Ministry of Health totals. Israel disputes them and notes that they do not disclose militant deaths [23][28]. The “Over 1,200” Israeli fatality figure in the OCHA snapshot is footnoted as coming from Israeli media citing official sources, and includes the immediate aftermath and foreign nationals, not civilians only [23].
- **Civilian harm in northern Israel and Iran, and a Yemen-specific death toll**, were not given because a primary or UN-verified figure was not opened for this revision.
- **Effect of protest on the 2024–2025 arms-export restrictions.** No peer-reviewed study found for this revision estimates the causal effect of protest or public opinion on the UK, Canadian or German decisions. The research cited in section 5.6 is general or US-based [55]–[61]. Peer-reviewed public-opinion series for the UK, Canada and Germany on this war were not found, so no opinion figures for those countries are given.
- **Antisemitic incidents and protest.** Whether, and how far, antisemitic incidents accompanied some protests was not assessed from peer-reviewed sources in this revision. The US finding that the protest wave was nonviolent [55] does not answer that question.
- **Contents of the restricted and unrestricted exports.** The governments did not publish item-level lists. Canada's 2025 exports to Israel [44] and Germany's 2026 approvals [47] are reported as totals and categories only. The UK written statement HCWS64 [34b] could not be opened.
- **Israeli government statements.** The Israeli government's own web pages could not be opened, so its reactions are given from SECONDARY reports [54a]–[54c].

## Counterarguments and balance

**4.0 Civilian harm. [figures as reported by the cited sources; several are disputed or unverified by the UN]**

The casualty totals below are attributed to the body that produced them. Several do not separate combatants from civilians. They are not used here to assign collective blame.

- **Palestinians in Gaza.** OCHA’s snapshot of 4 November 2026 records 74,618 reported Palestinian fatalities and 175,920 reported injuries [23]. OCHA states that figures “yet-to-be verified by the UN are attributed to their source,” and that these casualty numbers were provided by the Ministry of Health in Gaza [23]. OCHA’s weekly reporting describes that ministry as operating “under the de-facto authorities” [24]. The ministry’s totals do not, in the snapshot, separate combatants from civilians; a breakdown of 74,237 identified fatalities as of 7 October 2026 is given only by age and sex (men, children, women, elderly) [23]. The Israeli government disputes these figures. In a December 2023 Foreign Ministry paper it said that casualty statistics coming out of Gaza, “including those reported by the UN and humanitarian agencies, come from Hamas’s Ministry of Health or other Hamas authorities which cannot be seen as a reliable reference and which do not disclose information about militant deaths” [28]. On 28 March 2024 the ICJ, in a binding provisional-measures order, found that “famine is setting in” in Gaza [20b].

- **Israel, 7 October 2023 and the immediate aftermath.** The same OCHA snapshot records “Over 1,200” reported Israeli fatalities and about 5,400 reported injuries. OCHA’s general disclaimer says casualty numbers were provided by the Ministry of Health and the Israeli authorities; a footnote on this Israeli fatality figure says it is “According to Israeli media citing official sources” and “includes fatalities on 7 October and the immediate aftermath, including foreign nationals.” The snapshot also records 1,162 identified Israeli fatalities, including at least 33 children [23]. These totals are not a civilian-only count. The Commission of Inquiry, drawing on Israeli sources, reported more than 1,200 persons killed, of whom “at least 809 were civilians,” including at least 280 women and 40 children, together with 68 foreign nationals and 314 Israeli military personnel [33]. The Commission found that Hamas and other armed groups deliberately killed civilians, took hostages and committed sexual violence; it also found that Israeli authorities “failed to protect civilians in southern Israel on almost every front” that day [21]. On hostages, the Commission found, on reasonable grounds, that 252 people were abducted to Gaza, including 36 children and more than 20 soldiers [33]. The ICRC has said that “more than 250 people were taken hostage” [32]. Israel’s Foreign Ministry, in December 2023, gave the figure as “over 240” [28].

- **Lebanon.** OCHA, citing Lebanon’s Ministry of Public Health, reported 4,047 people killed and 16,638 injured in Lebanon since 8 October 2023, as of 5 December 2024, with nearly 84 per cent of the deaths occurring after the mid-September 2024 escalation [30a]. These totals are not separated into civilians and combatants in the cited update. A verified primary total for civilians killed in northern Israel by Hezbollah fire was not opened for this revision, so no such figure is given here.

- **The West Bank.** OCHA reported that, as of 3 July 2026, 1,109 Palestinians, including 243 children, had been killed by Israeli forces or settlers in the West Bank since 7 October 2023 [30b].

- **Yemen and the Red Sea.** The UN Panel of Experts recorded the hijacking of the *Galaxy Leader* on 19 November 2023, with 25 crew members of various nationalities still detained at the date of its October 2024 report, and “at least 134 attacks” on shipping launched from Houthi-controlled areas [11]. A single, UN-verified total for Yemeni civilian deaths caused specifically by the Red Sea campaign and the strikes that followed it was not opened for this revision.

- **Iran.** The IAEA records Israeli and US strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities in June 2025 [18b][18c]. A primary official or UN-verified death toll from those strikes was not opened for this revision, so no figure is given here.

**4.1 Palestinian grievances predate and are independent of Iran. [well-supported]** The ICJ advised in July 2024 that Israel's continued presence in the Occupied Palestinian Territory is unlawful, that new settlement activity should cease, and that settlers should be evacuated [20a]. Baconi traces Hamas's trajectory through Palestinian politics, the 2007 split and the blockade of Gaza [22]. The occupation of the West Bank and Gaza dates from 1967, more than a decade before the 1979 Iranian revolution (a matter of public record). An account that starts only from Tehran therefore omits those earlier, independently documented Palestinian grievances: the occupation, the settlements and the blockade [20a][22].

**4.2 "Proxy" framing can erase agency. [contested, with strong support for the critique]** Treating Palestinian armed groups as Iranian instruments assumes a control that the evidence does not show. The seized minutes, as reported, depict Hamas trying to recruit Iran, not taking orders from it [17]. Katz describes alliance dynamics rather than command [12b]. Principal–agent theory itself predicts that agents act on their own aims [13]. The critique does not deny Iranian support [10]. It warns that "proxy" can stand in for an analysis of motive.

**4.3 Humanitarian-law obligations bind all parties.** The principles of distinction, proportionality and the ban on collective punishment apply whatever side or sponsor is involved [21]. The legal findings below are listed with their precise status. Israel rejects the Commission’s findings [21] and has challenged the ICC’s jurisdiction [25].

- **[commission finding, non-judicial]** The UN Commission of Inquiry found that Hamas and other armed groups deliberately killed civilians, took hostages and committed sexual violence on 7 October, which it characterised as war crimes, and that Israeli authorities “failed to protect civilians in southern Israel on almost every front” that day [21]. As to Israel’s conduct in Gaza, the same May 2024 report found “the war crimes of starvation as a method of warfare” and “the crimes against humanity of extermination; murder; gender persecution,” as well as disregard of distinction, proportionality and precautions, and collective punishment [21].

- **[arrest warrants: reasonable grounds to believe, not a conviction]** On 21 November 2024, ICC Pre-Trial Chamber I issued arrest warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant, finding “reasonable grounds to believe” that each bears criminal responsibility as a co-perpetrator for “the war crime of starvation as a method of warfare; and the crimes against humanity of murder, persecution, and other inhumane acts.” The Chamber rejected Israel’s challenges to jurisdiction [25]. The same day it issued a warrant for Mohammed Deif, then commander of Hamas’s military wing, finding reasonable grounds to believe he is responsible for crimes against humanity including murder, extermination, torture, and rape and other forms of sexual violence, and for war crimes including murder, taking hostages and torture [26]. An arrest warrant is not a conviction. The Deif warrant was later terminated, on 26 February 2025, after the Prosecutor notified the Chamber of his death [26].

- **[binding provisional-measures orders; merits undecided]** In *South Africa v. Israel*, the ICJ ordered provisional measures under the Genocide Convention on 26 January 2024, with further orders on 28 March 2024 (when the Court found that “famine is setting in”) and 24 May 2024 (requiring Israel to halt its military offensive in the Rafah Governorate insofar as it “may inflict on the Palestinian group in Gaza conditions of life that could bring about its physical destruction”). The Court has stated that such orders have “binding effect.” They do not decide whether genocide occurred. The merits remain undecided [20b].

- **[advisory opinion, 11 votes to 4]** In its 19 July 2024 advisory opinion, which is not binding in the way a judgment in a contentious case is, the Court “Is of the opinion that,” by 11 votes to 4, Israel’s continued presence in the Occupied Palestinian Territory is unlawful and that Israel must end that presence “as rapidly as possible.” It is also of the opinion that Israel must “make reparation,” and that all States must not recognise the situation as legal [20a].

- **[commission finding, non-judicial; not a court ruling]** In September 2025 the same Commission of Inquiry concluded “on reasonable grounds” that Israeli authorities and security forces “have committed and are continuing to commit” four acts of genocide, and that the President, the Prime Minister and the then Defence Minister “have incited the commission of genocide” [27]. The Commission is a fact-finding body mandated by the Human Rights Council, not a court; its findings are not judicial determinations. The ICJ has not ruled on the merits of the genocide case [20b][27].

**4.4 The strongest objection to the cooperation thesis: normalisation "over the heads" of Palestinians is unstable. [well-supported by recent evidence]** The Palestinian Authority criticised the Accords for neglecting a two-state solution [5]. When the Palestinian question flared, the most visible cooperation stalled: Jordan withdrew from Prosperity [6b], Saudi talks froze [9], and Arab publics turned further against normalisation [8]. Arab Barometer concludes that a "warm peace" would only have a chance after an independent Palestinian state is established [8]. Defenders of the thesis reply that state ties held even under that strain: no Accord state cut relations [5], and both treaties survived [1][2]. On this evidence, cooperation can last as a cold peace, but deepening it appears to depend on progress for Palestinians.

**4.5 A fair conclusion.** The evidence supports both of the following: cooperation between states brings real security and economic gains, and Iran arms groups that attack Israel. It does not support reducing the conflict to Iranian design, and it does not support a cooperation model that leaves the Palestinian question aside. A credible case for cooperation also has to take civilian harm, humanitarian-law obligations and Palestinian political agency seriously.

## Part 5: How concern over Gaza changed some Western arms-export and trade policy

**5.0 What this part covers, and a correction of framing.** Between January 2024 and September 2025, several governments restricted some supplies to Israel, citing the treatment of civilians in Gaza. The record shows these were mainly **restrictions on military and dual-use exports**, plus some trade and sanctions measures. They were **not cuts to essential supplies**. The UK said its suspension did not cover "food-testing chemicals, telecoms, and data equipment" or other non-military items [34a]. Its legal assessment used the phrase "supplies essential to the survival of the population" about *Israel's* duties toward people in Gaza, not about anything the UK sent to Israel [35]. Only one of the three main measures came from a parliamentary vote: Canada's House of Commons passed a non-binding motion [42]. The UK and German measures were executive licensing decisions [34][45]. Each measure is set out below with its scope, legal basis, stated reason and dates, followed by the evidence on what drove the shifts. Figures come from the governments themselves and are not independently audited (see the note at the head of Sources F).

**5.1 United Kingdom. [well-supported as to what was decided]**
- *Measure.* On 2 September 2024 the government suspended "around 30, from a total of approximately 350" licences for items it assessed were for use by the Israel Defense Forces in Gaza, "such as important components which go into military aircraft, including fighter aircraft, helicopters and drones, as well as items which facilitate ground targeting" [34a]. A later ministerial answer gives the number as 29 [40].
- *Legal basis.* Criterion 2(c) of the Strategic Export Licensing Criteria: the government will not grant a licence if "there is a clear risk that the items might be used to commit or facilitate a serious violation of international humanitarian law" [35]. The Foreign Secretary called this a "forward-looking evaluation, not a determination of innocence or guilt" [34a].
- *Stated reasons.* Israel had not done enough to facilitate humanitarian access, and there were credible claims of mistreatment of detainees, with the International Committee of the Red Cross denied access. The government said it could not reach "a determinative judgment" on the conduct of hostilities [35].
- *Carve-out.* Components for the multinational F-35 programme that "could not be identified as destined for Israel" were excluded, because the government judged that suspending them would harm the whole programme and international security [36a].
- *Court challenge.* The High Court dismissed a judicial review of the carve-out on 30 June 2025. It treated the international-law questions as non-justiciable and deferred to the executive on defence and foreign policy [36a]. It did not decide whether Israel had breached international humanitarian law. The Court of Appeal refused permission to appeal on 12 November 2025 [36b].
- *Later steps.* On 20 May 2025 the UK suspended negotiations on a new free-trade agreement with Israel's government and added settler-related sanctions [37]. This followed a joint statement with France and Canada of 19 May 2025 [38a]. On 10 June 2025 the UK, Australia, Canada, New Zealand and Norway announced sanctions on two Israeli ministers, Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich, over incitement of violence in the West Bank [38b]. On 21 September 2025 the UK recognised the State of Palestine [41a]. The 2024 licensing position remained in place in the government's April 2026 data release [39], and a minister told Parliament on 1 September 2026 that "approximately 50 licences have been refused since then" [40].

**5.2 Canada. [well-supported as to what was decided; Canadian official sources, used here only for Canada-specific points]**
- *Measure.* Since 8 January 2024, Canada says it "has not approved any permits for items to Israel that could be used in the current conflict in Gaza". It also suspended 34 previously issued permits "over the course of 2024" [43]. Its 2025 annual report says all permits suspended in 2024 "have expired" and that this "remains the approach" [44].
- *Parliament.* On 18 March 2024 the House of Commons adopted, by 204 votes to 117, an amended opposition motion calling on the government to "cease the further authorization and transfer of arms exports to Israel to ensure compliance with Canada's arms export regime" [42]. The same motion also demanded "the release of all hostages" and that "Hamas must lay down its arms", and it reaffirmed "Israel has a right to exist in peace and security" [42]. It was a non-binding call on the government, not a law. The government's own account dates the halt on new permits to 8 January 2024, before the vote [43].
- *Legal basis.* The Export and Import Permits Act, under which the Minister "shall not issue" a permit where there is a "substantial risk" of serious violations of international humanitarian or human-rights law [43].
- *What was not stopped.* Canada still recorded $14,671,705.14 in military exports to Israel in 2025, under 50 permits [44]. The report does not say which items these were or when each permit was issued. Two court challenges to the permits were pending at the end of 2025 [44].
- *Later steps.* Canada joined the 19 May 2025 joint statement [38a] and the 10 June 2025 sanctions statement [38b], and recognised the State of Palestine on 21 September 2025 [41b].

**5.3 Germany. [well-supported as to what was decided]**
- *Measure.* On 8 August 2025 the Chancellor announced that "the German Government will, until further notice, authorise no military equipment exports that can be used in the Gaza Strip" [45].
- *Stated reason.* The Israeli security cabinet's decision of the previous night to expand military operations made it "increasingly unclear how these goals are to be achieved". The goals named were the release of the hostages, a ceasefire and the disarmament of Hamas. The statement also said "Israel has the right to defend itself against Hamas' terror", and it called for full access for aid [45]. It does not name a specific legal provision.
- *Reversal.* On 17 November 2025 the government said the restrictions would be lifted with effect from 24 November 2025. It cited the ceasefire in force since 10 October 2025 and said it would return to case-by-case review [46]. In the first half of 2026 Germany approved 122 licences for Israel worth about €799.5 million, mostly for a large naval project [47].
- *Court context.* In *Nicaragua v. Germany* the International Court of Justice declined, by 15 votes to 1, to order provisional measures on 30 April 2024. In the same order it reminded "all States of their international obligations relating to the transfer of arms to parties to an armed conflict" [48]. The Court noted Germany's statement that, since 7 October 2023, "only four licences for 'war weapons'" had been granted, and that the value of licensed material had fallen sharply after October 2023 [48]. The ICJ's binding provisional-measures orders against Israel in *South Africa v. Israel* (January, March and May 2024) are described in section 4.3 [20b].

**5.4 Other states (brief, for worldwide context).**
- *Netherlands.* After a Court of Appeal order of 12 February 2024, the minister amended the licence so that F-35 parts could not be exported to Israel. On 3 October 2025 the Supreme Court held that the court should not make its own risk assessment, and it ordered the minister to reassess within six weeks under the "clear risk" test [49a]. On 14 November 2025 the government kept Israel excluded and set a further review within six months [49b].
- *Spain.* A decree-law in force from 25 September 2025 bans exports to and imports from Israel of defence and dual-use material, with a power for the cabinet to make exceptions [50]. Its title and preamble use the word "genocide", which is the Spanish government's own characterisation. No court has ruled on the merits of the genocide question [20b].
- *Türkiye.* In May 2024 the government halted "export and import transactions with Israel, covering all products". This was the broadest measure found, and the only one that went well beyond military goods. Its stated aim was to press for a ceasefire and more aid [51].
- *Colombia.* Colombia banned thermal-coal exports to Israel from August 2024. A 2026 decree later repealed the ban [52].
- *The Hague Group.* On 31 January 2025, eight states from Africa, Latin America and Asia (Bolivia, Colombia, Cuba, Honduras, Malaysia, Namibia, Senegal and South Africa) pledged to block arms transfers and port calls where there is a clear risk of violations [53].
- *Gaps.* Measures by Belgian regions and by other states were not verified for this revision.

**5.5 Positions for and against the restrictions.**
- *Israel's government.* It objected to the UK, Canadian and German measures (its responses to the other states' measures were not checked here). On the UK suspension it said the decision "will only embolden Hamas" [54a]. On Canada's halt, its foreign minister said the step "undermines Israel's right to self-defense" [54b]. On Germany's restriction, the Prime Minister's Office said Germany was "rewarding Hamas terrorism" [54c]. Israel's stated war aims and its view of the casualty figures are set out in [28]. These are official positions of a party to the conflict.
- *Argument that the restrictions weaken self-defence or reward Hamas.* Some critics inside the restricting countries made the same argument. In Germany, the president of the Central Council of Jews called the restriction disappointing and asked for it to be withdrawn [54d]. The restricting governments rejected the "reward" argument. The UK Foreign Secretary told Parliament that "Opposing the expansion of a war that's killed thousands of children is not rewarding Hamas" [37]. The UK Prime Minister said recognition of Palestine "is not a reward for Hamas" [41a]. All three governments limited their measures to items that could be used in Gaza [34a][43][45], and the UK said its step would not have "a material impact on Israel's security" [34a].
- *Argument that the restrictions were legally required.* Export-control law in each state requires a refusal where there is a clear or substantial risk of serious violations [35][43]. The Dutch Supreme Court held that a minister who reassesses a licence must apply the mandatory Arms Trade Treaty and EU criteria [49a]. The ICJ reminded all states of their arms-transfer obligations [48]. Critics of the UK government argue that the F-35 carve-out shows these legal duties were applied only in part [62].
- *Argument that law does not settle it.* Courts in the UK and the Netherlands deferred to ministers on the risk judgement itself [36a][49a]. Legal scholarship notes "the reluctance of national courts to intervene in export licensing decisions" [63]. On this view, the measures were policy choices made within legal discretion, not decisions that the law compelled.

**5.6 Did public protest drive these shifts? [contested; causation not established]**
- *What the evidence shows.* Pro-Palestinian protest in Western countries was very large and diverse. In the United States alone, researchers counted nearly 12,400 pro-Palestine and over 2,000 pro-Israel protests between October 2023 and June 2024. They describe the pro-Palestine wave as nonviolent, and they warn that the binary labels are "misleading" [55]. That study is descriptive and US-only. It does not measure effects on any government. Critics included Jewish organisations as well as Palestinian and other groups. In one US survey experiment, criticism from a Jewish-American organisation, or information on the death toll, shifted respondents' views [56].
- *What research says in general.* There is good evidence that public opinion moves policy more on salient issues [58]. Experiments with Israeli legislators show elected officials respond to opinion when deciding on the use of force [61]. Large movements often have influence, but how much they matter, and under what conditions, remains unsettled [57]. Tactics matter too. Nonviolent protest can win attention and sympathy, while violent or highly disruptive actions can cause a backlash [59][60]. These are general findings from other settings, mainly the US. They are **not** direct evidence about London, Ottawa or Berlin.
- *What the governments said.* Each government gave legal and situational reasons: humanitarian access and detainee treatment for the UK [35]; the arms-export statute for Canada [43]; and the Israeli cabinet's decision to expand operations for Germany [45]. Germany's reversal cited the ceasefire [46]. None of these documents credits protest. Governments have reasons both to present decisions as legally driven and to avoid appearing to yield to street pressure, so this silence is not proof either way.
- *Timing and other pressures.* Canada's halt on new permits (8 January 2024) came before both the 18 March 2024 motion and the court challenge filed on 5 March 2024 [42][43]. That makes the parliamentary vote an endorsement of a policy already in place, not its cause. The UK decision came from a legal review, while litigation was pending [35][36a]. Coordinated diplomacy among allies (joint statements and sanctions) preceded or accompanied the 2025 steps [38]. Germany's measure was tied to a specific Israeli decision and lasted about three and a half months [45][46]. Canadian scholarship critical of government policy argues that the underlying alignment with Israel changed little [65]. Work on Germany stresses the constraint of *Staatsräson* (Israel's security as part of Germany's own reason of state) [64].
- *Assessment.* The most the evidence supports is a **correlational and contributory** account. Protest, shifting opinion, court cases, the ICJ orders and allied diplomacy all rose together, so their separate effects cannot be told apart. No peer-reviewed study found for this revision estimates the causal effect of protest on the UK, Canadian or German decisions. Saying that "the movement changed Western policy" is therefore **[unsupported as a causal claim]**. Saying that "humanitarian concern, legal pressure and public debate coincided with limited and partly reversed restrictions" is **[well-supported]**.

**5.7 How this connects to the rest of the case.** These measures bear on the cooperation thesis in Part 1. They show that ties with Israel in Europe and North America, as in the Arab states discussed in section 4.4, became conditional on the conduct of the war. They also show that the conditions were narrow and reversible. Germany lifted its restriction [46], Colombia repealed its ban [52], and the UK kept the F-35 pathway [36a]. The measures came from many regions (Europe, North America, Latin America, Africa, Asia and Türkiye) and took different legal forms. That variety is itself evidence against reading them as a single coordinated campaign.
