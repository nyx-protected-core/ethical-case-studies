# Safe drinking water and sanitation for everyone: an ethics case study

Status date: 18 Nov 2026. Every figure below carries a source tag `[S#]` that points to SOURCES.md. Items marked **(unverified)** could not be confirmed from a fetched primary or peer-reviewed source and should not be quoted as fact. Items marked **(attributed)** are figures that a verified source reports from another study we did not open ourselves. Items marked **(our arithmetic)** are simple calculations we made from cited figures.

## 1. Why this is an ethics question

Water and sanitation are a textbook case of a basic need that is cheap to meet at the margin for the well-off and expensive for the poor. Piped networks are natural monopolies, the poorest households are usually the last to be connected, and the costs of failure (diarrhoeal disease, time spent fetching water, unsafe sanitation, chronic arsenic exposure) fall hardest on people with the least political voice. The ethical questions are therefore distributive (who pays and who gets the subsidy), institutional (who runs the service, public or private, and how they are held to account) and evidential (which interventions actually work, and what do we do when a well-designed trial finds no effect).

This study covers SDG targets 6.1 (drinking water) and 6.2 (sanitation and hygiene). It also touches source protection, which sits next to target 6.6 (water-related ecosystems). It does not assess wastewater treatment and water quality (6.3), water-use efficiency and scarcity (6.4), integrated water resources management (6.5), international cooperation (6.a), or, except in recommendation 9, the participation of local communities in water and sanitation management (6.b) [S59]. Target 6.b bears directly on the accountability argument in this study: services fail when communities cannot see, question or help decide how water points and utilities are run.

## 3. Who pays, and what works

### 3.1 Utility reform: Phnom Penh as the classic case

The Phnom Penh Water Supply Authority (PPWSA) in Cambodia is the most-cited example of a public utility turned around without privatisation:

- Unaccounted-for water fell from 72% in 1993 to 6.19% in 2008. Over the same period production rose 437%, the distribution network 557% and the number of customers 662% [S8][S11].
- A separate account gives non-revenue water of 6.15% and bill collection above 99%, alongside 24-hour supply and pro-poor connection and tariff subsidies [S9].
- One governance analysis explains the turnaround by "getting prices and governance right": tariffs closer to cost, managerial autonomy and performance incentives inside a public body [S10].

**Critiques and caveats:**

- PPWSA's first pro-poor scheme sold water in bulk to community representatives who were meant to resell it cheaply. The same study that celebrates the utility reports that the representatives "mostly sold water to the poor people at 10 to 20 times the subsidized rate", and that the scheme did not perform as expected, which led PPWSA to change its approach [S11].
- Much of the evidence on PPWSA comes from detailed studies built on the utility's own records and interviews with its staff and customers (figure sources are marked "PPWSA records"). It is a strong descriptive case, not a controlled comparison [S8][S11].
- A single city, with strong donor support and unusual leadership continuity, is a weak base for a general rule. Its lesson is that ownership matters less than governance, tariffs and accountability, and that lesson cuts both ways in the privatisation debate (section 6).

### 3.2 Tariffs, lifeline blocks and increasing block tariffs

The standard pro-poor tariff is an increasing block tariff (IBT): a cheap first "lifeline" block and higher prices for higher use. The World Bank's *Doing More with Less* (2019) found that this design often does not reach the poor [S6]:

- Water and sanitation subsidies are pervasive and large. Excluding China and India, they are estimated at $289–353 billion a year, or 0.46–0.56% of these countries' GDP; the report's headline puts government spending at around $320 billion a year.
- In the 10 countries analysed, an average of 56% of subsidies reached the richest fifth of the population and only 6% the poorest fifth.
- Only 35% of the utilities in the IBNET database covered at least their operation and maintenance costs.
- The report gives three reasons IBTs mistarget. Many poor households are not connected to the network at all. The correlation between piped water use and income is low. Poor households are more likely to share a connection, which pushes them into the higher, more expensive blocks.
- In the five African countries analysed, errors of inclusion and exclusion were between 90% and 100%.

Fuente and colleagues reached the same conclusion for Nairobi: high-income and non-residential customers receive a disproportionate share of IBT subsidies, and targeting is poor even among metered households [S7].

**Ethical point:** a subsidy that looks progressive on paper can be regressive in practice, because the poorest are not on the network. Connection subsidies and targeted support for non-networked households tend to reach the poor better than cheap water for those already connected [S6].

### 3.3 Results-based and output-based aid

In output-based aid (OBA), subsidies are paid to a service provider only after an independent agent verifies agreed outputs, such as working connections or latrines [S13].

- The Global Partnership for Results-Based Approaches (GPRBA), set up in 2003 and hosted by the World Bank, had a grant portfolio of 60 projects in 31 countries and one territory by 30 June 2025, with cumulative disbursements of $241.7 million. Water and sanitation (including irrigation) accounted for 29% of funding committed since inception [S12].
- **Bangladesh OBA sanitation microfinance:** subsidies were paid through a government-backed wholesale lender to microfinance institutions, after verification by an independent agent. The project supported 170,679 latrines against a target of 170,000, built over nine months. A further 14,448 latrines were sold to customers without loans. The case study itself says targeting of poorer households could have been better, with longer repayment periods and a higher subsidy share [S13].

**Caveat:** these results are reported by the programme's own case study, not an independent impact evaluation. OBA rewards verified outputs (latrines built) rather than outcomes (sustained use, health), so slippage (section 4) is still a risk.

### 3.4 Blended finance

Blended finance uses public or concessional money to draw in commercial finance. The OECD found that, from 2012 to 2017, official development finance mobilised USD 2.1 billion of private finance for water and sanitation, out of USD 157.2 billion mobilised across all sectors. That is a 1.36% share, which the OECD calls limited compared with other sectors [S14]. The Bangladesh OBA project above is described by its sponsors as a blended approach, combining grant subsidy with microfinance loans [S13].

**Ethical point:** blended finance can only work where there is a revenue stream to repay commercial lenders. In water, that revenue comes from tariffs, transfers or taxes. It does not remove the question of who pays; it moves it.

## 4. Sanitation behaviour change: the CLTS evidence

Community-led total sanitation (CLTS) uses community "triggering" to end open defecation, usually without hardware subsidies. The randomised evidence:

| Study | Design | Main findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mali (Pickering et al., 2015) [S15] | Cluster RCT, 60 intervention vs 61 control villages | Private latrine access 65% vs 35%; adult open defecation about 9–10% vs 33%. No difference in diarrhoea (22% vs 24%). Height-for-age Z +0.18 (95% CI 0.03–0.32); stunting 35% vs 41% |
| Odisha, India (Orgill-Meyer et al., 2019) [S16] | 20 vs 20 villages, intervention in early 2006, follow-ups 2010 and 2016 | In 2010, latrine ownership +29.3 percentage points and open defecation −6.8 points. By 2016, functional latrines and open defecation no longer differed, because control villages caught up and latrines in treated villages were abandoned or deteriorated (slippage) |
| Indonesia (Cameron, Olivia and Shah, 2019) [S17] | Randomised scale-up | Modest increase in toilets and less roundworm infection; no effect on anaemia, height or weight. Poorer households were constrained; there was no benefit where local government implemented, and the effect was counterproductive in villages with low social capital |

**Lessons:**

- CLTS can reduce open defecation, but effects depend heavily on who implements it and on local social capital [S15][S17].
- Gains can fade over a decade (slippage) [S16].
- Toilets built without the means to maintain or upgrade them may not last.
- Poor households may need financing or subsidy alongside triggering [S13][S17].

### 4.1 The null results on child growth: WASH Benefits and SHINE

Three large trials tested basic household water, sanitation and handwashing (WSH) packages against child growth:

| Trial | Size | Diarrhoea | Linear growth (stunting) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| WASH Benefits Bangladesh (Luby et al., 2018) [S18] | 5,551 women, 720 clusters | Reduced by sanitation (PR 0.61), handwashing (0.60) and combined WSH (0.69); not by water treatment alone (0.89) | No effect from water, sanitation, handwashing or WSH; nutrition improved length-for-age by +0.25 |
| WASH Benefits Kenya (Null et al., 2018) [S19] | 8,246 women, 702 clusters | No intervention reduced diarrhoea | No effect from WSH arms; nutrition +0.13, nutrition plus WSH +0.16. Handwashing adherence under 25% by year 2 |
| SHINE Zimbabwe (Humphrey et al., 2019) [S20] | 5,280 women, 211 clusters | Neither intervention reduced diarrhoea | WASH had no effect on stunting or anaemia; infant feeding raised LAZ by 0.16 and cut stunting from 35% to 27% |

**Interpretation:** a consensus statement by trial investigators judged the trials to have high internal validity. They concluded that the *specific, relatively basic* interventions tested did not improve linear growth and had mixed effects on diarrhoea. They argued for "transformative WASH": interventions that cut faecal contamination across the whole environment, not just in one household [S21].

**What this does and does not mean:**

- It does **not** show that water and sanitation do not matter. Safe water and sanitation are human rights in their own right (section 6), and they bring dignity, safety and time savings that a growth trial does not measure.
- It **does** show that cheap household-level packages, with partial adherence, are not enough to change child growth where contamination is everywhere.
- That makes the case for community-wide coverage, piped water on premises and safely managed sanitation, which are more expensive. That brings the ethics back to financing (section 3).

## 5. Keeping water points working

### 5.1 Scale of the problem

- A review of handpump data from 47 countries estimates that about **one in four** handpumps in sub-Saharan Africa is non-functional at any time. That was roughly 175,000 inoperative water points in 2015 [S22]. An earlier mobile-data paper put it at "one in three" [S25].
- A study of more than 25,000 handpumps in Liberia, Sierra Leone and Uganda found that non-functionality was associated with the age of the system, distance from the capital and the absence of user fees [S23].
- The Hidden Crisis project dismantled and tested 145 handpump boreholes in Ethiopia, Uganda and Malawi [S24]:
  - 19% were sited in aquifers with transmissivity too low to sustain a handpump;
  - the cylinder sat less than 10 m below the water table at 38% of sites.

  Physical siting and construction failures matter, not just management.
- Handpumps serve around 200 million people in rural Africa [S26].

### 5.2 Professionalised maintenance and smart handpumps

The default model of community management, in which a village committee collects fees and fixes the pump, has often failed. Alternatives being tested:

- **Smart handpumps (Kenya):** in a 12-month Oxford-led trial in 2013 on 67 community pumps, a GSM transmitter in the pump handle reported usage and failures. Average outage fell from 27 days to under three [S27][S25].
- **Professional maintenance service (Kwale County, Kenya):** 90% of handpump faults were repaired within three days of being reported, an "order of magnitude" better than community management [S28].
  - Health: in a cross-sectional study of that service, reported child diarrhoea was lower only where pumps were repaired within 24 hours (adjusted odds ratio 0.35, 95% CI 0.24–0.51). There was no reduction where repairs took longer than 24 hours. The authors conclude that maintenance regimes that cannot bring downtime close to zero will struggle to produce health benefits [S28]. Reliability gains do not automatically become health gains.
- **Kitui County, Kenya (policy brief):** professional maintenance cut repair times to under two days for piped schemes (otherwise 46–67 days) and handpumps (otherwise 43 days). For handpump users, dry-season alternative water can cost up to USD 0.38 a day, or 6–14% of daily household spending [S29].
- **Collective willingness to pay:** a framed field experiment with 864 participants in 144 Kenyan communities found that communities contribute more to maintenance as the risk of failure rises. Intentions to sign up for real professional services followed months later [S30].
- **Pooling:** risk-pooling maintenance across many water points at scale is the key to financial sustainability [S26].

### 5.3 Open data: WPDx and remote monitoring

The Water Point Data Exchange (WPDx), run by Global Water Challenge, is an open platform that harmonises water-point records collected by governments and partners under a common data standard. Anyone can browse or download the full dataset through its data portal [S57][S58]. Combined with remote sensors such as the smart handpumps above, open functionality data let governments and maintenance providers target repairs, track service levels and check donor claims.

**Modest tech caveats:**

- Open data are only as good as the surveys behind them, and records are often snapshots.
- Sensors cost money and need their own maintenance.
- Monitoring does not fix pumps; it only helps where someone is paid and able to respond [S27][S28][S29].

## 6. The human right to water, and the privatisation debates

### 6.1 The legal recognition

- **UN General Assembly resolution 64/292**, adopted 28 July 2010, "recognizes the right to safe and clean drinking water and sanitation as a human right that is essential for the full enjoyment of life and all human rights" [S31]. The recorded vote was 122 in favour, none against and 41 abstentions [S32].
- **Human Rights Council resolution 15/9**, adopted without a vote on 30 September 2010, affirms that the right "is derived from the right to an adequate standard of living" and is linked to the rights to health, life and dignity [S33].

The right does not prescribe public or private provision. It does create duties on states to ensure availability, quality, affordability and non-discrimination, whoever operates the service.

### 6.2 Cochabamba, Bolivia, 2000

- A 40-year concession was awarded in September 1999 to the consortium Aguas del Tunari. Five months later there were mass protests against tariff increases, and the contract was cancelled [S34].
- A second account adds that a new water law passed soon after the contract was seen as threatening traditional rural water rights. Clashes in February and April 2000 led to a national state of siege, the contract's cancellation and amendment of more than 30 articles of the law [S35].
- Peer-reviewed analysis treats the case as a failure of regulatory capacity and contract design as much as of private provision per se [S34].

### 6.3 Manila, 1997: two concessions, two outcomes

In 1997 Metro Manila's water services (about 11 million people) were split between two private concessionaires, Manila Water (East Zone) and Maynilad (West Zone) [S36][S37].

- **Diverging outcomes:**
  - Maynilad went bankrupt and returned its concession in 2005 [S36][S37].
  - Its non-revenue water rose from 63% in 1997 (for all of Metro Manila) to almost 70% at the end of 2005 in the West Zone, about double the East Zone's losses [S37].
  - Manila Water prospered and listed on the stock exchange [S36].
- **Why:** a natural-experiment analysis attributes the divergence mainly to internal factors (corporate governance, financial management and operations), since the two firms shared the same city, regulator and external shocks [S36].
- **Equity critique:** the concessionaires recovered unpaid water from low-income communities through policing and by shifting responsibility onto communities, while high-volume non-payers got technical fixes and arrears settlements. Aggregate coverage and loss statistics can hide who remains unserved [S38].
- **Later developments** (re-tendering of the West Zone, later contract revisions) were not verified for this report **(unverified)**.

### 6.4 Jakarta: concession, court rulings and the end of privatisation

In 1997 Jakarta's water utility, PAM Jaya, signed 25-year concessions with two private operators, Palyja (west) and Aetra (east) [S40].

**Court history (from a 2024 socio-legal study [S39]):**

| Year | Court | Ruling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2012 (filed) | Central Jakarta District Court, case 527/PDT.G/2012/PN.JKT.PST | Citizens' lawsuit against the privatisation |
| 2015 | Same court | Upheld all the citizens' claims |
| 2016 | Jakarta High Court, 588/PDT/2015/PT.DKI | Overturned on civil-procedure grounds |
| 2017 | Supreme Court, 31 K/Pdt/2017 | Reinstated the citizens' win and ordered the partnership ended (see also [S40]) |
| 2018 | Supreme Court case review (PK), 841 PK/Pdt/2018 | Annulled the 2017 ruling and sided with the High Court, so the contracts remained valid |

- A national newspaper dates the case-review acceptance to 20 November 2018, on a petition by the Finance Ministry, on the ground that the lawsuit did not meet the formal requirements of a citizen lawsuit [S40].
- **End of the concessions:** PAM Jaya formally ended the contracts with Palyja and Aetra on 1 February 2023, after 25 years [S41].
- **What followed** (news reports; status as of 18 Nov 2026 not independently confirmed):
  - **October 2022 (before the end):** PAM Jaya signed a "bundling" financing and operating agreement with a private firm, Moya Indonesia, for water treatment and production only. PAM Jaya kept ownership, distribution, customer service and step-in rights, and said this was not a second privatisation [S42].
  - **At that time:** PAM Jaya gave its service coverage as 65.85% and non-revenue water as 46.67%, with a target of 100% piped coverage by 2030 [S42].
  - **July 2025:** PAM Jaya reported preparing an initial public offering targeted for 2027, including conversion to a limited-liability regional company. It reported more than 2.5 million customers [S43]. Whether the conversion or IPO has progressed by the status date is **(unverified)**.

**Ethical point:** the procedural loss did not end the rights claim. Civil society used the courts' substantive findings to push for public management [S39]. Yet "remunicipalisation" in Jakarta now sits alongside new private contracts and a possible share listing, so ownership labels can be misleading.

### 6.5 Remunicipalisation: balanced evidence

- **The cost argument:** a meta-regression of empirical studies found no systematic cost savings from private production of water or waste services [S44]. The cost case for privatisation is weak, but so is any general claim that private provision is always worse.
- **Scale of the movement:** a review in *Geoforum* cites a count of at least 267 water remunicipalisations in 37 countries over 15 years, affecting more than 100 million people **(attributed; the count comes from an advocacy-research report)** [S45].
  - The same review warns that motives vary widely, from managerial cost-cutting to anti-capitalist politics.
  - It notes that austerity, legal barriers and company resistance may slow the trend.
  - It records that about 25% of recorded cases are in the United States [S45].
- **Paris (2010):** the public utility says the switch to public management produced annual gains estimated at €35 million, mainly from recovering profits that had gone to private operators [S46]. This is the operator's own estimate, not an independent evaluation.
- **Overall:** what matters most is the quality of governance, regulation, tariffs and accountability, whether the operator is public or private. PPWSA (public, strong) and Manila Water (private, strong) both performed well. Maynilad (private) and many weak public utilities did not [S8][S10][S36][S44].

## 7. Climate, groundwater and geogenic contamination

- **Groundwater depletion:** a global study of about 170,000 wells in 1,693 aquifer systems found widespread rapid declines (over 0.5 m a year) in the 21st century. Declines accelerated in about 30% of regional aquifers. Some aquifers recovered after policy changes or managed recharge, so decline is not inevitable [S47].
- **Arsenic in Bangladesh:**
  - The national hydrochemical survey sampled 3,534 boreholes in 61 of the country's 64 districts [S49].
  - Using 2009 national survey data, about 20 million people were exposed above Bangladesh's 50 µg/L standard and about 45 million above the WHO 10 µg/L guideline [S48].
  - Exposure was estimated to account for about 24,000 adult deaths a year (above 50 µg/L) and perhaps as many as 19,000 more (10–50 µg/L). The authors say an arsenic-related mortality rate of 1 in 16 adult deaths could mean US$13 billion in lost productivity over 20 years [S48].
  - The authors recommend prioritising the estimated 5 million people exposed above 200 µg/L and building local testing capacity. UNICEF's 2006–2011 country programme provided safe water in arsenic-affected areas at US$11 per person [S48].
- **Fluoride in India:** a modelling study based on about 12,600 groundwater samples estimated that about 120 million people (about 9% of the population) are at risk of fluorosis [S50].
- **Climate-resilient WASH:** UNICEF defines a climate-resilient water or sanitation service by three tests:
  - a climate-risk assessment informing technology and siting;
  - continued operation during and after extreme events;
  - a focus on the whole service rather than the hardware.

  UNICEF reports that, from 2019 to 2024, 34.3 million people in 84 country programmes received a climate-resilient water service and 19.9 million in 72 programmes a climate-resilient sanitation service. It also reports 11,019 solar-powered water systems [S51]. UNICEF has also published guidance for its offices on moving to climate-resilient WASH programming [S52]. These are self-reported programme results.

## 8. South-South examples

- **Brazil, One Million Cisterns Programme (P1MC):**
  - Rainwater cisterns for households in the semi-arid north-east, run with the civil-society network Articulação do Semiárido (ASA). Government figures cited in a 2025 case study give 1,146,210 cisterns built by 2022.
  - More than 100,000 were built a year in peak years such as 2013–2015, but only 5,946 in 2022 after budget cuts.
  - Health evidence is thin: the review found only eight studies. One Pernambuco study reported 73% fewer diarrhoea episodes among households with cisterns (RR 0.27) [S53].
  - Lesson: low-cost, community-run storage can scale fast, but depends on stable public funding.
- **Senegal, urban water affermage:**
  - A 1995–1996 reform created an asset-holding company (SONES) responsible for investment and a private operator (SDE) under a 10-year affermage (lease) contract with performance targets.
  - Unaccounted-for water fell from 31.5% in 1996 to 20% in 2003. A follow-on project built 81,000 free "social connections" for poorer households [S54].
  - A second-generation reform re-bid the contract; a new operator, SEN'EAU, was selected [S55].
  - Lesson: a lease with clear performance targets, a strong public asset owner and explicit connection subsidies can work. The private operator does not finance the network.
- **India, Jal Jeevan Mission (rural tap water):**
  - Per an official release dated 14 Aug 2026, rural households with tap connections rose from 3.23 crore (16.72%) at launch in 2019 to more than 15.91 crore (82.24%). The mission has been extended to December 2028, with a "JJM 2.0" focus on service delivery, source sustainability and functionality [S56].
  - These are self-reported connection counts. A tap is not the same as a reliable, safe supply, and the government's own shift towards "functionality" acknowledges this. Independent functionality data were not checked for this report **(unverified)**.
- **Bangladesh, sanitation:**
  - The GPRBA case study notes that CLTS moved millions of Bangladeshis from open defecation to basic pit latrines. The national strategy then shifted towards improving the quality of facilities, which led to the OBA sanitation microfinance project described in section 3.3 [S13].
  - Bangladesh's arsenic mitigation (section 7) is the counterpart lesson: a rapid shift to shallow tube wells, which reduced faecal exposure, created a geogenic poisoning crisis [S48].

## 9. Counterarguments

1. **"Water should be free because it is a human right."** The rights resolutions require affordability, not zero price [S31][S33]. Free or underpriced water for those already connected mostly benefits richer households [S6][S7] and starves utilities of the money to connect the poor. Counterpoint: lifeline volumes and connection subsidies can protect the poorest if well targeted.
2. **"Private operators are more efficient."** Meta-analysis finds no systematic cost saving [S44]. Manila shows that both success and failure are possible under the same rules [S36]. Contracts need regulatory capacity, which Cochabamba lacked [S34].
3. **"Public utilities are always better for the poor."** PPWSA's own early pro-poor scheme left the poor paying 10–20 times the subsidised rate [S11]. Many public utilities do not even cover operating costs [S6]. Ownership is not a substitute for governance.
4. **"The WASH trials show sanitation does not help children."** The trials tested specific, basic interventions with partial adherence. They found effects on diarrhoea in Bangladesh, none in Kenya or Zimbabwe, and no effect on growth [S18][S19][S20]. The investigators themselves call for more comprehensive interventions, not fewer [S21].
5. **"Technology (sensors, open data) will fix sustainability."** Sensors cut downtime only when paired with a paid repair service [S27][S28]. Physical siting failures also need better construction [S24].
6. **"Blended finance will close the gap."** Private finance mobilised for water is a small share of total mobilisation [S14]. It needs revenue streams that bring us back to tariffs and subsidies.

## 11. Recommendations

1. **Report the honest gap.** Use JMP multiples (eightfold for water, sixfold for sanitation, up to 18-fold in low-income countries) when setting national targets, and plan explicitly for after 2030 [S1].
2. **Retarget subsidies.** Move money from consumption subsidies for connected users to connection subsidies, social connections and support for non-networked households. Publish who receives subsidies by income group [S6][S7][S54].
3. **Judge operators by results, not ownership labels.** Require published performance data (coverage of poor areas, non-revenue water, collection, continuity) from public and private operators alike, with an independent regulator [S8][S36][S44].
4. **Pay for verified outcomes where possible.** Use output-based aid with independent verification, and add follow-up checks on continued use to catch slippage [S13][S16].
5. **Fund maintenance, not only construction.** Budget recurrent maintenance and pool risk across many water points. Professional service providers can be paid partly from user fees and partly from government [S26][S28][S29][S30].
6. **Go beyond household-level packages for health.** Aim for community-wide sanitation coverage and safely managed water on premises [S21].
7. **Protect the water source.** Include groundwater monitoring, arsenic and fluoride testing, and climate-risk assessment in every new scheme [S47][S48][S50][S51].
8. **Open the data.** Publish water-point functionality data in the WPDx standard so citizens, researchers and governments can see which points work [S57][S58].
9. **Make the right enforceable, and give communities a part in it.** Use the 2010 rights resolutions to set minimum service and affordability standards and grievance routes. Jakarta shows courts can be a venue, but outcomes are uncertain [S31][S33][S39]. SDG target 6.b asks governments to support and strengthen the participation of local communities in improving water and sanitation management [S59]. That is part of the same accountability duty: publish who is served, who is not, and how a complaint is heard, and give users a formal role in those decisions.

## 12. Limits of this case study

- Several key sources are programme self-reports: GPRBA, PIB/JJM, UNICEF climate results and Eau de Paris. They are labelled as such.
- PPWSA and P1MC evidence is largely descriptive, not causal.
- Status claims (JMP report currency, Jakarta post-concession arrangements, JJM figures) are dated 18 Nov 2026 or to the date of the source, and may have changed.
- Not checked: the West Zone re-tendering in Manila and later contract revisions; independent JJM functionality data; the remunicipalisation count's primary source.
- SDG targets 6.3 to 6.a are out of scope, except for the brief mention of source protection. Target 6.b is taken up only in recommendation 9.
