# Biological weapons: governing a ban that cannot easily be checked

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. It covers policy, governance, verification and the ethics of biosecurity only. It deliberately contains no technical information about pathogens, toxins, methods or equipment, and it should never be extended to include any. Figures come from the sources listed at the end and were checked in November 2026. Proposals marked "original" are this study's own suggestions, not positions of any cited author or institution.*

## The ethical question

Biological weapons are banned almost everywhere. The 1972 Biological Weapons Convention (BWC) was the first treaty to outlaw an entire category of weapons of mass destruction, and 189 states are now party to it (UNODA, n.d.). Yet the same science that produces vaccines, diagnostics and medicines could, in the wrong hands, be turned to harm. Unlike nuclear weapons, there is no rare material to count and no large facility that only a weapons programme would need.

The ethical question is: **how should the world govern life-science research so that its huge benefits continue while the risk of deliberate or accidental catastrophe is driven as low as possible, and who should carry the costs of that governance?**

Three narrower questions follow:

1. Can a ban be trusted if it cannot be verified in the way nuclear and chemical treaties are?
2. When research could save lives but could also be misused, who decides whether it goes ahead, and how openly should results be shared?
3. How can rival states that distrust each other cooperate on a threat that ignores borders?

## Scale of the problem

Biological risk is hard to measure because the worst events are rare. The figures below describe the governance gap, not the probability of an attack.

**The treaty has almost no institution behind it.** The BWC has no verification body and no inspectorate. Its only standing staff is the Implementation Support Unit (ISU) in Geneva, which grew to four staff positions after the 2022 Ninth Review Conference. Its staff are on one-year contracts, its mandate must be renewed at every Review Conference, and it receives no support from the UN regular budget (UNODA, 2025). A 2025 UNODA publication describes the four-person unit's annual budget of about US$2.1 million as already stretched, and notes that an effective verification mechanism might need a budget "orders of magnitude bigger" (UNODA, 2025, citing Revill, 2023).

**Transparency is voluntary and partial.** States exchange annual Confidence-Building Measure (CBM) reports. In 2024 a record 111 states parties submitted them (UNODA, 2025), which means that roughly 40 percent of the 189 states parties did not.

**The number of maximum-containment laboratories is rising fast.** The Global BioLabs Report 2023 counted 69 BSL-4 laboratories operating, under construction or planned across 27 countries, up from 59 across 23 countries two years earlier. About three-quarters are in urban areas. The report found biosafety governance stronger than biosecurity governance, and oversight of dual-use research to be the weakest area of all (King's College London, 2023).

**Countries are not prepared.** The 2021 Global Health Security Index, produced by NTI and the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, gave countries an average score of 38.9 out of 100 for preparedness for epidemics and pandemics, and found that every country had significant gaps (NTI & Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, 2021).

**History shows violations happen.** The Soviet Union ran a vast secret biological weapons programme after it had signed and ratified the BWC (Leitenberg & Zilinskas, 2012). A 1979 outbreak in the city of Sverdlovsk, officially blamed on contaminated meat, was later shown by an independent scientific investigation to have been caused by an accidental release from a military facility; 66 of the 77 documented patients died (Meselson et al., 1994). The case shows both that a treaty can be secretly broken and that accidents at weapons sites can kill civilians.

## Root causes

### The dual-use problem

Almost all knowledge, skills, and equipment relevant to biological weapons also have legitimate uses in medicine, agriculture and public health. Gregory Koblentz explains why this makes biological weapons unusually difficult to monitor and why verification on the nuclear model does not transfer easily (Koblentz, 2009). The US National Research Council's 2004 report on biotechnology research in an age of terrorism made the same point for research governance: the challenge is not to stop biology, but to identify the small set of work whose results could most seriously be misused and to review it before it starts (National Research Council, 2004).

### No verification since 2001

States negotiated a verification protocol for six years, but the effort collapsed in 2001. Since then the BWC has relied on voluntary CBMs, consultations between states, the right to complain to the UN Security Council, and five-yearly Review Conferences (Permanent Mission of Italy to the Conference on Disarmament, 2026; UNODA, 2025). In 2022, the Ninth Review Conference set up a Working Group to develop measures on international cooperation, science and technology review, confidence-building, compliance and verification, national implementation, assistance, and institutional issues. Its seventh session in December 2025 did not finish examining the draft final report; an extra session followed in February 2026, a ninth in August 2026, and a concluding session is planned for December 2026 ahead of the Tenth Review Conference in 2027. Verification, governance of the new mechanisms, technology transfer and financing remain the hardest issues (Permanent Mission of Italy to the Conference on Disarmament, 2026).

### Fast-moving science, slow-moving rules

New tools spread capability faster than governance adapts. The industry that makes synthetic DNA has screened customers and orders on a voluntary basis for over a decade, and its own experts have called for stronger, regularly updated screening that borrows practices from cybersecurity (Diggans & Leproust, 2019). An open, free screening tool was released by an independent nonprofit in 2024 to help more providers screen (IBBIS, 2024). Separately, researchers showed that artificial-intelligence tools built for drug discovery could be misused to design harmful molecules, a warning that general-purpose AI raises new governance questions (Urbina et al., 2022).

Knowledge alone is not enough to cause harm. Studies of past weapons programmes show that practical, hands-on ("tacit") knowledge was a major barrier, and that ignoring it can lead policymakers to overstate or misjudge the threat (Revill & Jefferson, 2014). Good policy has to avoid both complacency and panic.

### Weak, uneven laboratory oversight

High-containment laboratories are spreading to more countries, often without matching national rules on biosecurity or dual-use research (King's College London, 2023). Lab accidents and lapses can cause harm even where no one intends it.

## What has worked

**The norm itself.** The BWC has made the possession of biological weapons almost universally illegitimate. No state now openly claims a right to them, and 189 states have joined (UNODA, n.d.). Norms do not stop every violator, but they raise the political cost of cheating and justify national laws.

**Independent science that exposes the truth.** The Sverdlovsk investigation shows how patient, independent epidemiology can establish what happened even when a government denies it (Meselson et al., 1994). Independent investigation capacity is a form of verification.

**Prior review of the riskiest research.** The 2004 National Research Council report led to formal review of a defined set of "experiments of concern" and a national advisory board (National Research Council, 2004). When concerns grew about research that could make dangerous pathogens more transmissible, the US government paused funding in 2014 and ran a deliberative review, a process analysed in ethical terms by Michael Selgelid (Selgelid, 2016). Marc Lipsitch and Thomas Inglesby argued that such work should require a rigorous, quantitative risk assessment and a search for safer alternatives before approval (Lipsitch & Inglesby, 2014).

**Shared international guidance.** In 2022 the World Health Organization published a global framework for the responsible use of the life sciences, giving all countries common values, principles and tools for governing dual-use research (WHO, 2022).

**Industry self-governance, where it exists.** Voluntary screening by DNA synthesis companies shows that the private sector can act before law requires it, though coverage is incomplete (Diggans & Leproust, 2019).

**Preparedness as deterrence.** Strong public health systems reduce the harm any outbreak can do, whatever its cause, and so reduce the value of a weapon to an attacker. The Global Health Security Index shows how far most countries still have to go (NTI & Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, 2021).

## Ethical tensions

**Openness versus security.** Science advances through open publication. But some results, once published, cannot be recalled. Restricting publication protects the public in rare cases but can slow defensive research and erode trust in science. The burden of proof should fall on those who want to do the very small set of highest-risk work, not on everyone else (Lipsitch & Inglesby, 2014; Selgelid, 2016).

**Benefit versus catastrophic risk.** A small chance of a catastrophic outcome can outweigh large, certain benefits. Piers Millett and Andrew Snyder-Beattie argue that reducing the most extreme biological risks can be highly cost-effective even under conservative assumptions (Millett & Snyder-Beattie, 2017). The Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security has proposed a working definition of "global catastrophic biological risks" to focus attention on these worst cases (Schoch-Spana et al., 2017). The counterweight is that overreaction can block cures and vaccines that save millions of lives.

**Sovereignty versus inspection.** Meaningful verification means outsiders entering national laboratories, including defence and commercial ones. States worry about espionage and loss of trade secrets. Without access, however, everyone has to take everyone else's word for it.

**Equity versus control.** Article X of the BWC promises states the fullest possible exchange of peaceful biological science. Many developing countries see export controls and screening rules as barriers that keep them dependent. Wealthy countries see the same rules as essential safeguards. Fair governance has to deliver both security and access to medicines, vaccines and research capacity (Permanent Mission of Italy to the Conference on Disarmament, 2026).

**Threat inflation versus complacency.** Exaggerating how easy it is to build a weapon can drive secrecy, militarised research and fear, and can itself spread interest in the idea. Understating it leaves gaps. Honest risk communication is an ethical duty (Revill & Jefferson, 2014).

**Distrust between rivals.** Many of the states whose cooperation matters most, including the United States, Russia, China, Iran and Saudi Arabia, are all BWC states parties, but they deeply distrust each other's intentions. A system built only on trust will fail; a system built only on suspicion will never be agreed.

## Original proposals

These are this study's own suggestions. None involves any technical detail about pathogens or methods.

1. **Fund the treaty like it matters.** Raise the ISU budget and staff to a level that can support a science-and-technology review mechanism and an international cooperation mechanism, with multi-year contracts and a continuing mandate. UNODA's own assessment is that verification would need resources far beyond the current unit (UNODA, 2025).

2. **Verification in layers, not all at once.** Rather than waiting for a single comprehensive protocol, build trust step by step:
   - make CBM submission universal and publish a public summary;
   - offer voluntary, reciprocal peer-review visits between pairs or groups of states, including states that are political rivals;
   - give the UN a permanent roster of independent experts and laboratories able to investigate alleged use, so that cases like Sverdlovsk are not settled decades later;
   - and use the planned science and technology mechanism to advise states on what to monitor as science changes.
   This follows the logic of the UNIDIR primer on verification options (Revill, 2023).

3. **Universal screening for synthetic DNA, as a global public good.** Make sequence and customer screening a legal requirement for providers in every major market, backed by a free, open, regularly updated screening tool and an international standard, so that small providers and lower-income countries are not priced out (Diggans & Leproust, 2019; IBBIS, 2024).

4. **A global rule for the riskiest research.** Through WHO and the BWC, agree that the narrow class of work most likely to create pandemic-capable pathogens requires independent, documented risk-benefit review, a search for safer alternatives, and publication of the decision, wherever it is funded (Lipsitch & Inglesby, 2014; WHO, 2022).

5. **Certify laboratory biosecurity.** Ask every country with maximum-containment laboratories to adopt the international biorisk management standard and to publish whether each facility is certified, as the Global BioLabs Report recommends (King's College London, 2023).

6. **AI governance for biology.** Require developers of powerful AI models and biological design tools to test for misuse before release and to report results to an independent body, without publishing anything that would itself help misuse (Urbina et al., 2022).

7. **Pair security with access.** Link every new control to a matching commitment on Article X: vaccine and diagnostic manufacturing in the Global South, fellowships, and laboratory partnerships. This turns biosecurity from a demand made by rich states into a bargain that benefits all.

8. **A shared health-security agenda for rivals.** Joint outbreak exercises, shared genomic surveillance for natural epidemics, and joint statements against biological weapons are areas where states with deep political disagreements can still cooperate, because no state benefits from a pandemic. Working together on natural outbreaks builds the habits and contacts that verification later needs.

## Risks

- **Security theatre.** New paperwork without real capacity could give a false sense of safety.
- **Espionage concerns.** Peer-review visits and inspections could be abused for intelligence gathering; rules on access and confidentiality must be agreed first.
- **Driving work underground.** Overly strict rules could push risky work to places with no oversight at all.
- **Unequal burdens.** Screening and certification costs could fall hardest on poorer countries and small firms unless they are subsidised.
- **Weaponised accusations.** Investigation mechanisms can be used for propaganda. Independence, transparent methods and published evidence standards are the defence.
- **Information hazards.** Public discussion of biosecurity must stay at the level of policy. Any document, including this one, should never describe how to make or use a weapon.

## Recommendation

Treat biological weapons prevention as a shared global public good that is chronically underfunded. The ban is nearly universal, but the institution behind it has four staff and a budget of about US$2.1 million a year (UNODA, 2025). The most ethical path is to strengthen the BWC step by step rather than waiting for a perfect verification treaty: fund it properly, make transparency universal, build a standing capacity for independent investigation, require screening of synthetic DNA everywhere, and subject the narrow class of highest-risk research to independent review. Pair every new control with real access to the benefits of biology for poorer countries, and use cooperation on natural outbreaks to build trust between rivals. The December 2026 Working Group session and the 2027 Tenth Review Conference are the next chances to act.
