## 1. Introduction: what market power is and why it matters for the poor

A firm has **market power** when it can keep its price above what it would be in a competitive market, or hold back quality, output or wages, without losing so many customers or workers that the move stops paying. A monopoly (one seller) is the extreme case. A *monopsony* is the mirror image: one dominant buyer, for example the only big employer in a town, which can pay less than a competitive wage.

Market power is not illegal in itself. Firms often earn it by being better. The harm comes when power is used to exclude rivals, fix prices, or block entry, and when nothing (new entrants, imports, regulation) pushes prices back down.

### How it is measured

- **Concentration ratio (CR4 or C4):** the combined market share of the four largest firms. It is easy to read but hides how shares are split; a CR4 of 80% could be four firms at 20% each, or one firm near 80% (OECD's seed-market work makes this point; see Deconinck, 2020).
- **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI):** the sum of the squared market shares of all firms, from near 0 (many tiny firms) to 10,000 (one firm). Under the 2023 US Merger Guidelines, a market with an HHI above 1,800 is "highly concentrated", and a merger that raises the HHI by more than 100 points in such a market is presumed to harm competition (US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission, 2023).
- **Markups:** the ratio of price to marginal cost. Concentration measures *structure*; markups try to measure *outcomes* directly. Markups are hard to estimate and the estimates are disputed (see Section 3).

None of these is a perfect test. A highly concentrated market can still be competitive if entry is easy, and a market with many firms can be cartelised.

### Why it matters for the poor

Poorer households spend a larger share of their income on basics such as food, medicine, fuel, cement and phone service, which are exactly the markets where concentration and cartels are often found. They also own few shares, so they pay the higher prices without receiving the higher profits.

- Using Australian household expenditure data, Creedy and Dixon (1998) found that the welfare loss from monopoly power was **higher for low-income households**, such as those depending on pensions and benefits, than for high-income households.
- Across eight OECD countries, Ennis, Gonzaga and Pike (2019) estimate that in an average country market power **raises the wealth of the richest 10% by 12–21%** and **cuts the income of the poorest 20% by 11% or more**.
- In South Africa, a World Bank and OECD report notes that enforcement against cartels in wheat, maize, poultry and pharmaceuticals produced income gains for the poorest 40% that were **3.4 times** those for the richest 40%, and that overall poverty stood to fall by **0.4 percentage points** (World Bank and OECD, 2017).

## 2. Where concentration does the most harm

### 2(a) Seeds and agrochemicals

**What happened.** Between 2016 and 2018 three mega-mergers reshaped the industry: Dow and DuPont merged (their agricultural arm became Corteva), ChemChina bought Syngenta, and Bayer bought Monsanto. The "Big Six" became four major firms (Deconinck, 2020).

**How concentrated it is.**
- Even *before* these mergers, a USDA study found the global four-firm share of seed and biotech sales had more than doubled, **from 21% in 1994 to 54% in 2009**, and the four-firm share of global pesticide sales had risen **from 28% to 53%** over the same period (Fuglie et al., 2011).
- Concentration is much higher in particular crops and countries. For maize seed in 2016, the four-firm share of the overall seed market was **99% in South Africa** and **97% in Brazil** (Deconinck, 2020, Table 1).
- Without the remedies imposed, Dow-DuPont would have raised the HHI for Brazilian corn seed by about 1,000 points to around 3,900; Brazil's competition authority required Dow to divest much of its corn seed business (Deconinck, 2020).

**A note of caution on headline figures.** Statements that "three companies would sell 59% of the world's seeds" after the mergers rest on global sales totals. The OECD warns that these figures are weighted heavily toward the large GM seed markets of the Americas and say little about concentration in any one crop or country (Deconinck, 2020).

**Evidence of harm.** It is real but thinner than often claimed.
- US studies found that higher concentration raised seed prices. For corn, moving from perfect competition to monopoly was estimated to raise the price of conventional seed by about **US$15 per bag, against an average of US$94** (studies reviewed in Deconinck, 2020).
- An advance US estimate suggested cotton seed prices **might have risen 18%** if Bayer's and Monsanto's cotton seed businesses had been combined without divestiture (cited in Deconinck, 2020).
- However, a cross-country OECD analysis found **no clear link** between concentration and seed prices, and the review concludes that evidence on price and innovation effects "is currently limited" and is partly offset by competition remedies (Deconinck, 2020).

**Why it matters for the poor.** Smallholders who buy hybrid or GM seed have few alternatives and little bargaining power. Where farm-saved seed and public breeding are strong, they act as a check on prices.

### 2(b) Food commodity trading

**What happened.** Four trading houses, Archer Daniels Midland, Bunge, Cargill and Louis Dreyfus (the "ABCD" traders), dominate the physical movement of grain and oilseeds. An Oxfam research report described them as controlling "as much as 90 per cent of the global grain trade" (Murphy, Burch and Clapp, 2012). That figure is an upper-bound estimate; firm-level trading data are largely private, so precise shares are hard to verify.

**The food price spikes.**
- **2007–08:** In a detailed review, Headey and Fan (2008) found that the explanations that held up best were rising oil prices, the falling US dollar, biofuel demand and commodity-specific factors.
- **2022:** The FAO Food Price Index averaged 159.3 points in March 2022, its highest level since the index began in 1990, after war in the Black Sea region disrupted grain and vegetable-oil exports. Russia and Ukraine had together supplied about 30% of global wheat exports and 20% of maize exports in the previous three years (FAO, 2022).

**The debate over the traders' role.** This is genuinely contested:
- One side argues that concentration, opacity about private stocks, and financial speculation amplified both spikes, and calls for curbs on excessive speculation and more transparency over stocks and markets (IPES-Food, 2022; Murphy, Burch and Clapp, 2012).
- The other side finds weak evidence that financial investors drove prices. Irwin and Sanders (2011) reviewed the evidence on commodity index funds and concluded that the lack of a direct empirical link "casts considerable doubt" on the claim that they fuelled a price bubble in 2007–08.
- A fair reading: the main triggers of both spikes were supply and macroeconomic shocks. Whether concentrated trading and speculation made them worse is unresolved, and the lack of public data is itself part of the problem.

### 2(c) Pharmaceuticals and patents

Patents give a legal, time-limited monopoly to reward invention. The ethical problems arise when that monopoly is stretched beyond its purpose or priced out of reach of the poor.

**HIV medicines: the clearest natural experiment.**
- In 2000, antiretroviral (ARV) treatment cost **US$10,000 to US$15,000 per patient per year** from patent-holding originators. Competition from generic producers, mainly in India, cut first-line prices **by 99% over a decade, to as low as US$67 per patient per year** ('t Hoen et al., 2011).
- More than **60 low- and middle-income countries** went on to buy generic versions of patented medicines on a large scale ('t Hoen et al., 2011). This made it possible to treat millions of people.

**Evergreening.** Firms can extend protection by patenting minor changes to existing drugs. Studying all drugs on the US market from 2005 to 2015, Feldman (2018) found that **78% of the drugs associated with new patents were existing drugs, not new ones**, and that **50%** of the drugs that gained an extension went on to become "serial offenders" with repeated extensions.

**Pay-for-delay.** A brand-name drug maker pays a generic rival to delay launching a cheaper copy. A US Federal Trade Commission staff study estimated these deals cost US consumers **US$3.5 billion a year, or US$35 billion over ten years** (FTC, 2010).

**Insulin.** Insulin was discovered over a century ago, and patents on human insulin expired long ago, yet three companies (Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk and Sanofi) control **99% of the global insulin market by value and 96% by volume** (Beran et al., 2019). The same study found that prices were lower where purchasers relied less on the three leading companies and had more import sources.

### 2(d) Digital platforms

Search, mobile operating systems, app stores and advertising technology show strong network effects: the more users a platform has, the more valuable it becomes, which can tip a market to one firm.

- **EU – Google Shopping.** In 2017 the European Commission fined Google about **€2.4 billion** for favouring its own comparison-shopping service in its search results in 13 EEA countries. The EU Court of Justice upheld the fine on **10 September 2024** (Court of Justice of the EU, 2024).
- **EU – Google Android.** In 2018 the Commission fined Google **€4.34 billion** for requiring Android device makers to pre-install Google Search and Chrome. The General Court partly annulled the decision and set the fine at **€4.125 billion**, and the Court of Justice dismissed Google's final appeal on **2 July 2026** (Court of Justice of the EU, 2026).
- **US – search.** In August 2024 a US federal court found that "Google is a monopolist, and it has acted as one to maintain its monopoly". Google had handled about 90% of US search queries. In September 2025 the court barred exclusive distribution deals for Google Search, Chrome, Google Assistant and the Gemini app, and ordered Google to share certain search data with rivals and to offer them syndication services. It did not order divestiture (US Department of Justice, 2025). Appeals were pending at the time of writing.
- **US – advertising technology.** A separate federal court found Google liable for monopolising ad tech. In September 2026 it ordered behavioural remedies rather than a break-up: integration with rival and open-source tools (Prebid), data portability for publishers, a ban on discriminatory bidding by Google's advertiser tool, and a monitor for six years (US Department of Justice, 2026).
- **EU Digital Markets Act (DMA).** Regulation (EU) 2022/1925 sets rules in advance for designated "gatekeeper" platforms, including bans on self-preferencing and interoperability duties, instead of relying only on case-by-case enforcement after harm has happened (European Union, 2022).

Why this matters for the poor: free consumer services can hide the costs, which fall on advertisers, app developers and small businesses and are passed on in prices. Dominant platforms also shape which information and markets people in low-income countries can reach.

### 2(e) State and crony monopolies in developing economies

In many low- and middle-income countries, the most damaging market power is not a global firm but a domestic incumbent: a state-owned enterprise, a politically connected conglomerate, or a cartel protected by regulation.

- **Cement in Africa.** Final cement prices in Africa were **183% higher on average than world prices**. Even a modest cartel overcharge of 9.7%, the level seen in the Southern African Customs Union (SACU) cement cartel, would mean African consumers overpay by **US$2.5 billion a year**. Enforcement against the SACU cartel saved South African consumers **US$79–100 million a year**, and advocacy that removed a suspended import duty in Tanzania led to a **26% fall in retail cement prices** (World Bank, 2016).
- **Fertiliser.** Global export cartels raised the final price of potassium fertiliser in sub-Saharan Africa by **29%**, and in about **58%** of the African countries studied, one supplier controlled more than half the fertiliser market (World Bank, 2016).
- **Telecoms.** In 30 African and Latin American countries from 1984 to 1997, competition from mobile operators not owned by the incumbent was linked to more mainlines, payphones and connection capacity, and to lower prices for local calls. Privatisation helped mainly when paired with an independent regulator (Wallsten, 2001). Research summarised by the World Bank and OECD found that each additional mobile operator raised subscriptions by an average of **57%** across more than 40 African countries. Weak competition in Mexican telecoms was estimated to cost consumers **US$25.8 billion a year over 2005–09, about 1.8% of GDP** (World Bank and OECD, 2017).
- **Food staples.** Cutting maize prices in Kenya by 20%, by limiting price-raising purchases by the state National Cereals and Produce Board, was estimated to have the same effect as a 1.2% real income rise, with gains for the poorest deciles far larger than for the richest, and a net fall in poverty of 1.8% (World Bank and OECD, 2017).
- **Hidden cartels.** In developing countries, at most about **24%** of cartels are estimated to be detected (World Bank and OECD, 2017), so known cases understate the problem.

## 3. Evidence on outcomes

### Prices and markups

- De Loecker, Eeckhout and Unger (2020) estimate that aggregate US markups rose **from 21% above marginal cost in 1980 to 61%** (in 2016), with average profit rates rising from 1% to 8%. The rise came mainly from the top of the distribution; **the median markup was unchanged**.
- **This is contested.** Berry, Gaynor and Scott Morton (2019) note that production-function methods have shown useful broad trends, but that industries are so different that careful industry studies are needed. They find rising markups have several possible explanations, including higher fixed costs that come with lower marginal costs, and that in some industries mergers have driven price increases.
- Cartel evidence is less disputed. A meta-analysis of 395 cartel episodes found a **median overcharge of 19% and a mean of about 29%** of affected sales (Connor and Bolotova, 2006).

### Wages and monopsony

- Using job-posting data for over 8,000 US local labour markets, Azar, Marinescu and Steinbaum (2022) found the average market to be highly concentrated under the US merger-guideline thresholds. Moving from the 25th to the 75th percentile of concentration was associated with **5% (simple regression) to 17% (instrumental-variable estimate) lower posted wages**.
- Caveats: posted wages are not paid wages, and the causal estimate depends on the instrument used. The direction of the finding has been repeated elsewhere, but its size is still debated.

### Innovation

- Aghion, Bloom, Blundell, Griffith and Howitt (2005) find an **inverted-U**: innovation rises with competition from low levels, then falls when competition is very intense. In markets that are already highly concentrated, more competition is likely to raise innovation. In very fragmented markets it may not.
- In seeds, the two US studies of concentration and innovation reached opposite results, and the review attributes this partly to flawed measurement (Deconinck, 2020). Here the evidence is weak in both directions.

### Poverty and inequality

- The distributional evidence above (Creedy and Dixon, 1998; Ennis, Gonzaga and Pike, 2019; World Bank and OECD, 2017) consistently finds that market power is **regressive**: it takes more, as a share of income, from poorer households.
- Limits: much of this evidence comes from simulations that assume how much of a price cut is passed through to consumers, and from high-income countries. Evidence from low-income countries comes mostly from sector case studies, not economy-wide estimates.

## 4. Remedies with the best evidence

### 4.1 Competition law and merger control

- **Merger remedies work in some cases.** In the seed mergers, authorities required divestitures and licensing (for example in Brazilian corn and South African corn), which prevented the largest increases in concentration (Deconinck, 2020).
- Limits: merger control can only prevent future concentration. It rarely undoes past concentration, and it needs well-funded, independent agencies, which many low-income countries lack.

### 4.2 Cartel enforcement

- Cartels raise prices by about 19% (median) where they operate (Connor and Bolotova, 2006). Enforcement against them has some of the clearest pro-poor evidence available: the South African cement and staple-food cartel cases above produced measurable savings and larger gains for poorer households (World Bank, 2016; World Bank and OECD, 2017).
- The main tools with good track records are leniency programmes (which reward the first cartel member to confess), meaningful fines, and screening of public procurement for bid-rigging.

### 4.3 Open standards and interoperability

- Interoperability lowers switching costs, so customers can leave a dominant provider without losing their network or data. Examples include mobile number portability, open banking (customers can share their bank data with competing services), and the DMA's interoperability duties for gatekeepers (European Union, 2022).
- The 2026 US ad tech remedy relies on the same idea: forced integration with an open-source standard (Prebid) and data portability, rather than a break-up (US Department of Justice, 2026).
- Limits: interoperability remedies are complex to design and monitor, and it is too early to measure the effects of the DMA and the recent US remedies.

### 4.4 Patent pools and voluntary licensing

- The **Medicines Patent Pool (MPP)** negotiates licences from patent holders so that generic firms can make and sell medicines in low- and middle-income countries. As of its latest public figures, MPP reports agreements with **23 patent holders**, sublicences to **93 generic manufacturers and product developers**, about **62 billion doses** supplied over 2010–2025, around **168 million patient-years** of treatment, **148 countries** benefiting, and an estimated **US$2.6 billion saved** (Medicines Patent Pool, n.d.).
- An independent peer-reviewed model of two MPP licences estimated that the dolutegravir licence for HIV alone would yield about **15.5 million additional patient-years** of treatment, roughly **152,000 deaths averted**, and about **US$3.1 billion saved** between 2017 and 2032 (Morin et al., 2022).
- Caveats: these impact numbers come from models with counterfactual assumptions, and the MPP figures are the organisation's own estimates. Voluntary licences also often leave out middle-income countries, where the patent holder sees a profitable market.

### 4.5 TRIPS flexibilities and compulsory licensing

- The WTO's 2001 Doha Declaration affirmed that patent rules should be interpreted to protect public health, and countries have used it to license patents without the holder's consent ('t Hoen et al., 2011).
- **Thailand (2006–08)** issued government-use licences for seven patented drugs. An assessment estimated that about **84,158 additional patients** gained access over five years, with **12,493 quality-adjusted life years** gained and about **US$132.4 million** in benefits to society. The efavirenz licence had the greatest benefit. Exports to the US of three products that lost US trade preferences fell, but overall Thai exports kept rising (Yamabhai et al., 2011).
- **Brazil (2007)** issued its first compulsory licence, for the HIV drug efavirenz ('t Hoen et al., 2011).
- Limits: compulsory licensing brings trade and diplomatic pressure, and it needs local or regional manufacturing capacity.

### 4.6 Market entry and deregulation

- Removing state-created barriers is often the cheapest pro-competition reform in developing economies. Examples above include opening mobile markets (Wallsten, 2001; World Bank and OECD, 2017), removing the suspended cement import duty in Tanzania (World Bank, 2016), and limiting price-raising state intervention in Kenyan maize (World Bank and OECD, 2017).
- Lesson: privatising a monopoly without competition and an independent regulator brought few benefits (Wallsten, 2001). A private monopoly is not a fix for a public one.

## 5. Fair counterarguments

1. **Economies of scale and scope.** Some industries have high fixed costs, such as seed biotech, drug development, cloud and search infrastructure, and grain logistics. Large firms can spread those costs and may cut marginal costs. Berry, Gaynor and Scott Morton (2019) note that rising markups can partly reflect higher fixed costs paired with lower marginal costs, not just abuse of power. The OECD review finds that complementarities between seed, biotech and crop chemicals explain much of the restructuring in that industry (Deconinck, 2020).
2. **Patents fund R&D.** Without temporary exclusivity, private firms may not invest in costly and risky drug or seed research. The evidence supports *targeting* remedies at abuses such as evergreening, pay-for-delay and pricing in poor countries, rather than abolishing patents.
3. **Network effects can benefit users.** One dominant search engine, payment network or marketplace can be more useful precisely because everyone is on it. Breaking it up could reduce that value. This is part of why recent remedies favour interoperability and data-sharing over break-ups.
4. **Superstar firms and productivity.** Autor, Dorn, Katz, Patterson and Van Reenen (2020) find that US concentration rose most in industries where the most productive firms gained share, and that **industries becoming more concentrated showed faster productivity growth**. On this view, some concentration rewards efficiency, though the same firms also have high markups and a low labour share.
5. **Regulatory capture and over-enforcement.** Competition agencies can be captured by incumbents, used for political ends, or can block efficient mergers. In developing economies, the "monopoly" problem is often *created* by the state (licences, import duties, state boards), so more state power is not automatically the answer.
6. **Measurement is uncertain.** Economy-wide markup trends are disputed (Berry, Gaynor and Scott Morton, 2019), headline global concentration ratios can mislead (Deconinck, 2020), and the role of traders and speculators in food price spikes is contested (Irwin and Sanders, 2011). Policy should rest on market-by-market evidence.

## 6. Synthesis: where action most clearly helps the poorest

Ranked by strength of evidence and how directly the benefits reach poor households:

1. **Generic competition for essential medicines.** The evidence is the strongest and the effects the largest: a 99% drop in first-line HIV treatment prices ('t Hoen et al., 2011), backed by patent pools, voluntary licensing and, where needed, compulsory licensing (Yamabhai et al., 2011; Morin et al., 2022). Tackling evergreening and pay-for-delay extends the same logic (Feldman, 2018; FTC, 2010).
2. **Cartel enforcement in staples and building materials.** Cement, fertiliser, maize, wheat and poultry cartels take a large share of poor households' budgets. Enforcement has documented savings and pro-poor effects (World Bank, 2016; World Bank and OECD, 2017; Connor and Bolotova, 2006).
3. **Removing state-created barriers to entry**, especially in mobile telecoms and food staples, paired with independent regulators (Wallsten, 2001; World Bank and OECD, 2017).
4. **Interoperability and conduct rules for digital gatekeepers.** These are promising and now backed by final court rulings and new law, but the evidence on how much they help poor households is still emerging.
5. **Seeds and commodity trading.** Concentration is high and the risks are plausible, but the evidence of large price harm is mixed. The best-supported steps are careful merger review with divestitures, support for public breeding and farm-saved seed, and more transparency over grain stocks and trading positions.

The common thread: **competition matters most for the poor in the markets for basic goods they cannot do without**, and the most effective tools are those that let a credible competitor in, whether a generic manufacturer, a new mobile operator, an importer, or an interoperable rival.
