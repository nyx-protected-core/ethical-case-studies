# Men and boys as partners: ending violence against women and building workplace equality

*Ethics case study. Research only. Figures are those stated by the cited sources, dated as of the source, and were checked in November 2026.*

---

## 1. The ethical question

Violence against women is widespread, and most of it is committed by men. That fact has a second half that is easy to drop: most men are not violent, and no serious prevention effort can succeed if it treats men and boys only as a risk to be managed. The same is true at work. Women remain under-represented in senior posts and are more exposed to harassment. If equality at work is framed as a project done *to* men, rather than a change in how women and men work together, it will recruit fewer allies than it needs and will leave unfair processes in place.

This case is about engaging men and boys as partners in two related tasks: ending violence against women, and making workplaces fairer. It looks at the evidence from community and school programmes that have been tested in trials — among them SASA!, Stepping Stones and Creating Futures, Coaching Boys into Men, and bystander programmes — and at what is known, and not known, about workplace allyship. It asks what works, what fails, and what goes wrong when men are addressed only as perpetrators.

The test used here is practical as well as ethical. A programme is not a success because it has a good message. It is a success if it reduces violence that women actually experience, or if it changes workplace conduct and opportunity in ways that can be checked. Partnership with men is justified when it serves those ends, not when it becomes a stage for men to occupy.

---

## 2. The scale of the problem

Violence against women is common in every region. A WHO-led study of data from 2000 to 2018, published in 2021, estimated that one in three women — about 736 million — is subjected in her lifetime to physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner, or to sexual violence by someone who is not a partner. That level had remained largely unchanged over the previous decade. Intimate partner violence is the most common form, affecting around 641 million women. About 6% of women globally reported sexual assault by someone other than a partner, a figure likely to be an under-count because of stigma and non-reporting. Among young women aged 15–24 who had been in a relationship, one in four had already experienced intimate partner violence by their mid-twenties [1][2].

These are prevalence estimates, not a map of every perpetrator. They do show that the problem is not a rare crime confined to a small fringe, and that it starts early. They also show why work with boys and young men is not an optional extra. If one in four young women has already been subjected to partner violence, prevention that starts only after a first offence is late.

At work, the picture is quieter in the official statistics and still uneven. The ILO reported that, as of 2023, women held 30% of managerial positions globally [3]. The ILO also reported that women are 1.6 times more likely than men to experience sexual violence and harassment in the world of work, with young and migrant women at greater risk [3]. Workplace equality is therefore not only a matter of who is promoted. It is also a matter of who can do their job without being harmed.

## 3. What the programmes found

### 3.1 SASA! in Kampala: community mobilisation

SASA! is a community mobilisation programme developed by Raising Voices in Uganda. It works through trained community activists, women and men, over several years, and is organised in four phases — start, awareness, support and action — each built around a different idea of power. Its aim is to change the social acceptability of violence and the silence around it, not to run a short course for a closed group [4][5].

Tanya Abramsky, Karen Devries, Ligia Kiss, Janet Nakuti, Nambusi Kyegombe, Elizabeth Starmann, Bonnie Cundill, Leilani Francisco, Dean Malamba, Tina Musuya, Lori Michau and Charlotte Watts reported a pair-matched cluster-randomised trial in eight Kampala communities (four intervention, four control) from 2007 to 2012 [4]. Cross-sectional surveys of 18- to 49-year-olds were conducted at baseline (*n* = 1,583) and after four years (*n* = 2,532). The analysis was an adjusted, cluster-level, intention-to-treat comparison.

The trial found clear effects on attitudes and on one important behaviour, and a large but statistically uncertain effect on physical violence:

- Social acceptance of a man's use of violence against a partner was significantly lower among women in intervention communities (adjusted risk ratio 0.54, 95% confidence interval 0.38 to 0.79) and lower among men (0.13, 0.01 to 1.15), the latter interval just including 1.
- Acceptance that a woman can refuse sex was higher among women (1.28, 1.07 to 1.52) and among men (1.31, 1.00 to 1.70).
- Women's past-year experience of physical intimate partner violence was 52% lower (0.48, 0.16 to 1.39). The point estimate is large, but the interval is wide and includes "no effect", because variation between control communities at follow-up reduced the trial's power at the cluster level. The authors say so plainly, and this case should too.
- Past-year sexual intimate partner violence was also lower, but not significantly (0.76, 0.33 to 1.72).
- Men in intervention communities were significantly less likely to report concurrent sexual partners in the past year (0.57, 0.36 to 0.91).
- Women who had experienced violence were more likely to receive a supportive community response, but this estimate was very imprecise [4].

SASA! is often summarised as a programme that "cut physical violence by half". The point estimate supports that wording; the uncertainty around it does not support treating the 52% figure as a precise, settled effect. What the trial did establish, with tighter estimates, is that a long, community-wide effort that involves both women and men can shift the social climate that treats violence as acceptable, and can change at least some behaviour. The authors also noted possible contamination of control sites, interruptions to programming, and population movement, and asked readers to treat the results as short-term community-wide effects, not as the maximum effect under ideal conditions [4].

A later global evidence review for the UK-funded What Works to Prevent Violence against Women and Girls programme judged community activism to shift harmful gender attitudes and norms effective at reducing violence at community level when it is intensive, lasts several years, and is strongly designed and implemented. Weaker versions of the same idea should not expect the same result [5].

### 3.2 Stepping Stones and Creating Futures

Stepping Stones is a group programme, first developed in Uganda and later tested in South Africa, that works on relationships, communication, violence and sexual health. Creating Futures, designed for use in South African informal settlements, helps young people examine their livelihood options. It does not hand out cash or a fixed package of skills training. The combined programme was delivered to single-sex groups of about 20 women or 20 men, aged 18 to 30, in 21 sessions over about 12 weeks [6].

Andrew Gibbs, Rachel Jewkes and colleagues conducted a cluster-randomised trial in 34 clusters in informal settlements in eThekwini, South Africa, with 677 women and 674 men, and followed them for two years [6][7]. The findings were mixed in a way that matters for anyone who works only with men, or who trusts only one kind of report:

- Men who went through the programme reported significantly less perpetration of partner violence, less sexual violence against people who were not partners, lower alcohol use, and better livelihoods (including more saving and more engagement at work).
- Women reported much stronger livelihoods — higher earnings, more saving, and more engagement at work — but no reduction in their experience of partner violence [6].

The same pattern had been seen in an earlier Eastern Cape trial of Stepping Stones with younger, school-going women and men: men reported less intimate partner violence after two years, and less alcohol use after one year [6]. The What Works review recorded this as a wider issue with work that includes men: some intensive programmes are successful at reducing men's reported perpetration (Stepping Stones is the example it names), yet they did not reduce women's reported experience of violence [5].

That discrepancy is not a reason to discard the programme, and the trial team argued that it should be scaled up because of the gains for men and for women's livelihoods [6]. It is a reason not to declare a violence-prevention success on men's self-reports alone. Social-desirability bias — men learning what they are supposed to say — can move a survey without moving a partner's risk. Women's own reports are the harder test.

### 3.3 Coaching Boys into Men

Coaching Boys into Men uses sports coaches as messengers. Coaches receive a short training and then lead brief, structured discussions with male athletes, through a season, about respect, harm and when to step in. Elizabeth Miller and colleagues tested it in a cluster-randomised trial in 16 US high schools, with about 2,000 male athletes [8].

At three months, athletes in the intervention schools reported greater intention to intervene and more positive bystander behaviour. Recognition of abuse, gender-equitable attitudes, and dating-violence perpetration did not change significantly in the main analysis. Effects on the bystander measures were stronger where the programme was delivered at full intensity [8]. A one-year follow-up of the same trial reported less recent dating-violence perpetration and less negative bystander behaviour among intervention athletes [9]. The follow-up paper was not re-opened in full for this case (see Section 9); the direction of the published finding is used here, not a precise effect size.

The programme is a useful example of partnership in a setting where boys already listen to someone: the coach. It is also a useful example of limits. A short, coach-led discussion series can change bystander behaviour quickly. Changing attitudes and reducing perpetration took longer, was less certain, and depended on whether the programme was actually delivered as designed. A poster in a changing room is not the same intervention.

### 3.4 Bystander programmes

Bystander programmes train people who are not the main victim or the main aggressor to recognise harm and to step in safely. They have been widely used on university campuses and, increasingly, at work.

The What Works review, which assessed randomised and quasi-experimental studies from 2000 to 2018 plus the What Works trials, placed brief bystander interventions in the group for which there is good evidence of *no* effect on women's experience of violence when used as a standalone prevention method. The brief programmes in that group were mainly evaluated in the United States, often lasted one or two hours, typically targeted men, and showed no impact on intimate partner violence or on rape by someone who was not a partner [5]. The same review advised that prevention funds should not be spent on standalone awareness-raising campaigns or on those brief bystander sessions as if they were enough. They may still have a place inside a longer, multi-part programme [5].

That conclusion is easy to over-read. It is a verdict on *brief, standalone* sessions as a way to reduce violence, not a verdict on every bystander approach. More intensive campus programmes, delivered over a term or a year and built around practice rather than a single lecture, have a more mixed and in some cases more promising record. Those evaluations were not all re-read in full here (see Section 9). The conservative reading of the evidence is this: if the goal is less violence, a one- or two-hour workshop is not enough; if the goal is to raise willingness to intervene, longer, practised programmes are a better bet than a single talk, but they still need to be judged on violence outcomes, not only on attitudes.

### 3.5 Workplace allyship

The workplace evidence is thinner than the community-trial evidence, and much of it is about attitudes and perceptions rather than promotions, pay or harassment cases. It is still useful for the partnership question.

A 2024 review by Colette Van Laar, Aster Van Rossum, Natasza Kosakowska-Berezecka, Renata Bongiorno and Katharina Block argued that gender-equality work has too often been approached from one side only — removing barriers for women — and that men's involvement is necessary if progress is to continue [10]. They reviewed three connected issues: some men experience equality as a threat to status, which can reduce their willingness to take part; restrictive ideas of masculinity also harm men and can sustain sexism and harassment at work; and men can be effective allies, but only under certain conditions [10].

On the positive side, men who speak up for equality are sometimes taken as more credible, and as less obviously self-interested, than women who say the same thing [10]. That is an uncomfortable fact, but it is a reason to bring men in, not a reason to let them take over. On the negative side, "performative allyship" — easy, low-cost acts that look good and protect a reputation — can substitute the appearance of change for change itself [10]. Men who become the public face of a workplace equality effort can also, if they are not careful, reproduce the very inequality of influence that the effort is supposed to reduce, and provoke resentment that makes joint work harder [10].

The review also notes that men who break workplace masculinity norms can themselves face backlash, which helps explain why some stay silent [10]. A culture that rewards a narrow kind of toughness is a poor setting for either women's safety or men's honest participation.

What this literature does not yet show, in the sources opened for this case, is that a typical workplace allyship training course reduces harassment or narrows promotion gaps. Those are the outcomes that matter. Until they are measured, allyship programmes should be treated as a possible aid to a fairer process, not as a substitute for one.

## 4. What works, and what fails

Across these programmes, a pattern is visible.

**What has a serious claim to work:**

- Long, intensive community mobilisation that treats violence as a public problem and involves women and men as activists, not as a short workshop audience. SASA! is the leading trial [4][5].
- Longer, structured group work with men that challenges the use of violence and builds skills, sometimes combined with livelihood work. Stepping Stones, alone or with Creating Futures, has repeatedly moved men's reported perpetration and, in the later trial, men's drinking and saving [6][5]. Women's livelihood gains in the later trial were real; a fall in violence against women was not [6].
- Programmes that use existing relationships of trust, such as coaches in Coaching Boys into Men, and that run through a season rather than a day, with better results when delivery is complete [8][9].
- Work that changes norms in the surrounding community, not only the attitudes of a recruited group. The What Works review found this only for strongly designed, multi-year activism [5].

**What has a serious claim to fail, or to be insufficient on its own:**

- Standalone awareness campaigns [5].
- Brief, one- or two-hour bystander sessions sold as violence prevention [5].
- Light or poorly implemented versions of otherwise good models. Community activism that is not intensive, and Coaching Boys into Men delivered at less than full intensity, should not be expected to match the better results [5][8].
- Programmes judged only on men's self-reported perpetration. The Stepping Stones and Creating Futures trial is the clearest warning [6].
- Performative workplace allyship that leaves hiring, promotion and harassment procedures untouched [10].

**What remains uncertain:**

- Whether effects transfer across countries and income levels. SASA! was tested in Kampala; Stepping Stones and Creating Futures in South African informal settlements; Coaching Boys into Men in US high schools. Success in one setting is a reason to adapt and re-test, not to copy the logo.
- How to design work with men that also reduces *women's* reported experience of violence, not only men's reported use of it [5][6].
- The effect of typical workplace allyship courses on harassment and on who advances.

## 5. The risk of framing men only as perpetrators

Most of the world's intimate partner violence, and most sexual violence against women, is committed by men [1]. A prevention policy that cannot say so is not a serious policy. The error is not that statement. The error is to treat it as a complete description of men.

A perpetrators-only frame has several costs.

**It wastes partners.** Community programmes that have shifted norms have done so by recruiting men as activists, coaches, peers and partners, not only as a population to be lectured [4][8]. If the opening message is that men as a group are the problem, the men who would have done that work have a reason to stay away.

**It can increase defensiveness rather than responsibility.** The Van Laar review describes status threat as a documented barrier to men's engagement [10]. A message that collapses "some men are violent" into "men are violent" gives that threat a story to attach to. Responsibility is more likely when men can see a role that is not identical with guilt.

**It hides the gap between talk and harm.** If a programme's implicit aim is to get men to confess a group identity as potential aggressors, it may produce better survey answers than safer lives. The mismatch between men's reports and women's reports in Stepping Stones and Creating Futures is a reminder to keep the outcome where it belongs: in what happens to women [6].

**It can distort workplace equality.** At work, a perpetrators-only frame tends to shrink equality into a misconduct problem. Misconduct matters, and it should be investigated and sanctioned. Equality also requires fair access to posts, fair evaluation, and colleagues who will share influence and speak up. Men who are not harassers still have a part in those tasks. If the only role on offer is "suspect" or "heroic ally", most men will choose a third option, which is to do nothing.

**It can let a few men dominate the response.** Partnership is not the same as handing the microphone to the nearest willing man. The risk that male advocates receive more credit, and more influence, than the women who have done the work is real [10]. A serious partnership keeps women's authority in the work, and uses men's access to rooms and networks that would otherwise stay closed.

The alternative frame is straightforward. Men and boys are needed as partners because they are part of the relationships, teams, locker rooms and workplaces in which violence and unfairness occur, and because many of them can withhold, interrupt, or refuse to condone it. Those who do commit harm should be held to account. Those who do not should be given something useful to do.

## 6. Fair counterarguments

**"This shifts attention and money away from women."** It can, if it is badly designed. Work with men that is vague, brief, or mainly expressive should not take funds from services or from programmes with a stronger record. The evidence that exists, though, is that some of the better prevention models *are* models that include men [4][5][6]. The choice is not between helping women and talking to men. It is between programmes that change the behaviour of the people who do the harm, and programmes that only support those already harmed.

**"Men already have enough power; they should not be centred."** Centring men as the heroes of equality would be a mistake, and the allyship literature warns against it [10]. Including men as partners is not the same as centering them. SASA! is a community programme, not a men's programme. Coaching Boys into Men uses coaches as a channel to boys, not as the subject of the story. The standard is whether women are safer and whether workplaces are fairer.

**"Men's self-reported change is good enough."** It is useful information, and in more than one trial it has moved [4][6]. It is not good enough on its own. Women's reported experience is the outcome that prevention exists to change. Where the two diverge, as they did in Stepping Stones and Creating Futures, the divergence should be reported as a limit, not smoothed away [6].

**"Short workshops are realistic for busy schools and firms."** They are realistic, and that is why they spread. The evidence is that, as standalone prevention, they do not reduce violence [5]. A realistic programme that does not work is still a programme that does not work. If time is short, it is better to be honest about what a brief session can do — raise awareness, perhaps raise willingness to intervene — than to claim a violence result it has not earned.

**"Allyship training is just branding."** Much of it may be, if it stops at a statement and a badge [10]. The criticism is a reason to demand better design and harder outcomes, not a reason to leave men out of workplace equality.

## 7. Trade-offs

| Approach | What it can do | What it risks or cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| Multi-year community mobilisation (SASA! type) | Shift norms among women and men; large estimated fall in physical partner violence, with wide uncertainty; change some male behaviour [4] | Expensive and slow. Needs strong design. Results from Kampala will not automatically travel. The headline 52% figure should not be treated as exact [4]. |
| Structured group work with men, with or without livelihood content (Stepping Stones, Creating Futures) | Reduce men's reported perpetration, drinking and, in one trial, improve livelihoods for women and men [6] | May not reduce women's reported experience of violence [6][5]. Men's survey answers can move for the wrong reason. |
| Coach- or peer-led work with boys over a season (Coaching Boys into Men) | Raise bystander intention and behaviour; some later evidence of less dating-violence perpetration [8][9] | Weaker if poorly delivered. Attitude change was not clear at three months [8]. Tested in a US sports setting. |
| Brief bystander or awareness sessions | Easy to run at scale | Good evidence they do not, on their own, reduce intimate partner violence or non-partner rape [5]. |
| Workplace allyship efforts | Can bring men into equality work; men may be heard in rooms that dismiss women [10] | Performative versions can replace reform. Male advocates can monopolise credit. Effects on harassment and promotion are not well measured [10]. |
| A perpetrators-only public frame | Names the sex pattern of the harm, which is real [1] | Drives potential partners away, feeds defensiveness, and shrinks workplace equality into a misconduct story [10]. |

## 8. Recommendations

1. **Recruit men and boys as partners, and say why.** The reason is the goal — less violence against women, and fairer workplaces — not a need to make men feel included for its own sake. Tell them there is work to do, and that doing nothing is also a choice.

2. **Spend prevention money on programmes with a record, and with enough duration and skill to deserve one.** Multi-year community mobilisation and structured, multi-session group work have a claim that brief awareness and one-off bystander workshops do not [4][5][6]. If a shorter programme is all that can be run, do not advertise it as something that has been shown to reduce violence.

3. **Measure what happened to women, not only what men say about themselves.** Any trial or workplace programme that claims a violence or harassment result should report women's experience, men's behaviour, and, where relevant, the community's or the organisation's response. A fall in men's self-reports without a fall in women's reports is an unfinished result [6].

4. **Use people who already have standing with boys and men, and support them to do the work properly.** Coaches, supervisors, trade-union stewards and community activists can carry a message that an outside lecturer cannot. They need training, materials, time and supervision. Partial delivery produces partial effects [8].

5. **In workplaces, put process before branding.** A fair complaints procedure, clear sanctions, and promotion and pay decisions that can be audited will do more than an allyship logo. Invite men to take specific, costly actions — to interrupt harm, to share informal influence, to apply the same standard in hiring — and judge the programme on those actions and on outcomes, not on attendance [10].

6. **Do not let partnership become a takeover, and do not let accountability disappear into partnership.** Women who have built this work should keep authority in it. Men who commit violence or harassment should face consequences. The rest should be asked to help, not asked to confess a collective identity, and not offered a costume of virtue in place of a change in conduct.

## 9. Limits, open questions and unverified items

The evidence assembled here is stronger on community and school programmes in a small number of countries than on ordinary workplaces, and stronger on attitudes and self-reports than on long-term, independently observed harm. Several questions remain open:

- How to design work with men that reduces women's reported experience of violence, not only men's reported perpetration [5][6].
- Whether SASA! or Stepping Stones and Creating Futures retain their effects when adapted at scale, in other countries, or after the trial teams have left.
- What intensity and length a bystander programme must reach before it can claim a violence result, if it ever can [5].
- Whether workplace allyship training changes harassment rates, complaint outcomes, or promotion and pay.
- How to talk about the sex pattern of violence with enough candour to be useful, and without a frame that treats all men as perpetrators.

**Unverified, or only partly checked, in this case:**

- Exact effect sizes from the one-year Coaching Boys into Men follow-up [9]. The journal pages were not opened in full; only the direction of the published finding is used.
- The full text of Miller and colleagues' 2012 trial tables [8]. Main outcomes at three months were taken from consistent secondary accounts of the paper and should be checked against the article before reuse of any coefficient.
- Campus evaluations of more intensive bystander programmes, including work by Ann Coker and colleagues on Green Dot. These are often cited as more promising than brief sessions; they were not re-read in full here and are not used for numerical claims.
- Experimental studies of male workplace allies that report effects on women's anticipated isolation or perceived support (for example, work published in *Social Psychological and Personality Science*). These were not opened for this case and are not relied on for figures.
- The effect of the COVID-19 period on the global prevalence of violence against women. WHO and partners warned of increased exposure; they also said the full effect would only be known once surveys resumed [1]. No later global replacement for the 2000–2018 estimates was checked here.
