# Repair, Maintenance and Hidden Labour: An Ethical Case Study

*Version 1.0, November 2026. Research and writing only; no fieldwork.*

## Summary

Things that are hard to repair get thrown away sooner, and much of what is thrown away in rich countries ends up being taken apart by hand in poorer ones, often by children. This study looks at three repair and maintenance problems and examines one in depth: the cross-border flow of used and broken electronics into informal recycling in low- and middle-income countries, and the child and forced labour risk that comes with it. It proposes a "repair first, trace, formalise" package and sets out the ethical trade-offs honestly, including the risk that well-meant controls make the poorest workers worse off.

The anchor value is ending human trafficking and forced labour. To be clear from the start, the published evidence documents **child labour and hazardous exposure** in informal e-waste work very well. It does **not** yet measure how much of that labour amounts to trafficking or forced labour. This study treats that gap as an open question rather than filling it with assumptions.

## Part 1. Three candidate problems

### 1.1 Unrepairable electronics and the export of e-waste to poorer countries

**Problem.** The world generated a record 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022, and only 22.3% of it was documented as formally collected and recycled [1][2]. About 5.1 million tonnes crossed borders in 2019, and 3.3 million tonnes of that moved in an uncontrolled way, mostly as "used equipment" or e-waste sent from high-income to middle- and low-income countries [3]. There it is often dismantled, burned or acid-leached by informal workers, including children [2][4][5].

**Labour link.** The ILO and WHO describe waste picking as one of the worst forms of child labour, and note that children are used for dismantling partly because their small hands suit small parts [2]. The minerals inside the same devices carry a second labour risk upstream: cobalt for phone and laptop batteries has been traced to artisanal mines in the Democratic Republic of the Congo where thousands of children work [6].

**Solution sketch.** Make devices last longer (repair rights), make the exports that do happen honest (tested-working rules for "used" equipment), and fund safe, formal recycling jobs in the countries that receive the waste. Developed in Part 2.

### 1.2 Broken medical equipment in low-income hospitals

**Problem.** A study of inventory reports covering 112,040 pieces of hospital equipment in developing countries found that on average 38.3% was out of service (range 0.83% to 47% across countries) [7]. The three main causes were lack of training, weak health technology management, and poor infrastructure [7]. Equipment designed for well-resourced hospitals often fails in settings with unstable power, heat, dust and no spare parts supply [8].

**Labour link.** Indirect. Broken donated equipment eventually becomes e-waste itself; the WHO lists medical equipment among common items in e-waste streams [2].

**Solution sketch.** Fund local biomedical technician training and maintenance budgets alongside every equipment purchase or donation; require donors to supply manuals, parts and service tools; prefer equipment designed for the operating environment [7][8].

### 1.3 Under-maintained open-source software that critical systems depend on

**Problem.** Hospitals, banks, governments and phones rely on free, public code that is often maintained by a small number of unpaid volunteers, with little institutional support for upkeep [9]. When that maintenance lapses, the result is security breaches and service interruptions [9].

**Labour link.** Weak. The ethical issue here is uncompensated labour and public risk, not trafficking.

**Solution sketch.** Treat critical open-source components as public infrastructure with long-term, decentralised maintenance funding that works with existing communities rather than taking control of them [9].

### 1.4 Why the first problem was chosen

| Criterion | E-waste and repair | Medical equipment | Open-source upkeep |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scale with good data | Global, measured yearly [1] | Strong single study [7] | Mostly qualitative [9] |
| Link to forced or child labour | Direct and documented [2][4][6] | Indirect | Weak |
| Clear policy levers today | Yes: new treaty rules and repair laws [10][11] | Partial | Partial |

Problem 1.1 has the largest measured scale, the clearest link to the anchor value, and concrete policy levers that came into force in 2025 and 2026. It is the subject of Part 2.

## Part 2. In depth: repair, e-waste flows and labour exploitation

### 2.1 Scale

**Global.**
- 62 million tonnes of e-waste generated in 2022, an average of 7.8 kg per person, up 82% from 2010 [1][12].
- Only 22.3% documented as formally collected and recycled; this rate is projected to fall to about 20% by 2030 as generation grows towards 82 million tonnes [1].
- US$62 billion worth of recoverable natural resources unaccounted for in 2022 [1].
- Bringing collection and recycling to 60% by 2030 would produce benefits exceeding costs by more than US$38 billion [1].

**Cross-border flows (2019 data, the latest global estimate).**
- 5.1 million tonnes crossed borders, just under 10% of all e-waste generated that year [3].
- 1.8 million tonnes moved in a controlled way; 3.3 million tonnes moved uncontrolled, as used equipment or e-waste, mostly from high-income to middle- and low-income countries [3].
- As much as one third of imported "used" appliances can be broken on arrival [3].
- Only 2 to 17 thousand tonnes of illegal e-waste were estimated to be seized from the European Union in 2019, a tiny fraction of uncontrolled flows [3].

**Africa as a receiving region.**
- Africa generated 3.5 billion kg of e-waste in 2022 (2.5 kg per person), of which only 0.7% was documented as formally collected and recycled [12].
- In 2019 the continent imported an estimated 546 million kg of e-waste, of which 527 million kg moved uncontrolled [12].
- Only 11 of 54 African countries analysed had a national e-waste policy, law or regulation [12].

**People and health.**
- As many as 18 million children and adolescents and 12.9 million women may be at risk from adverse health outcomes linked to e-waste recycling [4].
- In 2020 the ILO estimated that 16.5 million children were working in the industrial sector, of which waste processing is a subsector; how many work in e-waste specifically is unknown [2].
- A systematic review linked e-waste exposure with changes in thyroid function, reduced lung function in children, and adverse birth outcomes including stillbirths, premature births and lower birthweight [13].
- Informal recycling can release up to 1,000 different chemical substances, including neurotoxicants such as lead [2].

**The wider labour picture.**
- About 138 million children were in child labour worldwide according to the ILO and UNICEF's 2024 estimates [14].
- An estimated 50 million people were living in modern slavery in 2021, 28 million of them in forced labour [15].

These two global figures are context, not e-waste figures. No verified source in this study measures how many people in informal e-waste work are in forced labour or were trafficked.

### 2.2 Root causes

1. **Short product lives and limited repair.** The Global E-waste Monitor names limited repair options, shorter product life cycles and design shortcomings among the drivers of the widening gap between e-waste generated and recycled [1]. Repair has historically been hindered by restricted access to parts, tools and information [11][16].
2. **Exporting is cheaper than treating at home.** Exporting even to distant countries can cost less than managing e-waste domestically [3].
3. **The "used equipment" loophole.** There are no global registries or reporting duties for used equipment and no international regime dealing with its shipment, so e-waste is often declared as used goods [12]. Studies at ports have classed between one third and 77% of inspected "used" items as e-waste [12].
4. **Weak enforcement and reporting.** Inappropriate donation of computers from richer to poorer countries adds to the flow [20]. In 2022 only 91 of 187 parties to the Basel Convention submitted a national report [12]. International treaties leave loopholes that give receiving countries few incentives or resources to protect workers [5].
5. **Real demand and real livelihoods.** Used devices are in demand in receiving countries because they are cheaper than new ones, and informal recycling is an important income source for individuals and communities [2][17]. Any solution that ignores this will fail or cause harm.
6. **Informality and poverty.** Most informal workers lack protective equipment, legal status and bargaining power; women and children are frequently among them [5][20].

### 2.3 Recent policy changes (the baseline)

- **Basel Convention e-waste amendments (in force 1 January 2025).** All cross-border movements of e-waste, hazardous or not, now require the prior informed consent of the importing country, for all parties except Canada, Israel and New Zealand [10][19]. This closes the old gap for non-hazardous e-waste, but equipment declared as **used goods for reuse** is still not waste under these rules, so the used-equipment loophole remains.
- **EU Directive (EU) 2024/1799 on repair of goods (applies from 31 July 2026).** Manufacturers of listed products, such as fridges and smartphones, must repair them for a reasonable price and time; they may not use contract terms, hardware or software techniques that impede repair unless objectively justified; and they must sell spare parts at reasonable prices [11].

### 2.4 Proposed solution: "repair first, trace, formalise"

The package has four parts. Each addresses a root cause above.

**A. Repair first (root cause 1).**
- Extend repair duties of the kind in the EU directive to more product categories and to other large exporting markets [11][16].
- Require that parts, manuals and diagnostic tools be available on fair terms to **independent repairers in importing countries**, not only in the market of sale. Longer device life in the receiving country is the most direct way to delay a device becoming e-waste there.
- Ban software locks that refuse genuine replacement parts unless a specific, published safety or security reason applies.

**B. Honest exports (root causes 2 and 3).**
- Treat any device exported as "used" as e-waste unless it carries a **functionality test certificate** and protective packaging. The Global E-waste Monitor's own port studies show why: a third or more of inspected "used" items were actually waste [12].
- Publish anonymised shipment data so that receiving countries and researchers can check flows.
- Focus penalties on exporters and brokers who misdeclare waste, not on workers at the receiving end.

**C. Formalise without displacing (root causes 5 and 6).**
- Use producer responsibility fees (producers pay for end-of-life handling) to fund collection and safe pre-processing **in the receiving countries** where the devices actually end up, not only where they were first sold. The Global E-waste Monitor calls for improving and formalising informal-sector work rather than replacing it [12].
- Pay informal collectors and dismantlers to join cooperatives that receive protective equipment, safe tools, health checks and a guaranteed price for components, building on the ILO's analysis of cooperatives in e-waste [18].
- Ship the most hazardous steps (circuit boards, batteries, cathode-ray glass) to licensed facilities rather than doing them by open burning or acid baths [2].

**D. Child and forced labour safeguards (anchor value).**
- Make cooperative membership and price guarantees conditional on no workers under the legal working age and no confiscated documents, debts tied to work, or restricted movement, which are common markers of forced labour.
- Pair this with school stipends or family income support so that removing children from the sites does not push households deeper into poverty or move children to more hidden work.
- Give workers a confidential complaint channel that does not report them to immigration authorities.
- Extend the same due diligence upstream to battery minerals, where child labour in artisanal mining has been documented [6].

**How it would be measured.** Formal collection rate in receiving countries [12]; share of inspected "used" shipments found to be waste [12]; number of children identified at e-waste sites over time; blood lead levels in children near sites, following WHO's call to monitor communities [2]; device repair rates and lifespans where repair duties apply.

### 2.5 Ethical risks and trade-offs

1. **Cutting off affordable technology.** Stricter export rules may raise the price of second-hand phones and computers that many people in receiving countries rely on [17]. Mitigation: certify and allow working devices rather than banning used exports.
2. **Displacing the poorest workers.** Formalisation can hand the valuable work to licensed firms and leave informal workers with nothing. Mitigation: pay workers to join, guarantee prices, and let cooperatives own the licences [18].
3. **Pushing child labour out of sight.** Raids and bans can move children to less visible and more dangerous work. Mitigation: pair enforcement with income support and schooling, and measure outcomes, not only removals.
4. **Paternalism.** Rules written in exporting countries can override the choices of receiving countries. Mitigation: receiving countries set the standards for their own cooperatives and decide how producer fees are spent; the Basel consent procedure already places that decision with them [10].
5. **Cost passed to consumers.** Producer fees and repair duties may raise prices of new devices. This is arguably fair, since it moves the true cost of disposal from poor communities to the point of purchase, but it is a real trade-off.
6. **Security versus repairability.** Some part-locking is justified by anti-theft or safety concerns. Mitigation: allow locks only with a published, specific justification, as the EU directive's "legitimate and objective factors" test already requires [11].
7. **Data and surveillance.** Shipment tracing and worker registries can be misused against undocumented workers. Mitigation: collect only what is needed, anonymise published data, and never share worker records with immigration enforcement.
8. **Overclaiming the trafficking link.** Framing all informal recyclers as trafficking victims could stigmatise workers and misdirect resources. This study keeps the claim to what the evidence supports: documented child labour and hazardous exposure, with forced labour as a risk to be measured.

### 2.6 Open questions

1. How much informal e-waste labour involves forced labour or trafficking, as distinct from child labour driven by poverty? No verified global estimate exists [2].
2. Will the 2025 Basel amendments reduce uncontrolled flows, or shift them further into the "used equipment" category? The latest global flow estimate is from 2019 [3].
3. Do repair laws in one market extend device lives in the markets where those devices are later resold?
4. Which cooperative models have actually reduced child labour and exposure, and which have been captured by intermediaries?
5. How should producer responsibility fees follow a device across borders when it is resold several times?
6. Most health evidence in the systematic review came from one region of China [13]; how well does it transfer to West Africa and South Asia?
