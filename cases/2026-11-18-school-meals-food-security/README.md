# One budget line, two hungers: home-grown school meals as a way to move money into food security for children and smallholder farmers

Figures from different reports use different years and methods, so they should not be added together. Calculations marked "(this study's own calculation)" are simple arithmetic on cited figures. Proposals marked "(this study's own proposal)" are this study's suggestions, not positions of any cited author, government or institution. Anything that could not be confirmed is marked "unverified" or left out.*

*Revisions (18 November 2026).* Revised after an independent review. The changes correct facts, fill gaps and tighten wording. The review is not named here.

*Overlap with other cases in this collection.* The case on financing food security covers the wider menu of money channels, including school-meal debt swaps and the School Meals Coalition's headline numbers. The case on famine prevention covers early warning, anticipatory cash and reserves. This case looks only at one channel in depth: public school-meal budgets that buy food from local smallholders, and how that money is raised, governed and handed from aid agencies to national governments.

## The ethical question

Most public money for school meals is spent once and counted once, as an education or nutrition outlay. "Home-grown" school feeding tries to make the same money work twice. The meal feeds a child, and the purchase of the food gives local farmers a predictable buyer (Bundy et al., 2018; Drake et al., 2017). Brazil wrote this into law, and the African Union's heads of state endorsed the idea in 2016 (Brazil, 2025; African Union, 2016).

So the ethical question is: **when a government has one budget line, is it right to ask it to serve both hungry children and poor farmers, and who should pay where the government cannot?**

Three narrower questions follow:

1. If buying locally costs more, or delivers less varied food, whose interest comes first, the child's or the farmer's?
2. How quickly should aid agencies hand programmes to governments, when handing over too early can mean children lose meals?
3. Where national budgets are too small, which outside money (aid, debt swaps, levies) can fill the gap without taking decisions away from the country?

## Scale

**Children reached.** WFP's *State of School Feeding Worldwide 2024* estimates that about **466 million** children in pre-primary, primary and secondary school received school meals, up from 418 million in the 2022 edition and 388 million in the 2020 edition (WFP, 2024a).

**Money.** Estimated global spending in the 2024 edition ranged from **US$65.6 billion to US$84 billion**. US$65.6 billion is reported expenditure from 116 countries; US$84 billion adds estimates for other countries with programmes but no reported budgets (WFP, 2024a). The 2022 edition's global baseline was US$48 billion, but WFP warns that the 2024 scenarios "cannot be directly compared to those in the previous edition as they are based on different samples of countries" (WFP, 2024a). About **99 percent** of the 2024 total comes from national budgets (WFP, 2024a). International support was **US$445 million** in 2024, up from US$364 million in 2022 (WFP, 2024a). That is less than 1 percent of even the lower US$65.6 billion figure (this study's own calculation).

**Where the gap is.** About half of primary-school children worldwide get a school meal. Coverage is **27 percent in low-income countries**, 42 percent in lower-middle-income, 58 percent in upper-middle-income and 79 percent in high-income countries (WFP, 2024a). In low-income countries, international donors are still the main funders. Across the same sample of low-income countries, the domestic share stayed at about **34 percent** between 2022 and 2024 (WFP, 2024a). In 2020, WFP's own strategy put external funding at about 83 percent of programme costs in low-income countries (WFP, 2020).

**Value to families and jobs.** For families, a school meal is worth about **10 percent** of household income, more for families with several children (Bundy et al., 2018). WFP's survey of 76 countries counted about 2.25 million cooks employed directly, or 1,591 per 100,000 children fed. On that ratio WFP estimates about 7.4 million cooks worldwide (WFP, 2024a).

**Selected national programmes.**

- **India (PM-POSHAN, formerly the Mid-Day Meal Scheme):** one hot cooked meal for about **118 million children (11.80 crore) in 1.12 million schools (11.20 lakh)**. The five-year budget for 2021-22 to 2025-26 was ₹1,30,794.90 crore, including about ₹45,000 crore of central foodgrain costs. The approval also encouraged farmer producer organisations, women's self-help groups and locally grown traditional foods (Government of India, 2021).
- **Brazil (PNAE):** a law from 2009 (Law 11.947) required that at least 30 percent of federal school-food transfers be spent buying directly from family farmers. Law 15.226 of 30 September 2025 raised this floor to **45 percent from 1 January 2026**. It gives priority to land-reform settlements, Indigenous and quilombola communities, and formal and informal women's groups (Brazil, 2025; Câmara dos Deputados, 2025).
- **Kenya:** WFP and the education ministry ran school meals jointly from the 1980s. In 2009 the government launched a Home Grown School Meals Programme that sends cash from the treasury to schools to buy from local smallholders and suppliers. The handover from WFP was completed in 2018, covering **1.6 million children**. The 2024/25 allocation was **3 billion** shillings against an estimated requirement of **14 billion** (Kamau et al., 2024; WFP, 2018).
- **Rwanda:** coverage grew from **874,244 learners in 2021 to more than 4.5 million**, which is universal coverage of public pre-primary, primary and secondary schools. Government funding grew from **RWF 8 billion in 2021 to RWF 142 billion in 2026**, with a dedicated budget line and a school-feeding financing strategy (School Meals Coalition, n.d.-b). That 2026 budget is about RWF 31,500 per learner per year (this study's own calculation).
- **Ghana:** the Ghana School Feeding Programme began in 2005 with about 1,900 pupils and now reaches more than four million. It is designed as a home-grown model, with about 80 percent of the budget meant for food from local sources. The feeding grant is reported as GH¢2.00 per pupil per day, paid to caterers in arrears (Addo et al., 2025; **unverified** here, because that case study was not opened for this revision).

## Root causes: why the money does not reach the children and farmers who need it most

1. **The countries with the most hungry children have the least fiscal space.** Coverage rises with national income and is lowest where need is highest (WFP, 2024a). WFP's strategy calls low fiscal capacity "the first and most important challenge" to scaling up and handing over programmes (WFP, 2020). The debt pressures on these budgets are covered in this collection's debt-relief case.
2. **School meals are often charged to the education budget alone.** WFP notes that countries that handed over successfully, such as Kenya, Bhutan and El Salvador, treated school feeding as serving several sectors and paid for it from the national budget rather than the education budget alone (WFP, 2020).
3. **Payment delays push costs onto the weakest link.** In Ghana, caterers, who are mostly local women, must pay for food upfront and are reimbursed late, often borrowing at higher cost (Addo et al., 2025). In a randomised trial in Ghana, delayed disbursements meant caterers' actual food spending per pupil was about **50 percent** of the budgeted amount (Gelli et al., 2021).
4. **Procurement rules are built for large suppliers.** Brazilian food-service managers name farmers' limited output, lack of technical assistance, seasonality and bureaucratic procurement as the main obstacles (Soares et al., 2021). An earlier review of PNAE found farmers struggling to meet supply and technical requirements (Sidaner et al., 2013).
5. **The farm side is rarely measured.** A World Bank review notes that the goal of building food systems that help smallholders "has yet to be extensively studied", and that costs are poorly understood (Alderman et al., 2024).

## What has worked

**Benefits to children.** These are the best-evidenced part.

- A systematic review of 57 studies, with a meta-analysis of 19, in low- and middle-income countries found small but significant gains over 12 months: **0.32 cm in height, 0.58 kg in weight, and 2.6 percentage points more school days attended** (Wang et al., 2021). The earlier Cochrane review also found small average benefits (Kristjansson et al., 2007).
- In Ghana's national programme, a cluster-randomised trial found moderate gains in maths and literacy after two years, with larger gains for girls and disadvantaged children (Aurino et al., 2023). The same programme's nutrition trial found **no effect on height-for-age or BMI-for-age across all children aged 5–15** (intention-to-treat). Per-protocol subgroup analysis found height-for-age gains of 0.12–0.22 SD for children aged 5–8, for girls, and for 5–8-year-olds below the poverty line (Gelli et al., 2019).
- India's programme has some of the largest-scale evidence:
  - Daily protein deficiency among participants fell by 100 percent, calorie deficiency by almost 30 percent and iron deficiency by nearly 10 percent, at about 3 US cents per child per school day (nominal, early 2000s Indian prices) (Afridi, 2010).
  - The meals protected children's growth during drought in Andhra Pradesh (Singh, Park & Dercon, 2014).
  - The published article finds that prolonged exposure to the meals had a robust positive effect on learning. The working-paper version reports gains of about 10–20 percent for children with up to five years of exposure (Chakraborty & Jayaraman, 2019).
  - Mothers' full exposure as schoolgirls was associated with taller children (+0.40 SD height-for-age), with stronger associations in poorer groups (Chakrabarti et al., 2021).
- The standard reading of all this is that school meals are best seen as a **transfer and safety net** that also supports learning, rather than mainly a nutrition intervention (Alderman & Bundy, 2012).

**Benefits to farmers.** These are plausible but weakly proven.

- Modelled returns across 14 countries put local agricultural gains at about **US$23 billion a year**, on programmes costing US$11 billion. The overall benefit–cost ratios are 7 to 35 (Verguet et al., 2020). These are models, not measured effects.
- The only large randomised test of home-grown feeding found mixed results. In Ghana, home-grown feeding may have raised the share of food bought directly from smallholders, but traders stayed the main channel. The trial found **no strong evidence of effects on smallholders' market structure or farm, non-farm and household income** (Gelli et al., 2021).
- A randomised trial of home-grown feeding was also designed in Mali (Masset & Gelli, 2013).
- In Brazil, a national survey of 5,184 municipalities found that **78.5 percent bought from family farms**, but **half did not spend the legal minimum** (Machado et al., 2018). An Ipea study of 2013–2019 analyses which municipal characteristics predict compliance (Silva & Ciríaco, 2024). A participatory study in Mato Grosso found that land-reform farmers selling to PNAE and the Food Procurement Programme gained market stability, but also faced trade-offs in autonomy (Wittman & Blesh, 2017).

**Financing and ownership.** This is where the strongest progress is.

- **National budgets carry the load.** About 99 percent of global spending is domestic (WFP, 2024a). India splits costs between the centre and the states, and the centre also pays for grain (Government of India, 2021). Rwanda created a dedicated budget line (School Meals Coalition, n.d.-b). Brazil earmarks a share of federal transfers by law (Brazil, 2025).
- **Planned handover from WFP.** WFP's 2020–2030 strategy sorts countries into three groups:
  - crisis settings, where WFP will run programmes for about 35 million children in 30 countries;
  - stable low- and lower-middle-income countries, where it aims to **hand over programmes in 20 countries by 2030**;
  - middle-income countries, where it supports national programmes that have already been handed over.
  Kenya's 2018 handover is an example of the second group (WFP, 2020; WFP, 2018).
- **South–South learning.** WFP set up its Centre of Excellence against Hunger with the Government of Brazil in 2011, and a second centre opened in Côte d'Ivoire in 2019 (WFP, 2020). The African Union Assembly recognised "the immense value of Home Grown School Feeding" in boosting local income and entrepreneurship. It set 1 March as the African Day of School Feeding from 2016, and asked for an expert study supported by WFP and the Centre of Excellence (African Union, 2016).
- **The School Meals Coalition.** Launched by 46 countries at the UN Food Systems Summit in September 2021, the coalition was led first by Finland and France; Brazil became the third co-chair in 2023 (WFP, 2024b). It now lists **113 member countries and 6 regional bodies**, including the African Union, ECOWAS and SADC (School Meals Coalition, n.d.-a). Its task force includes the African Union, Guatemala, Honduras, Kenya, Rwanda and Senegal alongside high-income members (WFP, 2024b). This makes it one of the few food initiatives in which Global South governments help set the agenda.
- **Evidence and financing arms.** The Research Consortium for School Health and Nutrition launched in May 2021. It is hosted by the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine and organised as communities of practice that include Global South academics (LSHTM, n.d.; LSHTM, 2021). Its country case studies are cited above. The Sustainable Financing Initiative launched in July 2022 to help low- and lower-middle-income countries find multi-year financing, including innovative mechanisms (Sustainable Financing Initiative, 2023). Debt-for-school-meals swaps, studied for the initiative by Hurley and Martin (2024), are discussed in this collection's financing case and are not repeated here.

## Ethical tensions

1. **Child first or farmer first?** Ghana shows a programme can help children while its farm goals "remain aspirational" (Gelli et al., 2021). If local buying raises costs or narrows menus, the child's nutrition should come first, and the farmer goal should be pursued in ways that do not cut meal quality.
2. **Who carries the financing risk?** Late payment turns caterers and small suppliers, often women, into unpaid lenders to the state (Addo et al., 2025; Gelli et al., 2021). A programme meant to reduce poverty should not shift the cost of delayed budgets onto poor women.
3. **Universal or targeted, free or co-paid?** Kenya's centralised kitchens ask parents to pay about half the cost of a meal (Kamau et al., 2024). Co-payment can stretch budgets, but it risks excluding the poorest children, the ones the meals help most (Wang et al., 2021; Gelli et al., 2019).
4. **Speed of handover versus continuity.** Ownership is good, but low-income countries still fund only about a third of their programmes themselves (WFP, 2024a). Kenya's 2024/25 allocation was well below the estimated need (Kamau et al., 2024). A handover that leaves a gap is paid for by children who lose meals.
5. **Sovereignty versus outside money.** Debt swaps, levies and donor co-financing bring conditions. Who decides the menu, the suppliers and the share bought locally should stay with national and local governments, and school councils such as Brazil's (Sidaner et al., 2013).
6. **Quotas versus capacity.** A legal floor works only where farmers can supply it. In Brazil, half of municipalities missed the 30 percent floor (Machado et al., 2018). A higher floor, now 45 percent, will test supply further (Brazil, 2025).

## Original proposals

All proposals in this section are **this study's own proposals**. They are not positions of any cited author, government or institution, and none has been tested.

1. **A local-purchase floor that rises with supply, plus a payment clock.** Countries would set a legal minimum share of school-food money for smallholders and their cooperatives, Brazil-style. It would start at a level local supply can meet and rise on a published schedule. It would always come with:
   - a statutory maximum payment delay, for example 30 days, for caterers and farmers;
   - an advance tranche at the start of each term.

   This answers the problem the Ghana trial and case study found, where delays halved real food spending (Gelli et al., 2021; Addo et al., 2025).
2. **Declining-share transition compacts.** Donors and governments would sign a multi-year compact in which outside co-financing falls on a fixed, published schedule as the domestic share rises. Each step would require the government to show it has a budget line and a financing strategy, as Rwanda has (School Meals Coalition, n.d.-b). This is in the spirit of WFP's planned handovers but adds a public step-down timetable (WFP, 2020).
3. **Protect the school-meal line in fiscal adjustment.** When a country restructures its debt or enters an IMF programme, it and its creditors would treat school meals as protected social spending with a minimum floor. The reason is that home-grown meals support children and the rural economy at the same time (Bundy et al., 2018; Verguet et al., 2020).
4. **A South–South peer review of procurement.** Hosted by the African Union and regional bodies already in the School Meals Coalition, with Brazil's and Côte d'Ivoire's centres of excellence, India and other Asian and Latin American members. Member countries would publish:
   - their local-purchase share;
   - their payment delays;
   - their coverage of the poorest districts.

   They would review each other every two years. This builds on the AU's 2016 call for member states to learn from each other (African Union, 2016).
5. **Forward contracts announced before planting.** School-meal agencies would tell farmer cooperatives before the season what volumes they will buy and at what minimum price. This would give farmers a reason to invest, which is the mechanism home-grown feeding relies on and the Ghana trial did not find (Gelli et al., 2021).
6. **Measure the farm side, not just the plate.** Every programme claiming home-grown status would track a few farm indicators, such as the share bought direct from smallholders, prices paid and payment times. This would help close the evidence gap the World Bank review identified (Alderman et al., 2024).
7. **Earmarked levies as a last resort, not a first choice.** Some have suggested funding school meals through solidarity levies. This study **found no rigorous evaluation of a levy earmarked for school meals**, so the idea is **unverified**. If tried, a levy should top up, not replace, national budget lines, and be governed jointly by contributing and receiving countries.

## Risks

- **The farm benefit may not appear.** The best trial found no clear farm-income effect (Gelli et al., 2021). Local-purchase rules could add cost without helping poor farmers if traders capture the demand.
- **Quotas can be gamed or simply missed.** Half of Brazilian municipalities missed the earlier floor (Machado et al., 2018). Compliance depends on local capacity (Silva & Ciríaco, 2024).
- **Budget cuts in hard years.** Kenya's allocation fell from about 5.2 billion to 3 billion shillings in one year (Kamau et al., 2024). Rising food prices and droughts raise costs just as revenue falls.
- **Diet quality.** The nutrition goals of school meals now include healthy diets and lower risk of non-communicable diseases (Alderman et al., 2024). Cheap, filling but low-diversity menus, or processed foods, could undercut them.
- **Elite capture and corruption.** Caterer selection in Ghana has sometimes changed with local leadership (Addo et al., 2025). India made social audits of its programme mandatory in every district (Government of India, 2021). Such audits matter wherever large public contracts go to local suppliers.
- **Evidence limits.** Most child-benefit effects are modest on average and concentrated among the poorest (Kristjansson et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2021). Ghana's large randomised nutrition trial found no effect on height-for-age or BMI-for-age in the full 5–15 age group; the reported gains were in per-protocol subgroups (Gelli et al., 2019). The high benefit–cost ratios are modelled (Verguet et al., 2020).

## Recommendation

School meals are one of the few food-security channels where the money already exists at scale, about US$65.6–84 billion a year in the 2024 estimate and almost all of it domestic (WFP, 2024a). The ethical priority is not to invent new money first. It is to **make the money already flowing do more, and reach the low-income countries where coverage is only 27 percent.**

On the evidence reviewed:

1. Governments should treat school meals as a **multi-sector safety net**, funded from the national budget rather than the education budget alone (WFP, 2020; Alderman & Bundy, 2012).
2. They should **buy locally where local supply can meet quality and price**, with Brazil-style legal floors that rise with capacity. Payment rules should protect caterers and farmers from late payment (Brazil, 2025; Gelli et al., 2021).
3. Outside partners, including WFP, donors, development banks and creditors, should fund low-income countries' gaps through **compacts that hand over to national budgets on a published schedule**. The farm side of home-grown programmes should be measured before strong claims are made for it (WFP, 2020; Alderman et al., 2024).

Cooperation should rest where the momentum already is. That means a coalition where African, Latin American and Asian governments share the lead, the African Union's 2016 commitment, and South–South centres in Brasília and Abidjan (School Meals Coalition, n.d.-a; African Union, 2016; WFP, 2020). Cooperation should not depend mainly on traditional donors.

### Notes on verification

- Every DOI was checked against the Crossref registry or resolved through doi.org, and other links were opened, in November 2026. The LSHTM "About us" page blocked automated fetching; its contents were confirmed through search results and the School Meals Coalition's own page on the consortium.
- Programme facts were taken from primary sources, not secondary summaries:
  - Brazil: the text of Law 15.226/2025 and the Chamber of Deputies notice;
  - India: the 2021 Cabinet release;
  - the African Union: the 2016 Assembly decision;
  - Kenya and Ghana: the Research Consortium case studies;
  - Rwanda: the School Meals Coalition member page.
- The Kenya case study gives the 2022/23–2024/25 allocations without a currency label. They are read as Kenyan shillings from context, since the same paper gives the 2023/24 budget as about US$35 million, which is roughly consistent with the 5.2 billion figure in shillings at the stated exchange rate. This reading is the study's own.
- The Ghana case study gives both a GH¢2.00 per-pupil grant and a higher average meal cost. Only the grant is used here, and the grant figure itself is **unverified** in this revision (the case study was not opened).
- The Chakraborty & Jayaraman published abstract reports a "robust positive effect" of prolonged exposure and does not repeat the "10–20 percent" figure, which is retained only as a working-paper result.
- Cost-per-child figures in this case are not on a common price basis. Afridi's 3 US cents is nominal and dates from the early-2000s Indian rollout. The Ghana grant is a 2025 secondary report and unverified here. No current WFP per-child cost range from the 2024 edition is used, because those averages could not be confirmed live for this revision. These figures should not be compared with one another.
- The following are **unverified** and are not relied on:
  - any evaluation of levies earmarked for school meals;
  - Brazil's annual PNAE budget figure, which was found only in a secondary network release;
  - the specific per-meal cost split in Rwanda's 2021 guidelines, which was seen only in search summaries.
