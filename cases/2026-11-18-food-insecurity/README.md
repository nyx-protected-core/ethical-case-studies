# Food insecurity: who owes what to the hungry?

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. Figures come from the sources listed at the end and were checked in November 2026. Hunger estimates are modelled ranges, not head counts. Numbers from different reports use different methods and country coverage, so they should not be added together. Proposals marked "original" are this study's own suggestions, not positions of any cited author or institution.*

## The ethical question

The world grows enough calories to feed everyone, yet hundreds of millions of people go hungry every year. Hunger is therefore less a question of whether food exists than of who can get it, when, and at what price.

The ethical question is: **when people cannot get enough safe, nutritious food, what do governments, trading partners, humanitarian agencies, companies and better-off households owe them, and which actions actually discharge that duty?**

Three narrower questions follow:

1. Is it fair for a country to protect its own consumers by restricting food exports when that raises prices for poorer importing countries?
2. Should help be given as food, vouchers, or cash, and who decides?
3. When conflict is the main driver of famine, what does the rest of the world owe the people trapped inside it?

## Scale of the problem

**Chronic hunger.** The 2026 edition of *The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World* (SOFI), produced jointly by FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP and WHO, estimates that about 645 million people faced hunger in 2025, or 7.8 percent of the world's population, within a range of 608 to 696 million. That is about 14 million fewer than in 2024 and 43 million fewer than in 2022, but still above the level before the COVID-19 pandemic (FAO et al., 2026).

- **Africa now has the largest number of hungry people of any region**: 309 million in 2025, or 20.0 percent of its population, compared with 292 million (6.0 percent) in Asia and 32 million (4.8 percent) in Latin America and the Caribbean (FAO et al., 2026).
- **About 2.1 billion people** (25.8 percent of the world) were moderately or severely food insecure in 2025, and 778.6 million of them were severely food insecure (FAO et al., 2026).
- **About 2.7 billion people** could not afford a healthy diet (FAO et al., 2026).
- **150.2 million children under five** (23.2 percent) were stunted in 2024, down from 26.4 percent in 2012 but well short of the 2030 target (FAO et al., 2026).

**The health burden is still large.** A 2013 *Lancet* review estimated that stunting, wasting and micronutrient deficiencies underlie nearly 3.1 million child deaths a year (Black et al., 2013).

**Acute crises.** The 2026 *Global Report on Food Crises* (GRFC) found 266 million people in 47 countries and territories facing high levels of acute food insecurity in 2025, or 22.9 percent of the population analysed. The report stresses that the lower total than in 2024 mainly reflects fewer countries being covered, not an improvement. Six countries or territories had people in Catastrophe (IPC/CH Phase 5), about 1.4 million people, more than nine times the 2016 figure. Famine was identified in Gaza Governorate and parts of Sudan in 2025, the first time the report has recorded famine confirmed in two separate contexts in the same year. 35.5 million children were acutely malnourished (Global Network Against Food Crises & FSIN, 2026).

**Money is moving the wrong way.** The same report warns that funding for food-crisis response and for food security and nutrition has fallen back to levels last seen nearly a decade ago, and that fewer countries can now produce reliable food security data (Global Network Against Food Crises & FSIN, 2026). The 2024 SOFI report likewise named under-financing, and the lack of a shared definition of financing for food security and nutrition, as a core obstacle (FAO et al., 2024). The 2025 edition linked persistent food-price inflation to rising food insecurity and child malnutrition, especially among low-income households, women and rural communities, and found the world still off track for the hunger and malnutrition goals (FAO et al., 2025).

**Waste alongside want.** In 2022, an estimated 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted at retail, food-service and household level, 132 kg per person and about 19 percent of the food available to consumers. Households accounted for 60 percent. A further 13 percent of food is lost in supply chains before reaching retail. Food loss and waste together generate an estimated 8 to 10 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions (UNEP, 2024).

## Root causes

### Hunger is mostly a failure of access, not supply

Amartya Sen's study of famines showed that mass starvation has occurred when food was available, because particular groups lost their "entitlement" to it: their wages collapsed, their crops failed, or prices rose faster than their incomes (Sen, 1981). Christopher Barrett argues that food security has to be understood across availability, access, use and stability, and that measuring it well is itself a policy challenge (Barrett, 2010). Recent UN reports point the same way: adding more food to world markets does little for people who cannot afford it, and high food-price inflation hits low-income households first (FAO et al., 2025). The UN hunger and food-insecurity figures themselves are modelled indicators, and their methods are a subject of academic debate (Barrett, 2010; FAO et al., 2025).

### Conflict

Conflict is the largest single driver of acute hunger. In 2025 it was the primary driver in 19 countries or territories covering 147.4 million acutely food-insecure people, and it drove almost every case of Catastrophe (Global Network Against Food Crises & FSIN, 2026). The causal links run both ways: war destroys farms, markets and aid access, and food insecurity can feed grievances and recruitment. Researchers caution that untangling cause and effect is hard and that data are weak in exactly the places where it matters most (Martin-Shields & Stojetz, 2019). The UN Security Council condemned the starvation of civilians as a method of warfare, and the unlawful denial of humanitarian access, in Resolution 2417 (United Nations Security Council, 2018).

### Price spikes and the export-restriction trap

Derek Headey and Shenggen Fan, reviewing standard explanations of the 2007–08 food price crisis, find that rising oil prices, the depreciation of the US dollar, demand for biofuels, and some commodity-specific factors hold up best against the evidence. For rice in particular, they find export restrictions a compelling explanation, because several important exporters imposed them and because relatively little rice is traded internationally (Headey & Fan, 2008). Separately, Brian Wright shows that grain price spikes tend to occur when stocks are low relative to use, leaving the market with little buffer against small shocks (Wright, 2011).

Export restrictions turn a supply worry into a collective-action problem. Each exporting government that limits exports protects its own consumers, but when large exporters do it at once they push world prices higher and more volatile. Will Martin and Kym Anderson estimate that such price-insulating behaviour explains about 45 percent of the rise in world rice prices in 2006–08 and about 30 percent of the rise in wheat prices, and that it largely fails to stabilise domestic prices on average (Martin & Anderson, 2012).

High food prices also cause political instability. Using natural disasters to identify the effect, Marc Bellemare finds that increases in food price levels caused more social unrest between 1990 and 2011, while price volatility on its own did not (Bellemare, 2015).

### Climate

Droughts and extreme heat cut national cereal production by about 9 to 10 percent in the years they struck, based on disasters recorded between 1964 and 2007 (Lesk et al., 2016). Human-caused climate change has already reduced global agricultural productivity growth by about 21 percent since 1961, with larger losses in warmer regions such as Africa and Latin America (Ortiz-Bobea et al., 2021). Climate stress therefore falls hardest on the regions where hunger is already rising.

### Income shocks

Economy-wide shocks hit food access quickly. Modelling at IFPRI projected that the COVID-19 recession would push almost 150 million people into extreme poverty and food insecurity, with the loss of income, not a shortage of food, as the main channel (Laborde et al., 2021).

## What has worked

**Cash transfers.** In a randomised trial in rural Kenya, large unconditional cash transfers raised household monthly consumption from US$158 to US$193 (in purchasing-power terms) nine months after the transfers began, and also improved psychological well-being. Monthly payments improved food security more than a lump sum did, while lump sums were more often spent on durable goods (Haushofer & Shapiro, 2016). A larger trial then gave about US$1,000 to more than 10,500 households across 653 villages. It found gains for non-recipient households and firms, little price inflation, and a local multiplier of about 2.5 (Egger et al., 2022). Those Kenyan trials are among the strongest pieces of evidence, but they come from particular market settings. How long the gains last, and whether inflation stays low in thinner or more isolated markets, is still debated; later long-run follow-ups were not reviewed for this study.

A review of 165 studies of cash transfers in low- and middle-income countries found that they mostly moved outcomes in the intended direction, with limited evidence that they reduce adult work effort (Bastagli et al., 2018).

**Social protection and diet quality.** A meta-analysis of social-protection programmes finds that the average programme raises the value of food consumed by about 13 percent and calorie acquisition by about 8 percent. Spending rises faster than calories because households shift toward higher-quality foods. The same programmes also raise livestock, productive assets and savings (Hidrobo, Hoddinott, Kumar, & Olivier, 2018). Reviews that pool across programme designs do not, however, settle whether cash or in-kind help is best in a given setting (Bastagli et al., 2018; Hidrobo, Hoddinott, Kumar, & Olivier, 2018).

**Choosing the form of help with evidence, not habit.** A randomised comparison in northern Ecuador found that food, vouchers and cash all improved diets. Food raised calories the most and vouchers raised dietary diversity the most, but delivering food cost far more per transfer than delivering cash or vouchers (Hidrobo, Hoddinott, Peterman, Margolies, & Moreira, 2014). A World Bank review of humanitarian programmes reached a similar conclusion: cash is often cheaper to deliver and usually performs as well as in-kind aid, but the right choice depends on whether local markets work (Gentilini, 2016; Alderman et al., 2017).

**"Graduation" programmes for the very poorest.** Across six countries (Ethiopia, Ghana, Honduras, India, Pakistan and Peru), a package of a productive asset, temporary consumption support, training, coaching and savings produced statistically significant, cost-effective gains in consumption, assets, food security and psychosocial status a year after the programme ended (Banerjee et al., 2015). Cost-effectiveness varied across sites.

**Safety nets joined to farm support.** Ethiopia's Productive Safety Net Programme had little average effect in its early years because many households received far less than the intended transfer. Households that did receive substantial transfers, and especially those that also got agricultural support, were more likely to be food secure and to invest in production (Gilligan et al., 2009). Design and delivery matter as much as the idea.

**Proven nutrition interventions.** A *Lancet* review estimated that scaling ten proven nutrition interventions to 90 percent coverage in 34 high-burden countries could avert about 15 percent of deaths in children under five and about a fifth of existing stunting, at an estimated extra Int$9.6 billion a year. Larger gains would need "nutrition-sensitive" measures as well, such as social protection and agriculture (Bhutta et al., 2013).

**School feeding has real but modest effects, concentrated among the most disadvantaged children.** A Cochrane review of 18 studies found small weight gains in lower-income countries (for example 0.39 kg over about 19 months in randomised trials), about 4 to 6 more days of school attendance a year, and some maths gains. Its conclusion is that school meals "may have some small benefits" (Kristjansson et al., 2007). Ghana's national programme had no overall effect on height-for-age in a cluster randomised trial, but it did improve height-for-age for children aged 5–8, for girls, and for children in poor households, with an effect of 0.22 standard deviations in the poorest group (Gelli et al., 2019). The case for school meals often rests on these subgroup, education or local-agriculture gains rather than on large average growth effects.

**Biofortification can raise micronutrient intake at scale.** In a randomised effectiveness study in Mozambique, promoting orange sweet potato raised vitamin A intakes among young children and women; among the study's reference children, the crop provided about 80 percent of total vitamin A intake (Hotz et al., 2011). A HarvestPlus review reports that more than 20 million people in farm households were growing and eating biofortified crops by 2016, and identifies breeding, consumer demand and policy integration as the main barriers to scale (Bouis & Saltzman, 2017). That review is written by the programme's own researchers. The strongest evidence is on intake; effects on biomarkers and functional health outcomes vary by crop and setting.

**Early-childhood nutrition can pay off decades later.** In a long-run follow-up of a village-randomised trial in Guatemala, men who received a nutritious supplement between ages 0 and 2 earned hourly wages about 46 percent higher as adults. No such wage effect was found for exposure after age 3, or in the study's estimates for women (Hoddinott et al., 2008).

**Small acts of trade cooperation.** In June 2022, WTO members agreed not to impose export prohibitions or restrictions on food bought by the World Food Programme for non-commercial humanitarian purposes (WTO, 2022). Since 2011, the G20's Agricultural Market Information System has published shared data on supply, stocks and prices for wheat, maize, rice and soybeans to reduce panic-driven policy (AMIS, n.d.). Both show that rival exporters can agree on narrow rules that protect the hungriest people.

## Ethical tensions

**National duty versus global harm.** A government has real duties to its own citizens, and a short export ban can look like responsible protection. But if many exporters act the same way, the evidence shows they mostly shift hardship onto poorer importing countries without stabilising their own prices (Martin & Anderson, 2012). The tension is between partial loyalty and a duty not to harm outsiders.

**Respect versus paternalism.** In-kind food aid lets donors control what people eat. Cash respects people's own judgement and is usually cheaper (Gentilini, 2016). Yet food or vouchers can do better on specific nutrition goals in some settings (Hidrobo, Hoddinott, Peterman, Margolies, & Moreira, 2014). The ethical default should be to respect choice, with clear and evidence-based reasons for exceptions.

**Sovereignty versus access.** When a party to a conflict blocks aid, insisting on consent can mean letting people starve, while forcing access risks escalation. Resolution 2417 states the norm but does not resolve how to enforce it (United Nations Security Council, 2018).

**Efficiency versus dignity in targeting.** Targeting help to the poorest stretches budgets but requires collecting personal data, and errors exclude people who need help. Surveillance-heavy targeting can harm the people it is meant to serve, especially refugees and members of persecuted groups.

**Waste is not a transfer.** It is tempting to say wasted food could feed the hungry. Most household waste in rich countries cannot be moved to a famine zone, and reducing it does not automatically lower prices for the poor. Waste matters mainly for climate and land use (UNEP, 2024); the duty to the hungry has to be met through access and income.

**Present relief versus future resilience.** Every dollar spent on emergency food is not spent on irrigation, roads or climate adaptation, and the reverse is also true. With funding falling (FAO et al., 2024; Global Network Against Food Crises & FSIN, 2026), this trade-off is getting sharper.

## Original proposals

These are this study's own suggestions, built on the evidence above.

1. **A humanitarian "green lane" for food trade.** Extend the 2022 WTO decision (WTO, 2022) so that export restrictions also cannot apply to food bought by UNICEF and by national safety-net programmes in low-income countries during declared emergencies, and require any export restriction to be notified within days, time-limited, and published through AMIS. This targets the collective-action problem that Martin and Anderson describe, without denying governments the ability to protect their own people.

2. **Pre-arranged, automatic cash for predictable shocks.** Agree in advance that when early-warning data cross a set threshold (for example, a projected rise into IPC Phase 3 or higher, or a sharp local price spike), monthly cash payments start automatically, using financing set aside beforehand. Monthly cash is the form most strongly linked to food security (Haushofer & Shapiro, 2016), and acting before a crisis peaks is cheaper than acting afterwards. Because the best-identified local multipliers come from particular market settings (Egger et al., 2022), the rule should include price monitoring and a switch to in-kind help if markets fail.

3. **A public "access denial" ledger.** Independent humanitarian monitors would record, in a public and evidence-based register, every documented case of aid being blocked, of farms, water systems or markets being destroyed, and of crossings being closed, by any party. This gives Resolution 2417 a factual base for diplomatic and legal follow-up. It should report patterns and parties, never the identities of aid workers or civilians.

4. **Healthy-diet affordability as a trade and tax goal.** Since about 2.7 billion people cannot afford a healthy diet (FAO et al., 2026), governments should review tariffs, subsidies and taxes on fruits, vegetables, pulses and other nutritious foods, and reduce those that make healthy diets dearer for poor households.

5. **Graduation plus climate.** Pair graduation-style packages (Banerjee et al., 2015) with drought- and heat-resilient farming support in the warmer regions where climate is already cutting productivity (Ortiz-Bobea et al., 2021).

6. **Food as common ground between rivals.** Grain trade is one of the few areas where countries in tense relationships still depend on each other. Shared stock and price data, the WFP export exemption, and agreed humanitarian corridors are low-cost confidence-building steps that major exporters and importers across different political blocs could expand together, independent of their wider disputes.

## Risks

- **Cash in broken markets.** If markets do not function, cash can push up local prices. The Kenyan general-equilibrium trial found little inflation (Egger et al., 2022), but that result may not hold in thinner markets. Cash should be paired with market monitoring and a switch to in-kind help where needed (Gentilini, 2016).
- **Trigger errors.** Automatic triggers can fire too late or too early. They need human review and public rules.
- **Weaponised data.** An access-denial ledger or targeting database could be misused by armed groups or governments. Data must be minimal, secure and aggregated.
- **Political capture of trade rules.** Exporters may resist new limits, or accept them only with loopholes. A narrow first step, like the 2022 decision, is more likely to hold.
- **Donor fatigue.** With funding already down (FAO et al., 2024; Global Network Against Food Crises & FSIN, 2026), new mechanisms that rely on voluntary money may never be filled.

## Recommendation

Treat hunger as a failure of access and a collective-action problem, not a shortage. The strongest evidence supports putting regular, predictable cash in the hands of poor households, paired with nutrition services and farm support, and agreeing narrow trade rules that stop exporters from collectively exporting hardship. Social-protection programmes improve both the quantity and the quality of diets, and graduation packages have lasting effects in several countries, but school feeding and biofortification should be judged on their more modest, often targeted, measured gains. Where conflict causes famine, the ethical duty shifts to documenting and confronting deliberate starvation. Food waste deserves action for climate reasons, but it should not distract from the duty to make healthy food affordable to the 2.7 billion people who cannot buy it today.
