# The Right to Work for Refugees and Displaced People

*An ethical case study. Version 1.0, November 2026. Desk research only; no fieldwork.*

## Summary

Most refugees live for years in countries that did not choose to receive them, and whether they may work legally shapes almost everything else: income, children's schooling, exposure to exploitation, and how host communities see them. This study examines three host-country approaches to labour market access: Colombia's temporary protection statute for Venezuelans, the Jordan Compact work permits for Syrians, and Uganda's settlement and self-reliance model. It then gives a serious hearing to the concern that refugee workers displace host workers, reviewing studies from Turkey, Colombia, Jordan and the Mariel boatlift. It sets out the evidence linking lack of legal work to forced labour, trafficking, debt and child labour, and is explicit where that evidence is associational rather than causal.

Main findings, each sourced below:

- **Legal status helps displaced people.** In Colombia, an earlier regularisation programme raised beneficiaries' consumption per person by 48% and monthly labour income by 22% [1]. In Jordan, holding a work permit is associated with higher income and less child labour [2].
- **Host effects are real for some groups but usually small on average.** The clearest losses fall on informal, low-educated and female host workers, and sometimes on other migrants [3][4][5][6][7]. Average effects on host wages and employment are small in most studies [8][9][10].
- **The Mariel debate shows how fragile large estimates can be.** One reanalysis found low-skilled wages fell 10% to 30% [11]; others attribute that result to tiny samples and a change in sample composition [12][13].
- **Exclusion from legal work is a recognised forced-labour risk**, but direct causal evidence that granting work rights reduces trafficking is thin [14][15].

## Part 1. Three cases

### 1.1 Colombia: the Temporary Protection Statute for Venezuelans (ETPV and PPT)

**Policy.** Under Decree 216 of 2021, Colombia created a Temporary Protection Statute for Venezuelan migrants (ETPV), which grants a Temporary Protection Permit (PPT) giving access to public and private services, including work [16]. Registration for most groups ran from 29 May 2021 to 24 November 2023 and stays open for children until 2031; it was later reopened for people who could not register on time [16]. An earlier, smaller amnesty, the Special Permanence Permit (PEP), gave regular status and work permits to nearly half a million undocumented Venezuelans in August 2018 [17].

**Take-up.**
- 2,164,860 PPTs had been authorised and 2,113,830 delivered by October 2025 [16].
- In August 2025, 2,831,561 Venezuelans were identified in Colombia, of whom 83% had regular migration status and 17% did not [18].

**Outcomes for displaced people.** The strongest causal evidence concerns the 2018 PEP rather than the PPT. Using an eligibility cut-off date that was set after the fact and was unknown to the public, Ibáñez and co-authors found beneficiaries gained 48% in consumption per person, 22% in monthly labour income and 1.2 standard deviations in health status, mainly through access to social protection, subsidised healthcare and financial services. Fiscal costs per regularised household were lower than for irregular households [1]. Comparable published estimates for the larger PPT were not found for this study.

**Effects on host workers.**
- For the 2018 PEP, Bahar, Ibáñez and Rozo found only negative but negligible effects on Colombians' formal employment, concentrated among highly educated and female workers [17].
- For Venezuelan migration in general (with or without status), Caruso and co-authors estimate that a 1 percentage point rise in immigration reduced urban informal-sector wages by 10 percentage points; given the actual increase in 2017, they estimate urban wages fell by about 2 percentage points in total [3].
- Lebow, reconciling earlier studies, finds negative effects on hourly wages concentrated among less-educated natives in the informal sector: about −1.05% per 1 percentage point rise in the migrant share, or −0.59% after controlling for regional trends, with little to no effect on native employment. Effects are larger where informality is higher and starting a business is harder [8].
- Otero-Cortés and co-authors find low-educated Colombian women's labour force participation fell, while highly educated women with children increased participation by 1.6 percentage points [4].

### 1.2 Jordan: the Jordan Compact work permits for Syrians

**Policy.** At the 2016 London conference, Jordan pledged to make 200,000 job opportunities available to Syrian refugees by 2020, measured by work permits issued, in return for multi-year grants of about US$700 million a year for three years and US$1.9 billion in concessional loans [19]. Work permit fees were waived in 2016 under a World Bank loan agreement; a self-employment option followed in 2021; full fees were reinstated on 1 July 2024 [20]. Permits are typically valid for 12 months, renewable, and usually tied to an employer sponsor, with "freelance-flexible" permits in sectors such as agriculture and construction [20].

**Take-up.**
- Jordan issued 458,135 work permits to Syrians between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2024 [20]. Because permits are annual and renewable, this counts permits issued, not distinct people.
- By May 2021, 233,699 permits had been issued to refugees, around 90% of them to Syrians; annual issuance fell from about 48,000 in 2019 to about 39,000 in 2020 [21].
- Women's take-up has been very low. Only 7% of Syrian women participated in the labour market, compared with 16% of Jordanian women [21].
- Jordan hosted 672,952 UNHCR-registered Syrian refugees, 81% of them in Amman and the northern governorates outside camps [20].

**Outcomes for displaced people.**
- Syrians' labour force participation rose from 28% in 2014 to 33% in 2018, and unemployment fell from about 60% to about 8%; the ILO notes this cannot be attributed directly to permits [21].
- About 37% of employed Syrians without a permit earned under 150 Jordanian dinars a month, against about 20% of permit holders [21].
- 25% of permit holders had written contracts versus 9% without permits; 18% versus 9% had social security coverage [21].
- Almost 70% of permit holders said the permit made them feel safe in public spaces [21].
- Using matched data on almost 75,000 Syrian refugee households from 2017 to 2019, Peitz and co-authors find holding a work permit is significantly associated with higher income, better food security, fewer protection needs and less child labour [2].
- The 2024 fee reinstatement shows how fragile gains can be: 84% of surveyed employers said they did not intend to pay the fee, and 43% of employers in the northern governorates said they would employ workers informally [20].

**Effects on host workers.**
- Fallah, Krafft and Wahba find that Jordanians in areas with more refugees had no worse labour market outcomes than those with less exposure, using panel data from 2010 and 2016 [9].
- Malaeb and Wahba find the competition fell on other immigrants instead: male immigrants in high-refugee areas were more likely to work informally, work fewer hours and earn lower monthly wages [5].
- The ILO judges that Syrian participation has likely influenced informal workers' wages but had minimal impact on formal wages [20].

### 1.3 Uganda: settlement and self-reliance

**Policy.** Uganda's Refugees Act of 2006 and Refugee Regulations of 2010 grant refugees freedom of movement, the right to work and access to national services; in settlements, refugees receive plots of land for housing and cultivation [22][23]. A national transition strategy for 2025 to 2035 aims to shift refugee health, education and water services from partner-led to government-managed systems; 92% of refugee health facilities had been coded into the government system and 41% of settlement schools integrated [22].

**Scale.**
- Uganda hosts 1,999,576 refugees and asylum-seekers, the largest refugee population in Africa; 52% are South Sudanese and 33% Congolese, and women and children make up 77% [22].
- In February 2026, the government ended prima facie refugee recognition for new asylum-seekers from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Sudan and South Sudan, moving to individual status determination [22].
- UNHCR had received only 14% of its US$361 million funding requirement for 2026 and had cut its field presence by 23% since 2025 [22].

**Outcomes for displaced people.** The World Bank's 2018 refugee and host household survey found:
- 46% of refugees lived in poverty, against 17% of hosts [24].
- 28% of refugees were employed. Their 72% unemployment rate compared with 36% for hosts; the World Bank still called the employment level a major achievement given the right to work [24].
- Unemployment fell with time in the country, from 77% for refugees there under two years to about 54% for those there five years or more [24].
- 54% of refugee households reported aid as their main source of income; for those who arrived over five years earlier, it was still 37% [24].

A study of Bidi Bidi, Uganda's largest integrated settlement, found refugees adapted the layout to their needs but also documented large-scale deforestation affecting the most vulnerable, and the limits of a land-based route to self-reliance [23].

**Effects on hosts.**
- Zhou, Grossman and Ge find that host communities with greater refugee presence, particularly after more than one million South Sudanese arrived from 2014, saw substantial improvements in local development (schools, roads, health clinics), and found no evidence of more negative attitudes towards migrants [10].
- Direct estimates of effects on Ugandan workers' wages were not found among the verified sources for this study, so none are claimed. A related simulation study of three Congolese refugee camps in Rwanda found that an additional adult refugee receiving cash aid raised annual real income in the local economy by US$205 to US$253, significantly more than the US$120 to US$126 in aid each refugee received [25].

## Part 2. The host-worker displacement concern

Displacement worries are not imaginary. The evidence shows when and for whom they materialise, and how large the effects are.

### 2.1 Turkey: where the clearest losses were found

Turkey hosted millions of Syrians who mostly lacked work permits, which makes it the closest test of what happens when refugees can work only informally.

- Del Carpio and Wagner find large-scale displacement of informal, low-educated and female Turkish workers, especially in agriculture. Their estimates are consistent with roughly one-for-one displacement of Turkish workers in informal jobs, though they note the true effect is probably smaller. About one quarter of this reflects Turks relocating, with the arrival of 10 refugees causing about 2.5 natives to leave a province. Refugees also created higher-wage formal jobs, allowing some Turkish workers to upgrade [6].
- Ceritoglu and co-authors find considerable employment effects but negligible wage effects, with notable losses of informal jobs; most who lost informal jobs left the labour force or stayed unemployed. Women, younger and less-educated workers were hit hardest, and widespread informality amplified the damage, but they judge the overall impact limited [7].
- Tumen finds moderate informal employment losses, lower prices for goods produced in informal-labour-intensive sectors, and higher rents for better-quality housing only [26].
- Altındağ, Bakış and Rozo find refugee inflows increased firm production, especially for small firms in construction and hospitality, driven mostly by increased informal activity [27].

**Reading.** Turkish losses fell mainly on the people who already competed in the informal market. Arguably this is partly a result of refugees being confined to informal work, not only of their numbers; the Turkish studies themselves highlight informality as the amplifier [6][7].

### 2.2 Colombia and Jordan

- Colombia: wage effects concentrated among less-educated informal workers, roughly −0.6% to −1% per percentage point of migrant share, with little employment effect [8]; low-educated native women reduced participation [4]; legalising status itself had negligible effects on formal employment [17].
- Jordan: no measurable harm to Jordanians on average [9], but other immigrant workers absorbed the competition [5].

### 2.3 The Mariel boatlift debate

- In 1980 about 125,000 Cubans arrived in Florida within months [11]. Card found they raised Miami's labour force by 7%, yet had virtually no effect on the wages or unemployment of less-skilled workers [28].
- Borjas reanalysed the data for high school dropouts, who were at least 60% of the arrivals, and found their wages in Miami dropped by 10% to 30% [11].
- Peri and Yasenov, using a synthetic control method, found no significant wage difference for non-Cuban dropouts and argue that the large differences arise from very small subsamples and measurement error [12].
- Clemens and Hunt show the conflicting Mariel results can be explained by a large change in the racial composition of tiny survey subsamples, unrelated to the boatlift, and conclude that refugee waves reinforce the consensus that effects on average native workers are small and fail to substantiate large harms for workers without a high school diploma [13].

### 2.4 What a fair reading concludes

1. **Average effects are small in most settings** [8][9][13].
2. **Concentrated losses are real.** They fall on informal, low-educated, female and earlier migrant workers [4][5][6][7].
3. **Informality is the amplifier.** Effects are largest where refugees can only work informally and where informality is already high [6][7][8].
4. **Legalisation itself appears to add little extra harm.** The one direct test, Colombia's 2018 PEP, found negligible effects on formal employment [17].
5. **Distribution matters ethically.** A small average loss can be a large loss for an informal worker in a border town. Policies that ignore those losers risk both injustice and political backlash.

## Part 3. No legal work, exploitation and trafficking

### 3.1 The mechanism

When displaced people cannot work legally, they work informally. Informal workers lack contracts, social protection and access to courts, so wage theft and abuse carry little cost for employers. Debts for travel, housing or documents become leverage. Families short of income send children to work. In the worst cases, these conditions meet the legal definition of forced labour or trafficking.

### 3.2 The evidence

- **Migrants and forced labour.** The ILO estimates the forced-labour prevalence of adult migrant workers is more than three times that of non-migrant workers. It links this to migrants not being protected by law, irregular or poorly governed migration, and unfair recruitment [14].
- **Debt.** The ILO reports that income disruption during the COVID-19 pandemic led to greater indebtedness and reports of a rise in debt bondage among workers without access to formal credit [14].
- **Displacement and trafficking.** The UN Office on Drugs and Crime states that as conflict, climate disasters and displacement converge, some people are pushed directly into trafficking and others are left without homes and prospects at huge risk of it. Detected victims rose 25% in 2022 compared with 2019, and women and girls were 61% of detected victims [15].
- **Work permits and protection.** In Jordan, workers without permits are more vulnerable to exploitation, such as non-payment and underpayment of wages, and are not legally recognised within the judicial system; workers caught working without a permit face deportation or, for Syrians, relocation to a camp [20]. Permit holders are more likely to have written contracts and social security [21].
- **Child labour.** In Turkey, where most Syrians worked without permits, 17.4% of refugee boys aged 12 to 14 and 45.1% of those aged 15 to 17 were in paid employment, associated with poverty and difficulty integrating into school [29]. In Jordan, holding a work permit is associated with less child labour [2].
- **Regularisation and wellbeing.** Colombia's 2018 regularisation raised consumption and labour income and improved access to services [1].

### 3.3 Where the evidence is thin

- **No causal estimate links work rights to trafficking rates.** None of the verified sources measures whether granting work rights reduces trafficking or forced labour among refugees. The ILO's migrant figure is a prevalence comparison, not a causal effect of legal status [14].
- **Association, not causation.** The Jordanian work-permit findings come from matching, which cannot fully rule out that refugees who obtain permits differ in unobserved ways [2].
- **Hidden populations.** Trafficking victims are hard to count, and UNODC itself notes that rising detections may partly reflect better detection [15].

The mechanism is plausible and consistent with every piece of evidence reviewed here, but its size is unknown.

## Part 4. Policy lessons

1. **Grant legal work early and simply.** Benefits for displaced people are large where measured [1][2], and the one direct test of legalisation found negligible harm to host formal employment [17].
2. **Keep fees and paperwork low.** Jordan's reinstated fees led most surveyed employers to plan not to pay and many to plan informal hiring [20].
3. **Decouple permits from single employers where possible.** Employer sponsorship and full-time fixed contracts do not fit seasonal sectors and push workers into informality [20].
4. **Target support at host workers who compete directly.** Informal, low-educated and female host workers and earlier migrants carry most of the losses [4][5][6][7]; training, enforcement against wage undercutting and social protection should reach them.
5. **Invest in places, not only people.** Shared services in refugee-hosting areas of Uganda improved local development without measurable backlash [10].
6. **Address women's barriers directly.** Syrian women's participation in Jordan remained very low [21].
7. **Fund the model.** Uganda's approach depends on finance that is now sharply short [22].

---

## Part 5. Ethical risks and trade-offs

1. **Concentrated costs on the poorest hosts.** A policy that raises total welfare can still harm informal workers who are already poor [6][7][8]. Ignoring them is unjust and fuels backlash.
2. **Displacing other migrants.** In Jordan the losers were other immigrant workers, who are often more vulnerable than citizens and less visible in policy debate [5].
3. **Transactional refugee policy.** The Jordan Compact tied refugee work rights to trade concessions, grants and loans [19]. This mobilised resources but risks making rights conditional on donor money; the 2024 fee reinstatement shows how quickly access can narrow [20].
4. **Temporary status as a trap.** Temporary permits can leave people in prolonged uncertainty; renewal requirements and deadlines can push people back into irregularity [16][20].
5. **Self-reliance as cover for withdrawing aid.** Uganda's model is admired, but refugee poverty and aid dependence remain high [24] and funding is collapsing [22]. "Self-reliance" must not become a justification for cutting support before livelihoods exist.
6. **Environmental and land pressure.** Land-based self-reliance can drive deforestation and conflict over resources [23].
7. **Restricting asylum to protect a generous model.** Uganda's end of prima facie recognition in 2026 [22] illustrates a trade-off between keeping rights for those admitted and limiting new admissions.

---

## Part 6. Open questions

1. What are the measured welfare and host labour-market effects of Colombia's PPT, which covers about four times as many people as the 2018 PEP [16][17]?
2. Does granting work rights reduce trafficking and forced labour among refugees, and by how much [14][15]?
3. How much of the Turkish displacement effect would disappear if refugees had been able to work formally from the start [6][7]?
4. How can compensation reach informal host workers who lose income, when they are hard to identify and outside tax systems?
5. Can Uganda's model survive the current funding collapse without sliding into restrictions [22]?
6. Why do women refugees remain largely outside formal work even where permits exist, and which measures change that [21]?
