# Opening fields both ways: equal participation through merit, not head-counting

*Ethics case study. Research only. Figures are those stated by the cited sources, dated as of the source, and were checked in November 2026.*

---

## 1. The ethical question

Women remain under-represented in engineering, computing and senior leadership. Men remain under-represented in nursing, in primary teaching and, in most regions, in higher education. Boys, on average, lag behind girls in reading and in completing school. These are not competing claims. They are the same claim looked at from both sides: people should be able to enter, stay in and lead a field if they are capable of the work, and they should not be steered away or held back because of their sex.

This case is about that principle. It is not about forcing each occupation or classroom to a 50/50 split. Numeric balance can be a useful warning light when a fair process keeps producing an odd result. It is a poor goal in itself. A target that fills seats without regard to who can do the job can shut capable people out, just as biased gatekeeping can. The test used here is whether an approach opens a field to whoever is capable.

The case looks at four things:

1. the size and direction of the main participation and attainment gaps, using official statistics;
2. the debate over the "gender-equality paradox" in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM);
3. evidence on merit-based approaches that widen the qualified pool or make selection fairer: outreach, mentoring, role models, and the repair of biased gatekeeping;
4. where numeric targets have helped, where they have not, and where they can do harm.

It does not argue that any one cause explains every gap, and it does not treat a remaining gap, on its own, as proof of bias or as proof that bias is absent.

---

## 2. The gaps, in both directions

### 2.1 Women in engineering, computing and senior leadership

In G20 countries, women accounted for 35% of STEM university graduates, 29% of graduates in information and communication technologies, and 26% of graduates in engineering, manufacturing and construction, according to a UNESCO report prepared for the G20 and summarised by UNESCO in October 2024 [1]. The same briefing put women's share of STEM occupations in the G20 at 22% in 2021, up only three points since 2005. Among a sample of 13 countries, women received 37% of scientific grants in 2022. UNESCO also reported that more than 40% of women studying STEM subjects said they had been victims of sexist behaviour [1]. These are G20 figures, not a global census, and some of the underlying survey items are self-reported.

The picture in the wider OECD is similar in direction. An OECD education brief of March 2024 reported that only 31% of new entrants to tertiary STEM programmes were women, compared with more than 75% in both education and health and welfare. The share of women among STEM entrants exceeded 40% only in Iceland and New Zealand in the data shown [2]. Young women in every OECD country were more likely than young men to hold a tertiary qualification, yet they remained under-represented in the fields most in demand in the labour market [2].

Senior leadership shows a related pattern. An ILO statistical brief issued in 2025, drawing on ILO modelled estimates, reported that women held 30% of managerial positions globally as of 2023, a modest rise over two decades. In low-income countries the rise was larger, from 24.7% to 36.5% [3]. A headline share of this kind mixes very different jobs and firms. It is not a measure of whether a given organisation promotes fairly.

Two points follow. First, women's under-representation is not uniform across "STEM": it is sharpest in computing and engineering, not in every scientific field. Second, a graduate pipeline of around one-quarter to one-third in engineering and computing cannot, on its own, produce equal shares in those occupations or in the leadership posts that often draw from them. That does not make bias irrelevant. It does mean that a 50% leadership target in a field with a much smaller qualified intake will, if pursued as a headcount goal, put pressure on standards or on neighbouring fields.

### 2.2 Men in nursing, primary teaching and higher education

The same occupational sorting runs the other way.

The World Health Organization's *State of the World's Nursing 2025* report, as summarised in a WHO news release of 12 May 2025, found that women made up 85% of the global nursing workforce, leaving men at about 15% [4]. ILO figures for a related but not identical group are less extreme: in a 126-country sample covering 57% of global employment, women were 78.0% of nursing and midwifery professionals and 79.8% of nursing and midwifery associate professionals [3]. The difference is expected. The ILO numbers include midwifery and are a weighted occupational sample, not a full global nurse headcount. In either series, men are a clear minority.

Teaching is also highly skewed, especially at the first levels of school. The OECD's *Education at a Glance 2026*, reporting 2024 data, found that women accounted for 96% of pre-primary teachers and 83% of primary teachers in OECD countries, but only 46% of teachers at tertiary level [5]. Women were 67% of primary-school managers, still a majority, but well below their 83% share of primary teachers [5]. In the ILO's broader 126-country sample, women were 68.1% of the ISCO group that combines primary-school and early-years teachers [3]. Again the definitions differ, but the direction does not.

In higher education, the gap now favours women in most of the world. UNESCO's 2022 global report on boys' disengagement from education found that, in 2019, 88 men were enrolled in tertiary education for every 100 women. Almost no country with data had reached gender parity at that level. In all regions except sub-Saharan Africa, young men were under-represented. The gap was widest in North America, Western Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean, at 81 young men for every 100 young women, and stood at 87 in East Asia and the Pacific and 91 in the Arab States and in Central and Eastern Europe [6][7].

In the United States, Richard Reeves and Ember Smith, writing at Brookings, reported the same reversal. In 1970, 20% of men aged 25 to 34 held a bachelor's degree, compared with 12% of women. By 2020 the figures were 32% for men and 41% for women, a nine-point gap the other way, or about 1.6 million more young women than young men with a bachelor's degree [8]. By 2019 the gap in bachelor's awards was 14 points, wider than the 12-point gap that favoured men in 1972 when Title IX was passed, and in the opposite direction. In 2018–19, about 74 men received a bachelor's degree for every 100 women. Men were 41% of students enrolled in post-secondary institutions in fall 2020 [9]. These US figures are not a global rate, but they match the UNESCO and OECD direction for high-income countries.

### 2.3 Boys falling behind in school

The tertiary gap is not a sudden preference that appears at age 18. It is the late stage of an earlier attainment gap.

In PISA 2022, on average across OECD countries, girls outperformed boys in reading by 24 score points, roughly a year of schooling, and boys outperformed girls in mathematics by nine points. The science difference was not statistically significant. Girls outperformed boys in reading in every participating system except Chile and Costa Rica, where the difference was not significant. Among low performers (below Level 2), 31% of boys and 22% of girls were in that group in reading; in mathematics the shares were almost identical (31% of boys, 32% of girls). Among top performers, 11% of boys and 7% of girls reached Level 5 or above in mathematics, while 8% of girls and 6% of boys did so in reading [10][2]. Boys also showed more variable scores than girls in all three subjects, on average [10].

The OECD notes that these average gaps have been fairly stable since 2015, but they vary widely by country. In Finland, Norway and Slovenia, the reading gap for girls exceeded 40 points; in Chile, Mexico and Peru it was under 10 [2]. The 2021 PIRLS study found girls already ahead in reading in the fourth year of primary school in all OECD countries [2].

UNESCO's 2022 report, drawing on evidence from more than 140 countries, found that boys in many countries are at greater risk than girls of repeating grades, failing to complete a level, and leaving with weaker learning. In 2020, 132 million boys of primary and secondary age were out of school [6][7]. A UNESCO briefing updated in April 2026 put the later global totals at 140 million boys and 133 million girls out of school, with boys more than half of the out-of-school population [11]. Those two figures refer to different dates and should not be subtracted from each other. In 130 of 142 countries with data, boys were more likely than girls to repeat primary grades. In 73 countries, fewer boys than girls were enrolled in upper secondary education; the reverse was true in 48 countries [6][7].

The 2022 report and the later briefing both stress that girls still face serious barriers, especially in access at primary level in some countries, and that helping boys does not mean that girls lose out [6][11]. The drivers they emphasise for boys' disengagement include poverty and the need to work, norms that treat schoolwork as at odds with masculinity, streaming and sex-segregated classes, harsh discipline, and physical bullying [11]. Those are barriers to merit. A boy who leaves school early is not revealing a lack of interest in a fair contest for later study or work; he is leaving the contest.

In the United States, Reeves and Smith estimated that 88.4% of girls and 81.9% of boys graduated from high school on time in 2021, a 6.5-point gap, in the 33 states with published data. Girls were more than 40% of a grade level ahead of boys in reading in every state [8]. Reeves has argued that the college enrolment gap is largely explained by earlier academic preparation, and that boys' under-achievement should be treated as a problem in its own right, without setting aside women's remaining disadvantages at work [9][12].

## 3. The gender-equality paradox: the finding, the correction and the critique

In 2018, Gijsbert Stoet and David C. Geary reported what they called an educational gender-equality paradox [13]. Using PISA 2015 and UNESCO degree data, they argued that sex differences in relative academic strengths, and in the pursuit of STEM degrees, were larger in countries that scored higher on the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Index (GGGI). Finland, they noted, ranked very high on gender equality and on educational performance, yet had one of the largest STEM degree gaps; Norway and Sweden, also high on the GGGI, had fewer than 25% women among STEM graduates in the data they used. In their sample, the share of women among STEM graduates ranged from 12.4% in Macao to 40.7% in Algeria [13].

Their explanation was not that girls in more equal countries are worse at science. In 19 of 67 countries, girls outperformed boys in science. It was that students, they argued, choose paths based on their own relative strengths. Most boys scored relatively higher in science than in their all-subject average; most girls scored relatively higher in reading. Even in Finland, where girls outperformed boys in science, girls' individual strength was still typically reading. The authors reported a Spearman correlation of *rs* = −.47 (*n* = 50, *p* < .001) between gender equality and the STEM graduation pattern they measured, and they reported that sex differences in science self-efficacy, interest and enjoyment were also larger in more gender-equal countries [13]. They estimated that, on ability alone, far more women could complete STEM degrees than actually did (an international mean of 49% in their ability-based estimate, against an actual mean of 28%) [13].

The paper was subsequently corrected and contested. After readers, including Sarah S. Richardson and colleagues, had trouble reproducing the reported STEM figures from the description in the text, Stoet and Geary issued a corrigendum in *Psychological Science* [14]. The substance of the dispute, as set out by Richardson and Joe Bruch on the GenderSci Lab site and in a 2020 commentary in the same journal, is that the original article described its outcome as "the percentage of women among STEM graduates", the usual official statistic, but the numbers used were a ratio of women's and men's *propensities* to earn a STEM degree [15][16]. A propensity ratio and a simple share can move in different directions when overall graduation rates differ by sex and by country. The exact wording of the corrigendum was not independently re-read from the journal's site for this case (see Section 9). The authors' later reply is on the record [17].

The 2020 commentary by Richardson, Meredith W. Reiches, Joe Bruch, Marion Boulicault, Nicole E. Noll and Heather Shattuck-Heidorn pressed further points [16][15]. The GGGI, they argued, is a benchmarking index of outcome gaps, not a measure of opportunity or of STEM-specific conditions, and was not designed for causal explanation. Country-level correlations cannot identify individual preferences or experiences. There was no longitudinal design, so the data cannot show that a rise in equality caused a fall in women's STEM share. And the finding was not robust, on their account, to other ways of measuring gender equality and STEM outcomes: in one re-analysis they reported, the correlation became small and non-significant (*rs* = 0.070, *p* = 0.647) [15]. Stoet and Geary replied that the pattern was part of a broader phenomenon, and they defended their measure and interpretation [17].

What this debate can settle should be stated plainly. It is not in serious dispute that women's share of engineering and computing graduates varies widely by country, or that some highly equal, high-income countries have large gaps. It is in serious dispute whether "more gender equality causes fewer women to choose STEM", whether the GGGI is the right measure for that claim, and whether the original numbers were the statistic most readers thought they were seeing. Comparative advantage — choosing the subject in which one is relatively strongest — is a plausible mechanism and is consistent with the PISA pattern in which girls' reading advantage is larger than boys' mathematics advantage. It has not been shown, in a design that can support a causal claim, to be the main driver of occupational sex differences. Alternative accounts, including labour-market incentives, the way countries classify STEM, who studies abroad, and unmeasured barriers, remain open.

The policy implication that survives is modest and useful. A 50/50 split in a field is not, by itself, a test of fairness, and a large gap is not, by itself, a test of unfairness. The right next step is to look at the actual barriers and the actual pool of capable people, in both directions.

## 4. Merit-based approaches, and the evidence they work

A merit-based approach has two halves. It widens the pool of people who can do the work, so that talent is not lost before the contest begins. And it judges applicants on the work, so that sex is not allowed to stand in for competence. Outreach, mentoring and role models serve the first half. Repairing gatekeeping serves the second.

### 4.1 Role models and outreach

Thomas Breda, Julien Grenet, Marion Monnet and Clémentine Van Effenterre reported a large field experiment in French high schools, later published in *The Economic Journal* [18]. Women scientists gave a one-hour classroom visit. The visits reduced stereotypical views of scientific jobs and of sex differences in ability. They also raised enrolment, among high-achieving girls in grade 12, in selective and in male-dominated STEM programmes (mathematics, physics, computer science and engineering), by 3.1 and 3.4 percentage points respectively [18]. The effect did not appear in the ordinary grade-11 science track, where girls were only slightly under-represented (47%) [18].

A result that matters for design, and not only for women's STEM entry, is this: the most effective visits were those that improved students' view of STEM careers *without* over-emphasising women's under-representation in science [18]. A message that says "this field is not for people like you, but come anyway" is a weaker invitation than a message that shows the work and the path. The same logic applies to boys and nursing or teaching, even though this particular trial was not designed to test that case.

The OECD notes national outreach programmes of a similar spirit, including schemes in Chile and Japan. Those programmes have not, in the sources checked here, been evaluated with the same experimental design as the French visits, so their effects should not be assumed [2].

### 4.2 Mentoring

A randomised study by Tara C. Dennehy and Nilanjana Dasgupta, published in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* in 2017, assigned women in undergraduate engineering a female peer mentor, a male peer mentor, or no mentor [19]. Reports of the trial, including the official abstract, state that a female peer mentor, rather than a male mentor, sustained belonging and self-efficacy and raised retention and engineering-career aspirations over two years [19]. The full paper could not be re-opened from the journal or PubMed Central for this case (see Section 9), so exact retention percentages are not quoted here. The direction of the finding is consistent with the role-model evidence above: early, credible encouragement from someone who has already taken the path can keep capable students in the pool.

Mentoring is not a guarantee, and it is not only for women. If the test is merit, then students who are capable but isolated — including men in nursing and primary-teaching courses, and boys who are falling behind in reading — are the people who need it, not only members of a group that is under-represented in a fashionable field.

### 4.3 Repairing biased gatekeeping

Gatekeeping is where merit either holds or fails. If two people with the same record are scored differently because of their sex, the process is not a merit process, whatever the official criteria say.

Corinne A. Moss-Racusin, John F. Dovidio, Victoria L. Brescoll, Mark J. Graham and Jo Handelsman reported a randomised, double-blind study of science faculty in biology, chemistry and physics [20]. Faculty were sent an application for a laboratory-manager post that was identical except for the applicant's first name. They rated the male applicant as more competent and more hireable, offered more career mentoring, and offered a higher starting salary ($30,238.10, compared with $26,507.94 for the female applicant). Female and male faculty showed this bias to a similar degree. A mediation analysis indicated that the hiring difference ran through perceived competence [20].

This is direct evidence that a supposedly merit-based review can smuggle sex in through competence ratings. It is also a 2012 study with 127 faculty and one type of post. Later replication attempts, including a large registered replication of the same materials, have been reported as failing to recover the original bias and, in some cases, as finding a bias in the other direction [21]. Those later papers were not opened in full for this case, so their results are flagged in Section 9 and should be read before anyone treats the 2012 effect as a stable constant. The right institutional response does not depend on any single experiment remaining the last word. It is to build processes that make it harder for a name or a stereotype to do the work of a score: clear, pre-set criteria; work samples; structured comparison; anonymised review where the work can be judged without a name; and audits of like-for-like outcomes.

The same discipline should apply when men are the ones under-represented. A hiring panel in nursing or primary teaching that downgrades a man for being "the wrong fit" is not applying a higher standard. It is applying an extra one.

### 4.4 Opening the earlier path

For boys, the merit problem often arrives years before a job application. If a large share of boys are already low performers in reading at 15 [10], later "equal competition" for university and professional jobs will reproduce that gap. UNESCO recommends action that is specific to context: addressing poverty-related dropout, countering norms that make schoolwork unattractive to boys, and ending harsh discipline and bullying [6][11]. The OECD reports that Australia has revised textbooks to engage boys in reading, and that Canada and Sweden have trained teachers to counter gender stereotypes [2]. Those are examples of practice, not trial-tested effect sizes. None of this requires lowering the bar for boys, any more than outreach to girls in engineering requires lowering the bar for girls. It requires that the bar be one they are given a fair chance to meet.

## 5. Where numeric targets help, and where they harm

Numeric targets and quotas are the main alternative, or supplement, to the approaches above. The best-studied case is Norway's board reform.

In late 2003 Norway passed a law requiring 40% representation of each sex on the boards of public limited-liability companies. Compliance was achieved by 2008, from a median of 0% women per board in 2003 (a firm-level median; measured as a share of all director seats, women held about 9% when the law passed) [22][23]. Marianne Bertrand, Sandra E. Black, Sissel Jensen and Adriana Lleras-Muney, in a study later published in *The Review of Economic Studies*, found that the women appointed after the reform were observably more qualified than their female predecessors, and that the earnings gap between women and men *on those boards* fell substantially [22]. Compared with the women who had served on boards before, the fear that firms would appoint weak candidates was not borne out on observable measures.

The effect on the firms themselves is disputed. Kenneth Ahern and Amy Dittmar, in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, found that the quota caused a significant drop in share prices when it was announced and a large decline in Tobin's Q over the following years, and that it led to younger and less experienced boards, more leverage and acquisitions, and weaker operating performance [23]. B. Espen Eckbo, Knut Nygaard and Karin Thorburn re-examined that study in *Management Science*. They argue that quota news is hard to pin to specific dates, that the earlier significance tests did not allow for stock returns moving together on the same days, and that quota effects are hard to separate from firm characteristics and the financial crisis. Their new evidence suggests the valuation effect was statistically insignificant, and that enough qualified women were available to avoid the costs reported earlier [24]. Both readings come from careful work on the same reform, so this case treats firm-level costs of the Norwegian quota as an open question, not a settled finding either way.

They also found the limits. There was no robust evidence that the reform improved outcomes for women employed in the companies subject to the quota, including in top posts below board level. It had no clear effect on highly qualified women who resembled board members but were not appointed. Female enrolment in business education did not change. Some early-career gains for young women with graduate business degrees were matched by similar gains among women with graduate science degrees, which suggests those gains may not have been caused by the quota. Seven years after the rule was fully in force, the authors concluded that it had "very little discernible impact on women in business beyond its direct effect on the women who made it into boardrooms" [22].

That record cuts both ways in the argument over targets.

Targets can help when a process is closed by networks or habit, and when the people who are then appointed are qualified. In Norway, the reform changed the composition of boards and raised the observable qualifications of the women who sat on them. A rule written as a floor for *each* sex, rather than as a one-sided preference, also fits the principle of this case better than a target that can only ever move in one direction.

Targets can harm, or simply disappoint, when they are treated as a substitute for a wider fair path. The Norwegian quota did not, in this study, change the pipeline, the enrolment choices of young women, or the prospects of qualified women outside the boardroom. A hard target that outruns the qualified pool can create the very problem opponents fear: seats filled to make a number, and a stigma attached to those who fill them. A target of 50% women in a computing department that graduates 23–29% women [1][2], or a target of 50% men in a nursing service that is 85% women [4], will do that if it is pursued as a headcount goal rather than as a reason to inspect recruitment, teaching and promotion.

A better use of numbers is diagnostic. If a process that is open, structured and audited still produces a lopsided result, that is information. It may point to a leak earlier in the pipeline, to a hostile climate, or to differences in who applies. It does not, on its own, tell an organisation to change the standard. The standard is the work.

## 6. Fair counterarguments

**"Focusing on men dilutes work for women."** UNESCO's own conclusion is that support for boys does not come at girls' expense, and that educated men are more likely to support equality and to condemn gender-based violence [6][11]. Women's under-representation in engineering, computing and leadership is real and unfinished [1][3]. The claim in this case is not that this work should stop. It is that the same principle — open the field to whoever is capable — applies when the under-represented group is male.

**"Gaps that favour women are a correction, not a problem."** Some of those gaps sit on top of older exclusions, and women's pay and leadership still lag [2][3]. That is true. It does not make a 6.5-point high-school graduation gap, a 24-point reading gap, or a tertiary ratio of 88 men to 100 women an irrelevance [8][10][6]. A fairness rule that only works in one direction is not a fairness rule.

**"Interest and relative strength, not bias, explain occupational gaps."** The paradox literature and the PISA relative-strength pattern give this view some support, and it should not be dismissed as an excuse [13][10]. The Moss-Racusin experiment, UNESCO's finding on sexist behaviour in STEM study, and the remaining leadership gap after decades of rising female attainment show that bias and climate still need to be checked [20][1][3]. Interests are also shaped by stereotypes and by who is seen in a role, which is why a one-hour visit can change enrolment [18]. Both channels can operate. A merit policy should address both: do not block capable people, and do not treat every imbalance as a block.

**"If role models and mentoring favour one sex, that is also a preference, not merit."** A time-limited effort to show a closed path to people who have been steered away is different from a standing rule that awards a post by sex. The French visits worked in the tracks where girls were severely under-represented, not where they were already close to half [18]. The same test should apply to efforts to attract men into nursing and primary teaching. Once the path is visible and the process is fair, the preference should lapse.

**"Quotas are the only way to break closed networks."** They can break a closed board, and in Norway they did so without a collapse in observable qualifications [22]. They are not the only way, and they did not, in that study, open the rest of the path. Repairing gatekeeping and widening the pool are slower, but they apply at every level and in both directions.

## 7. Trade-offs

| Approach | What it can do | What it risks or cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| Outreach and role models that show the work | Bring capable students into fields they had ruled out, with experimental evidence for high-achieving girls in selective STEM [18] | Effects can be narrow (one track, one group). Evidence for the mirror case (men into nursing and teaching) is much thinner. Messages that dwell on under-representation can weaken the effect [18]. |
| Mentoring | Keep capable students in a demanding course [19] | Quality varies. Pairing rules can themselves become a sex preference if they outlive the need. |
| Fairer gatekeeping (clear criteria, work samples, anonymised review, audits) | Stops a name or a stereotype standing in for a score [20] | Costs time. Does not, on its own, fix an earlier pipeline gap. A single hiring experiment should not be treated as a permanent measure of bias [21]. |
| Reading and school-engagement work for boys | Attacks the attainment gap that later looks like a "choice" not to enter higher education [6][10] | Poorly designed programmes can stereotype boys. Girls who still face barriers must not be pushed aside [11]. |
| Numeric targets and quotas | Can open a closed top tier and, as in Norway, raise the observable qualifications of those appointed [22] | Little evidence of spillover down the organisation [22]. Can outrun the qualified pool, attach a stigma to appointees, and treat 50/50 as a goal rather than a possible result of fair process. |

## 8. Recommendations

1. **Use the same rule in both directions.** A field should be open to whoever can do the work. Women's under-representation in engineering, computing and leadership, and men's under-representation in nursing, primary teaching and much of higher education, should be addressed with the same tools: widen the qualified pool, and judge people on the work.

2. **Treat school attainment as part of merit policy.** A large, persistent reading gap for boys, and higher rates of grade repetition and early leaving, are not side issues [6][10]. They decide who is later in a position to compete. Invest in reading, in school climates that do not treat study as unmasculine, and in an end to harsh discipline and bullying, without lowering standards.

3. **Show the work, not the deficit.** Outreach and role-model programmes should present the substance of the job and the path into it. The French evidence suggests that stressing under-representation is a weaker approach than making the career itself look real and attainable [18]. Build the same kind of evidence for men in nursing and primary teaching, where it is now scarce.

4. **Fix gatekeeping in a way that can be checked.** Pre-set criteria, structured review, work samples, anonymised assessment where it is feasible, and routine audits of like-for-like outcomes are the operational core of merit. They should protect women in laboratories and board searches, and men in nursing and teaching searches, on the same terms.

5. **Use numbers as a warning light, not as the destination.** Publish sex-disaggregated data on applications, offers, acceptances, completion and promotion. Investigate lopsided results. Do not set a 50/50 headcount as the definition of success, and do not let a target outrun the qualified pool. Where a quota is used to break a closed network, write it as a floor for each sex, watch qualifications, and review whether it has done anything beyond the seats it directly filled [22].

6. **Do not make this a contest between sexes.** The aim is more capable people in the jobs that need them, and fewer people pushed off a path they could have taken. That is a gain for women, for men, and for the work itself.

## 9. Limits, open questions and unverified items

This case could not settle several important questions.

- Whether the gender-equality paradox, once measured as a simple share of women among STEM graduates and with other equality indices, is a stable fact or an artefact of the GGGI and of a propensity ratio [15][16][17].
- How far differences in interest and relative academic strength would remain if stereotypes and biased gatekeeping were removed.
- Whether role-model and mentoring effects of the size found for girls in French STEM programmes [18] can be produced for boys in nursing and primary teaching.
- How much of the leadership gap is leftover unfairness in promotion, and how much is a pipeline that was already uneven at graduation.
- What happens to quality, morale and public trust when a numeric target is missed, or is met by stretching the standard.

**Unverified, or only partly checked, in this case:**

- The exact wording of the Stoet and Geary corrigendum [14]. The journal page blocked automated retrieval. The account given here follows the authors of the 2020 commentary and their accompanying explanation [15][16].
- The full text of the 2020 commentary as paginated in *Psychological Science* [16]. A public author copy was blocked on retrieval; the argument was read from the authors' own open explanation [15] and from the published bibliographic record.
- Exact retention and self-efficacy figures in Dennehy and Dasgupta (2017) [19]. The journal and PubMed Central pages were blocked on retrieval. Only the direction of the finding is used.
- The full results of later registered replications of Moss-Racusin and colleagues (2012), including work associated with Nathan Honeycutt, Lee Jussim and colleagues [21]. These have been reported as not reproducing the original male-favouring bias. That report was not confirmed from a final published paper for this case.
- Any global (as opposed to G20) share of women among all STEM graduates or STEM jobs from the UNESCO *Global Education Monitoring* gender report of 2024. The G20 figures in [1] were checked; a direct read of the GEM 2024 PDF was not obtained.
- Causal estimates for the national outreach schemes named by the OECD [2].
