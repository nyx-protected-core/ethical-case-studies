# Living together on one planet: pathways to closer cooperation with Iran, Russia, China and Saudi Arabia

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. Several of the precedents discussed here changed sharply in 2025 and 2026, and some are still moving. Facts are described as of mid-November 2026, from the sources listed at the end. Where governments disagree, each position is attributed to the government that holds it rather than presented as fact. Proposals marked "this study's own proposal" are suggestions made here. They are not positions of any cited author, institution or government. This study does not take the side of any government, including those of the four countries discussed or of the countries in dispute with them.*

## The ethical question

Iran, Russia, China and Saudi Arabia are very different countries. China is the world's second-largest military spender (SIPRI, 2026a). Russia is a nuclear superpower at war with Ukraine (UN General Assembly, 2022). Iran is under heavy sanctions and has been at war with the United States and Israel since February 2026 (IISS, 2026). Saudi Arabia is a close security partner of the United States that restored relations with Iran in 2023 (China, Saudi Arabia & Iran, 2023). What they share is that each is, for different reasons, in tension with large parts of the rest of the world, and each is home to people whose lives depend on how that tension is handled.

The question this study asks is:

**What realistic pathways could bring these four states into closer cooperative coexistence with the rest of the world, without asking anyone to stay silent about aggression or human rights abuses, and without punishing ordinary people for what their governments do?**

Three narrower questions follow:

1. When does trade and investment make conflict less likely, and when does it become a weapon?
2. Which forms of cooperation (science, health, food, climate, arms control) have survived deep rivalry in the past, and why?
3. How can sanctions and other pressure be designed so that they do not fall hardest on people who have no say over their government?

"Cooperative coexistence" here does not mean friendship, alliance or approval. It means a working relationship in which disputes are handled without war, shared dangers are managed together, and people in all these countries can live, trade, study and stay healthy.

## Current state

**Military spending is at a record high.** World military spending rose for the 11th year in a row in 2025, to $2,887 billion, or 2.5 percent of world GDP. SIPRI estimates that China spent about $336 billion (12 percent of the world total), Russia about $190 billion (7.5 percent of its GDP) and Saudi Arabia about $83.2 billion (6.5 percent of its GDP). Iran spent about $7.4 billion, down 5.6 percent from 2024. The United States remained the largest spender at $954 billion. SIPRI marks the figures for China, Russia and Saudi Arabia as estimates (SIPRI, 2026a).

**Nuclear arsenals are growing again.** SIPRI estimates that the world had about 12,187 nuclear warheads in January 2026, of which about 9,745 were in military stockpiles and about 4,012 were deployed. China had about 620 warheads and is expanding its arsenal faster than any other country. SIPRI describes states as relying more on nuclear weapons "as instruments of national power", reversing decades of effort to reduce their number and role (SIPRI, 2026b).

**The last US–Russia arms treaty has lapsed.** New START expired on 5 February 2026, leaving no legally binding limits on US and Russian strategic nuclear forces for the first time in decades. Russia's foreign minister said Russia would keep to the treaty's central limits informally for as long as the United States does. The United States said it wants a new, "modernized" treaty that also addresses China's arsenal. China's disarmament ambassador said China "will not take part in nuclear disarmament negotiations at this stage", restating China's long-standing view that the United States and Russia must cut first (Liang, 2026).

**The Iran nuclear deal has unravelled, and its legal status is disputed.** The 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) gave Iran sanctions relief in return for limits on its nuclear programme and a ten-year monitoring framework. The United States withdrew in 2018, and Iran then reduced its commitments. In September 2025, France, Germany and the United Kingdom said the "snapback" mechanism had restored earlier UN sanctions. Russia and China reject this and argue that the Security Council's consideration of the issue ended on 18 October 2025. Because of this split, the Council has been unable to choose a chair for the relevant sanctions committee or appoint its panel of experts (UN Security Council, 2026). This study does not take a side on that legal dispute.

**Nuclear inspectors cannot see what they need to see in Iran.** The head of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) told the Security Council in October 2026 that inspectors had to withdraw for safety reasons after military attacks began in June 2025, and that Iran has not provided the information and access needed to verify previously declared nuclear material. Russia's representative disputed this account and said Iran had continued to engage with the IAEA (UN Security Council, 2026). Under the JCPOA, by contrast, the IAEA had verified in January 2016 that Iran was not enriching above 3.67 percent and held no more than 300 kg of low-enriched uranium (IAEA, 2016).

**The Gulf is at war.** The US–Israeli war with Iran began on 28 February 2026. According to the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), Iran's strikes quickly widened from US bases to civilian and dual-use targets across the Gulf states, and most Gulf states have reportedly responded militarily, although most have not confirmed it officially. IISS calls this "the first instance of large-scale direct military confrontation between the Gulf states and Iran" (IISS, 2026).

**Russia's war in Ukraine continues.** In March 2022, 141 UN member states voted for a General Assembly resolution demanding that Russia withdraw its forces from Ukraine; 5 voted against and 35 abstained (UN General Assembly, 2022). The war continued through 2025, when SIPRI estimates Ukraine spent about 40 percent of its GDP on its military (SIPRI, 2026a).

**The economic cost of splitting the world is large.** An International Monetary Fund staff study estimated that severe fragmentation of world trade could cost up to about 7 percent of global output in the long run, with some countries losing far more if technology flows are also cut (Aiyar et al., 2023).

**Sanctions are more common than ever.** Economists tracking sanctions since 1945 describe "a remarkable increase" in their use as a foreign-policy tool (Morgan et al., 2023), and the Global Sanctions Data Base was built to measure that growth across countries and decades (Felbermayr et al., 2020).

**The four countries matter for the climate.** Using the Global Carbon Budget's national data, China, Russia, Iran and Saudi Arabia together produced about 40 percent of the world's fossil carbon dioxide emissions in 2024: China about 31.8 percent, Russia about 4.6 percent, Iran about 2.1 percent and Saudi Arabia about 1.8 percent (Friedlingstein et al., 2026; shares calculated for this study from the published national data). No serious climate plan can succeed without them.

## Root causes of distrust

### The security dilemma

When one state arms to feel safe, its neighbours often feel less safe and arm in turn, even if no one wants war. Jervis (1978) showed that this "security dilemma" is worst when defensive and offensive weapons look alike and when it is hard to tell what the other side intends. Kydd (2005) adds that what matters is whether each side believes the other is a "security seeker" or an expansionist. Reassurance can work, but only through costly signals the other side can believe.

### Arms control is hard to verify, and that is why it is rare

Coe and Vaynman (2019) argue that the main barrier to arms control is monitoring. Monitoring must make a state's weapons transparent enough to prove it is keeping its word, but not so transparent that it exposes the state to attack. They estimate that arms races driven by this problem account for almost 40 percent of all global arming over the past two centuries. Iran today is a sharp example: without inspector access, no one outside the Iranian government can confirm what has happened to its enriched uranium (UN Security Council, 2026).

### Broken promises and competing memories

Each government has its own account of who broke faith first. Western governments point to Russia's invasion of Ukraine and to Iran's reduced nuclear commitments. Russia points to NATO enlargement after the Cold War, a dispute that historians still argue about (Sarotte, 2021). Iran points to the United States leaving the JCPOA in 2018 while Iran was, according to the IAEA, meeting its limits (IAEA, 2016; UN Security Council, 2026). These accounts cannot all be fully right, but each is sincerely held by many people, and any lasting settlement has to address them rather than dismiss them.

### Economic ties can be turned into weapons

Farrell and Newman (2019) show that states sitting at the centre of global financial and information networks, such as the dollar payment system and the SWIFT messaging network, can use those positions to watch others (a "panopticon effect") or to cut them off (a "chokepoint effect"). Targets learn from this. A Chatham House study finds that the growing number of Western sanctions has itself pushed China, Russia, North Korea and Iran into closer cooperation with one another (Chatham House, 2025).

### Human rights abuses deepen the gap

Distrust is not only about weapons. United Nations bodies have documented grave abuses in all four countries (see "Human rights" below). For many people elsewhere, closer ties with governments that commit such abuses feel like complicity. For the governments concerned, outside criticism is often seen as interference. This gap is real and cannot be wished away.

## What has worked historically

### Détente and the Helsinki Final Act (1975)

During the Cold War, the Soviet Union and Western states signed the Helsinki Final Act. The Soviet side wanted recognition of post-war borders. Western states insisted on including human rights commitments. Thomas (2001) shows that those human rights commitments, which many saw as empty words at the time, were taken up by citizen monitoring groups inside the Soviet bloc and by networks abroad, and became a lasting source of pressure for change. Snyder (2011) traces how this transnational "Helsinki network" kept human rights on the agenda of East–West diplomacy.

**Lesson:** a cooperation agreement can include human rights without collapsing, if each side also gets something it values and if independent civil society can use the text.

### Scientists talking across the Iron Curtain

Evangelista (1999) documents how unofficial contacts between Soviet and Western scientists, for example through the Pugwash Conferences, carried ideas about arms restraint into Soviet policy circles, ideas that later shaped real arms reductions.

**Lesson:** unofficial expert channels can keep ideas alive when official diplomacy is frozen.

### Track II diplomacy

"Track II" means informal dialogue among scholars, retired officials, religious leaders and others who can speak more freely than serving diplomats. Jones (2015) describes how such dialogue can feed new ideas into official talks, while warning that officials often misunderstand what it can do. A RAND study of Track II security dialogues in the Middle East and South Asia found them most useful as a slow process of shaping how elites think, rather than as a route to quick breakthroughs (Kaye, 2007). Rigorous measurement of Track II's effects is thin, so claims about it should be modest.

### Rivals cooperating on health: smallpox

In 1958 the Soviet Union tabled a proposal at the World Health Assembly for a worldwide smallpox eradication programme. It passed after intense diplomatic negotiation, and the effort later drew in a US-supported programme in western Africa (Bhattacharya & Campani, 2019). The disease was eventually eradicated. The same historians caution that the real story is more complex and contested than the usual heroic version.

**Lesson:** rivals can work together on a shared enemy that threatens everyone.

### The JCPOA (2015): verification can work while political will lasts

For several years the JCPOA did what it promised on paper. The IAEA verified in January 2016 that Iran's enrichment level and stockpile were within the agreed limits and installed equipment for continuous monitoring (IAEA, 2016). The deal later unravelled for political reasons, not because inspection failed (UN Security Council, 2026).

**Lesson:** technical verification can be trusted; the hard part is keeping the political bargain alive across changes of government on all sides.

### The 2023 Saudi–Iran agreement

After seven years without diplomatic relations, Saudi Arabia and Iran agreed in Beijing in March 2023 to restore relations and reopen embassies within two months, affirm respect for each other's sovereignty and non-interference, and revive a 2001 security cooperation agreement. Their joint statement with China thanked Iraq and Oman for hosting earlier rounds of talks in 2021 and 2022 (China, Saudi Arabia & Iran, 2023). Those rounds were official contacts, not strictly Track II, but they show how quiet mediation by neighbours can prepare the ground for a public deal.

The agreement's record is mixed and still contested:

- *Evidence it endured:* a Carnegie analysis published in November 2026 says the diplomatic framework has survived even though the current war draws Saudi Arabia in on the side of the United States, and that Saudi and Iranian officials reaffirmed it in 2026 (Alghannam, 2026). IISS similarly says that before 2026 the de-escalation survived even though the agreement's terms were not fully implemented (IISS, 2026).
- *Evidence of its limits:* IISS reports that Riyadh carried out limited retaliatory strikes against Iran and Iran-backed militias in March 2026, after attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure, and argues that Iran disregarded its diplomatic arrangements with Gulf states during both the 2025 and 2026 wars (IISS, 2026). Carnegie notes that Saudi Arabia is now pursuing diplomacy and deterrence together, including a 2026 defence pact with Türkiye and Pakistan that could raise tensions with Tehran if it becomes an anti-Iran alliance (Alghannam, 2026).

**Lesson:** a channel kept open between rivals is worth a great deal in a crisis, even if it cannot stop a war on its own.

### Arctic science cooperation

The Arctic Council brings together the eight Arctic states, including Russia, and six Indigenous peoples' organisations. In March 2022 the other seven Arctic states paused their participation in Council meetings after Russia invaded Ukraine, while restating the Council's "enduring value" (Canada et al., 2022). Working-level project work resumed through written procedures in August 2023, and virtual working-group meetings resumed gradually from February 2024, while diplomatic-level meetings stayed paused (Arctic Council, 2024). In February 2026 the chair of the Senior Arctic Officials said political-level meetings were still paused, but scientific and working-group meetings continued with all states, and linked this resilience to the founding decision to keep security and defence outside the Council's remit (Arctic Council, 2026). Koivurova and Shibata (2023) examined which Arctic arrangements kept working and tested whether treaty-based cooperation, such as the Central Arctic Ocean fisheries agreement and the Arctic science cooperation agreement, held up better than soft-law forums. They report, for example, that the fisheries agreement's parties, including Russia, adopted rules of procedure by consensus in November 2022. That 2018 agreement, whose parties include Canada, China, Denmark (for the Faroe Islands and Greenland), Iceland, Japan, Korea, Norway, Russia, the United States and the European Union, commits them to prevent unregulated commercial fishing in the newly ice-free central Arctic high seas while joint scientific research proceeds, for an initial period of 16 years (Agreement to Prevent Unregulated High Seas Fisheries in the Central Arctic Ocean, 2018). It is a rare example of a precautionary deal reached *before* a resource conflict could start, and it includes both China and Russia. (This study did not confirm from a primary source when the agreement entered into force or how it has been carried out since 2022.)

**Lesson:** cooperation built on binding, specific, technical commitments may survive political shocks better than cooperation built on goodwill alone.

### Shared science facilities

- **ITER**, the fusion energy experiment being built in France, counts China, the European Union, India, Japan, Korea, Russia and the United States as members in a decades-long collaboration (ITER Organization, n.d.).
- **SESAME**, a synchrotron light source in Jordan, has Cyprus, Egypt, Iran, Israel, Jordan, Pakistan, Palestine and Türkiye as members, with China among its observers (SESAME, n.d.). Its members include states in deep dispute, such as Iran and Israel, sharing one research machine.

**Caution:** these projects show that "low-politics" cooperation is possible and can survive rivalry. The sources reviewed here do not show that it reduces rivalry on security issues.

### Climate and water

**US–China climate statements.** In 2014 the United States and China announced climate targets together: the United States set an economy-wide target of 26–28 percent below its 2005 emissions in 2025, and China said it intended to peak carbon dioxide emissions around 2030. They said they hoped to "inject momentum into the global climate negotiations" (The White House, 2014). In 2023, the Sunnylands statement set up a joint working group on energy transition, methane, circular economy, subnational action and deforestation, committed both countries to economy-wide 2035 climate targets covering all greenhouse gases, and even promised to "facilitate track II activities" (U.S. Department of State, 2023). These ties proved only as durable as domestic politics: the United States notified its withdrawal from the Paris Agreement on 27 January 2025, effective 27 January 2026 (UN Secretary-General, 2025). This study could not confirm whether the bilateral working group continues in any form.

**Saudi Arabia in climate talks.** A 2008 study described Saudi Arabia as a long-standing "obstructionist" in climate negotiations, taking part in order to slow or block agreement, while asking whether it might be moving beyond that role (Depledge, 2008). That study is old. Saudi Arabia's positions at recent climate conferences, and the results of its regional green initiatives, were not verified for this study, so no claim is made about them.

**Shared rivers.** A study of every reported event of water conflict and cooperation between nations over 50 years found that physical scarcity is only weakly linked to disputes. Institutions such as river-basin bodies and treaties matter as much or more, and most tension arises when rapid change outpaces institutions (Wolf et al., 2003). But "cooperation" between unequal neighbours is not always fair: the hydro-hegemony framework shows how treaties and knowledge production can also serve a stronger upstream state's control of a river (Zeitoun & Warner, 2006). This study did not examine specific basins shared by these four countries and their neighbours, so it makes no claim about how fair or effective those arrangements are.

**Does environmental cooperation build peace?** The first comprehensive review of the evidence found that environmental cooperation can help reduce violence within states and bring "symbolic rapprochement" within and between states, mainly by building understanding, trust and especially institutions. The same review found it unlikely to produce deep integration between states, and its effects depend strongly on context (Ide, 2019).

**Lesson:** climate, water and Arctic cooperation is best seen as a resilient floor that keeps channels open, not a proven route to security trust.

### Food in wartime: the Black Sea Grain Initiative

Even during the war in Ukraine, a UN- and Türkiye-brokered deal allowed about 32.8 million tonnes of grain and other food to leave Ukrainian ports between August 2022 and mid-July 2023, when Russia ended its participation (United Nations, 2023).

**Lesson:** narrow humanitarian deals can be made between states at war, but they need constant tending and can be withdrawn.

## Ethical tensions

### 1. Does trade make peace? The evidence is genuinely split

- *For:* using data from 1885 to 1992, Oneal and Russett (1999) found that democracy, economic interdependence and international organisations each significantly reduce the chance of militarised disputes.
- *Against:* using data from 1870 to 1938, Barbieri (1996) found that extensive interdependence *increased* the likelihood of militarised disputes, though it had no effect on whether full wars occurred, and that low to moderate interdependence may reduce disputes while very high interdependence may increase them.
- *Conditional:* Copeland (2014) argues that what matters is expectations. Leaders who expect trade to continue have reasons to keep the peace. Leaders who fear being cut off have reasons to act first.
- *Recent experience:* IISS notes that during the 2026 war Iran directed most of its strikes at the United Arab Emirates, its second-largest trading partner and home to over half a million Iranian expatriates, which IISS reads as showing the limits of relying on economic incentives alone (IISS, 2026).

This study treats the question as open. Trade is most likely to help when partners expect access to be stable and fair, and least likely to help when it is used as a lever.

### 2. Sanctions: pressure on governments, harm to people

Sanctions are meant to change what governments do without war. The ethical problem is that their costs often fall on people with no power.

- Rodríguez, Rendón and Weisbrot (2025), studying 152 countries from 1971 to 2021, estimated that unilateral sanctions were associated with about 564,000 deaths a year, a toll comparable to armed conflict, with children under five most affected. They found the strongest effects for unilateral, economic and US sanctions, and no statistical evidence of an effect for UN sanctions. The paper states that it was funded by the Center for Economic and Policy Research, which readers may wish to weigh.
- Gutmann, Neuenkirch and Neumeier (2021), using a different method and sample, found the opposite ranking: an average episode of UN sanctions reduced life expectancy by about 1.2 to 1.4 years, compared with 0.4 to 0.5 years for US sanctions, and women were affected more severely than men.
- Peksen (2009) found that economic sanctions tend to *worsen* governments' respect for physical integrity rights, such as freedom from torture and political imprisonment, and that broad sanctions are more harmful than targeted ones.
- Drezner (2011) reviews "smart" targeted sanctions aimed at leaders and elites, and finds that they reduce some humanitarian costs but are not a cure-all.

These studies disagree about which sanctions hurt most, but they agree that broad economic sanctions carry serious humanitarian costs. In 2022 the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 2664, a broad humanitarian exception to UN financial sanctions. Chatham House calls it a turning point, but notes that it does not cover sanctions imposed by individual states or the European Union, and that banks often "de-risk" by refusing lawful humanitarian payments anyway (Gillard, 2022).

The tension: dropping sanctions entirely could reward aggression and abuse. Keeping broad sanctions in place could cost many innocent lives. An ethical approach tries to keep pressure on decision-makers while keeping food, medicine and humanitarian payments flowing.

### 3. Human rights: engage without excusing

UN bodies have documented serious abuses in all four countries:

- **China:** the UN Human Rights Office found in 2022 that serious human rights violations had been committed in Xinjiang, and that the extent of arbitrary and discriminatory detention of Uyghurs and other mostly Muslim groups "may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity" (OHCHR, 2022). The Chinese government's response disputing the assessment is annexed to the same report (OHCHR, 2022).
- **Iran:** a UN fact-finding mission concluded in 2024 that the repression of the 2022 "Woman, Life, Freedom" protests involved crimes against humanity, including murder, imprisonment, torture, rape and gender persecution (UN Human Rights Council, 2024). The UN High Commissioner reported that at least 1,500 people were reportedly executed in Iran in 2025 (OHCHR, 2026).
- **Russia:** the UN Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine found in 2025 that enforced disappearances and torture by Russian authorities amount to crimes against humanity (UN Human Rights Council, 2025).
- **Saudi Arabia:** the UN High Commissioner reported that at least 356 people were reportedly executed in 2025, a new record, 78 percent of them for drug-related offences, including at least two people convicted of crimes committed as children (OHCHR, 2026).

The same UN statement also criticised executions in the United States, which carried out 47 in 2025, the most in 16 years (OHCHR, 2026). Human rights standards apply to every government, including those that criticise others. Applying them evenly is part of what makes them credible.

The ethical tension is between two duties: not to legitimise abuse, and not to cut off the contact through which abuses are sometimes reduced. The Helsinki experience suggests both can be held at once (Thomas, 2001; Snyder, 2011).

### 4. Who speaks for the people?

Governments negotiate, but people live with the results. Citizens of these four countries are not their governments. Survivors of abuse, exiles, workers, scientists and ordinary families each have a stake. Cooperation that only serves governments may entrench the very abuses that cause distrust.

### 5. Sovereignty and accountability

Every state has a right to its own political system, and outside pressure has sometimes been used for self-interested ends. But sovereignty does not cover crimes against humanity or invading a neighbour. Holding both ideas at once is hard, and every government involved has been accused of double standards.

## Original proposals (this study's own)

These are this study's own suggestions. They are offered for discussion and are not endorsed by any cited source.

### Proposal 1: A "humanitarian channel guarantee" for every sanctions regime (this study's own proposal)

Building on UN Resolution 2664 (Gillard, 2022), every sanctioning state and the European Union would publish a standing humanitarian exception covering food, medicine, medical equipment, agricultural inputs and payments for them, with a named public bank channel that is legally protected from penalties. Each regime would publish an annual humanitarian impact review using the kinds of measures researchers already use, such as child mortality and life expectancy (Rodríguez et al., 2025; Gutmann et al., 2021). Sanctions that fail the review would be redesigned toward individual decision-makers.

### Proposal 2: Verification first, as a confidence-building ladder (this study's own proposal)

Because monitoring is the main obstacle to arms control (Coe & Vaynman, 2019), start with small steps that increase transparency without exposing military secrets: data exchanges on deployed strategic forces, notification of missile launches, and joint technical work on how to verify warhead numbers. For Iran, the immediate step is restoring IAEA access to declared material, which the IAEA says is the foundation for any diplomatic agreement (UN Security Council, 2026). Each step would be paired with a reciprocal, verifiable step by the other side.

### Proposal 3: Regional "neighbour" channels that stay open in wartime (this study's own proposal)

The Iraq- and Oman-hosted talks before the 2023 Saudi–Iran agreement (China, Saudi Arabia & Iran, 2023) and the survival of that channel through war (Alghannam, 2026) suggest a model: standing, low-profile channels between neighbours, hosted by trusted third countries, with a written commitment that they continue during crises. Their agenda would start with practical shared risks such as maritime incidents, attacks on civilian infrastructure, prisoner exchanges and the safety of nuclear facilities.

### Proposal 4: A "shared dangers" science and health compact (this study's own proposal)

Using the ITER, SESAME and smallpox models (ITER Organization, n.d.; SESAME, n.d.; Bhattacharya & Campani, 2019), create a protected list of cooperation that continues regardless of political disputes: pandemic surveillance and vaccine research under the WHO Pandemic Agreement adopted in 2025 (WHO, 2025); Arctic and climate observation (Arctic Council, 2024; Koivurova & Shibata, 2023); and seed, crop and water research. Projects would be bound by specific, treaty-like commitments rather than goodwill alone, following the Arctic experience in which binding technical agreements kept working after political meetings stopped (Koivurova & Shibata, 2023; Arctic Council, 2026). Funding would come from several sources, so that no single government can switch it off.

### Proposal 5: Human rights clauses with independent monitors (this study's own proposal)

Following the Helsinki model (Thomas, 2001; Snyder, 2011), any new trade, investment or security framework with these states would include human rights commitments that apply to *all* parties, with an independent monitoring body and a public reporting process that citizens and civil society can use. Commitments would be specific and measurable, such as ending executions for drug offences and for crimes committed as children (OHCHR, 2026), or allowing UN investigators access.

### Proposal 6: Trade rules that make access predictable (this study's own proposal)

If Copeland (2014) is right that expectations of future trade matter most, then predictable rules matter more than trade volume. This would mean clear, published conditions under which trade restrictions would be lifted; no surprise measures on food, medicine or humanitarian goods; and dispute settlement through recognised institutions. It also means accepting that some technology and security restrictions will remain, but stating them openly and narrowly, to limit the fragmentation costs estimated by the IMF (Aiyar et al., 2023).

### Proposal 7: Climate as a shared project (this study's own proposal)

Because these four countries together produce about 40 percent of fossil carbon dioxide emissions (Friedlingstein et al., 2026, with this study's calculation), invite them into joint, verifiable projects with measurable results, such as methane leak detection and reduction in oil and gas, grid connections for renewable power, and shared climate data. These projects can create mutual benefit even when wider relations are poor. The evidence suggests environmental cooperation can build institutions and symbolic rapprochement but rarely deep integration (Ide, 2019), and that bilateral climate pledges can be reversed by domestic politics (UN Secretary-General, 2025). So this proposal should be tested, not assumed to work, and it should be backed by binding commitments rather than pledges alone.

## Risks

- **Legitimising abuse.** Closer ties could be used by governments as proof of acceptance while repression continues. Human rights clauses with real monitoring (Proposal 5) are meant to reduce this risk, but cannot remove it.
- **Rewarding aggression.** Easing pressure on a state that has invaded a neighbour, or that blocks nuclear inspectors, could encourage more of the same. Every step should be tied to verifiable action.
- **Dependence as a weapon.** Deeper economic ties create new chokepoints that can be used for coercion by either side (Farrell & Newman, 2019).
- **Track II as cover.** Informal channels can be used for propaganda or intelligence gathering. Participants need clear rules and honest expectations (Jones, 2015; Kaye, 2007).
- **Science projects as technology transfer.** Shared research can raise real dual-use and security concerns. Projects should be chosen for open, civilian purposes.
- **Deals that collapse.** The JCPOA, New START and the Black Sea Grain Initiative all ended or unravelled (UN Security Council, 2026; Liang, 2026; United Nations, 2023). Broken agreements can deepen distrust and make the next one harder.
- **Uncertain evidence.** The research on trade and peace and on sanctions' human costs is contested. Policies built on it should be reviewed regularly and changed when evidence changes.
- **Bad faith on any side.** None of these pathways work if a government is determined to dominate or to destroy another. Cooperation is a tool for managing rivalry, not a guarantee against it.

## Recommendation

There is no single key that unlocks cooperation with Iran, Russia, China and Saudi Arabia. History suggests something slower and more practical: keep channels open, verify rather than trust, protect people from the costs of disputes between governments, and keep human rights on the table for everyone.

In order of urgency, this study recommends:

1. **Protect people first.** Make humanitarian exceptions to all sanctions real and usable, and measure their human costs every year (Proposal 1).
2. **Restore verification.** Get IAEA inspectors back to Iran's declared material and begin rebuilding US–Russia transparency, inviting China into risk-reduction talks even if it refuses to join arms reduction talks yet (Proposal 2).
3. **Keep neighbour channels open in war.** Strengthen the kind of quiet regional mediation that produced the 2023 Saudi–Iran agreement (Proposal 3).
4. **Protect shared science, health, food and climate work** from political shocks through binding, specific commitments (Proposals 4 and 7).
5. **Hold every government to the same human rights standards**, with independent monitoring built into any new framework (Proposal 5).

Ending hostility is beyond any one study. Reducing the harm it does to ordinary people, and keeping open the paths that have worked before, is within reach.
