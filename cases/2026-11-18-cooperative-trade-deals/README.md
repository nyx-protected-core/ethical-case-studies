# Cooperative Trade Deals, Peace and Poverty: A Research Case

*A research note on how trade agreements have helped, and sometimes failed, to reduce conflict and poverty, and on what a more cooperative global trade deal could look like. It is research and writing only. Figures and findings are taken from the sources listed at the end. Each DOI was checked against the Crossref registry and each link was opened on 18 November 2026.*

---

## 1. Which trade agreements have reduced conflict and poverty, and how

The evidence is stronger for some claims than for others. It is clearest in three areas.

**Peace between former enemies: the European Coal and Steel Community.** The Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950 proposed putting French and German coal and steel production under a common High Authority. The aim was to make war between the two "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible" [24]. The plan was more than tariff cuts. Coal and steel moved between members free of customs duty, and the proposal included a production and investment plan, price-equalising machinery and "a restructuring fund to facilitate the rationalization of production" [24]. The six founding members were France, West Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg. The ECSC was the first of the supranational institutions that became the European Union [24]. Two lessons carry over: the deal targeted the industries of war directly, and it budgeted for the people who would bear the cost of change.

**Poverty reduction through market access: the US–Vietnam Bilateral Trade Agreement (2001).** A careful study used the fact that the US tariff cuts were not shaped by Vietnamese industries. It found that Vietnamese provinces more exposed to those cuts saw faster falls in poverty between 2002 and 2004, and faster wage growth for workers with low levels of education [17]. It also found that workers moved between provinces only on a limited scale, especially the less educated. Gains therefore depended on where people already lived [17].

**Labour conditions linked to market access: the US–Cambodia Textile Agreement.** This agreement gave Cambodia higher garment export quotas into the US market in return for better working conditions and labour regulation, with the ILO acting as the compliance monitor [16]. Brand-conscious buyers came to rely on the ILO's public factory reports, which helped the scheme continue after the global garment quota system ended in 2004 [16]. The monitoring programme, now Better Factories Cambodia, started in 2001. It reports more than 660 participating factories and over 645,000 workers, about 80 per cent of them women [21]. The programme reports that its growth coincided with a 17 per cent fall in the national garment-sector gender pay gap, and that participation raises average factory productivity by 26 to 31 per cent [21]. These are the programme's own figures, not independent estimates.

**Projected gains, not yet realised: the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA).** The World Bank's 2020 study estimates that full implementation could lift an additional 30 million people out of extreme poverty and 68 million out of moderate poverty by 2035. It projects a 7 per cent rise in regional income, nearly US$450 billion [19][20]. Only about US$153 billion of that comes from cutting tariffs and non-tariff barriers. The remaining US$292 billion would come from trade facilitation: less red tape, lower compliance costs and easier customs [20]. The same study projects slightly larger wage gains for women (10.5 per cent) than for men (9.9 per cent) [20]. These figures are model projections that depend on implementation, not measured outcomes.

**A caution from the same evidence base.** Liberalisation can also slow poverty reduction in the places most exposed to it. After India's 1991 trade liberalisation, rural districts more exposed to tariff cuts saw slower declines in poverty. The effect was worst among the least mobile people at the bottom of the income distribution, and in states where rigid labour laws slowed movement between sectors [18]. In the United States, rising Chinese import competition from 1990 to 2007 raised unemployment and lowered wages in exposed local labour markets. It explains about one quarter of the decline in US manufacturing employment over that period [15]. Cross-country work finds that, on average, faster growth in globalising economies raised the incomes of the poor in proportion [14]. Averages, however, hide who loses.

## 2. Trade interdependence and peace: the evidence and its critics

### The commercial peace

- **The opportunity-cost argument.** An early economic model argued that the mutual dependence trade creates raises the cost of conflict, since conflict puts the gains from trade at risk [1].
- **Large-sample support.** One influential study tested whether interdependence and democracy both reduce interstate conflict over 1950–1985, and reported that the classical liberals were right on both counts [2]. A later re-analysis responded to critics who said trade is itself shaped by conflict. Using simultaneous equations, and adding the "gravity model" factors of national size and distance, it found that trade does promote peace, while conflict also reduces trade [6].
- **The capitalist peace.** Economic development, free markets and similar interests between states all predict fewer militarised disputes. This account also explains much of the effect usually credited to democracy in statistical tests of the democratic peace [3].
- **Bilateral versus global openness.** A widely cited study found that two countries that trade more with each other are less likely to fight. Countries more open to global trade, however, are more likely to go to war, because many trading partners reduce their dependence on any single one [4]. The study used data on military conflicts from 1950 to 2000. A follow-up found that country pairs with more past wars are more likely to sign regional trade agreements, the more so when the trade gains are larger. Governments appear to use such agreements partly as peace tools [5].

### The institutional finding

PTA here means a preferential trade agreement, such as a free trade area, common market or customs union. Post-war evidence shows that trade flows have relatively little effect on disputes between states outside the same PTA. Within PTAs, however, there is a strong inverse relationship between trade and conflict, and members are less likely to fight than other states [10]. The institution matters, not just the volume of trade.

### The critics

- **Interdependence can raise conflict.** A study of 1870–1938, covering 14,341 dyad-years with 270 militarised disputes and 14 wars, found that extensive economic interdependence increased the likelihood of militarised disputes, though it had no effect on whether wars occurred [7]. A dyad is a pair of countries. The relationship looked curvilinear: low to moderate interdependence reduced disputes, while extreme interdependence, whether symmetric or asymmetric, raised them [7].
- **Expectations matter.** The trade-expectations theory argues that high dependence is pacifying when states expect trade to continue. When they expect it to be cut off, the same dependence can make war attractive, as a way to secure vital goods [8].
- **Weaponised interdependence.** Global economic networks are highly asymmetric. States with jurisdiction over central hubs, such as the SWIFT financial messaging system, can use a "panopticon effect" to gather intelligence and a "chokepoint effect" to deny adversaries access [9]. Interdependence can be a means of coercion, not only a restraint on it.

**Summary.** The best reading of the evidence is conditional. Trade tends to reduce conflict when the ties are balanced, embedded in institutions such as PTAs, and expected to last. It can fail, or be turned into a weapon, when dependence is lopsided or the future of trade looks uncertain.

## 3. Regional blocs

### EU and ECSC: the founding example

The ECSC is described in Section 1. Its design features, a supranational authority, a sector chosen for its link to war, and a restructuring fund for adjustment, remain the clearest template for using trade as a peace instrument [24].

### ASEAN: peace without a strong trade mechanism

A study of the "long peace" among ASEAN members from 1968 to 1994 contrasts it with the earlier, violent Malaysian confrontation. It attributes the peace mainly to a shared sense of common interests, norms and identity, built among non-democratic governments [12]. It does not attribute the peace to trade or democracy. The lesson is that regional peace can come from diplomatic community-building as much as from commerce, and that trade bloc membership may be a sign of cooperation as well as a cause of it.

### Mercosur: rapprochement first, then a trade bloc with a democratic clause

Argentina and Brazil moved from long rivalry to cooperation. Research dates this security cooperation to the late 1970s, when a 1979–80 settlement of disputes over hydroelectric power and nuclear technology ended a long militarised competition [13]. Mercosur came later, so in this case trade integration built on a political settlement rather than producing it. The Ushuaia Protocol, signed on 24 July 1998, makes democracy a condition of membership [25]:

- Article 1 states that full operation of democratic institutions is an essential condition for integration.
- Article 4 requires consultations if democratic order breaks down in a member state.
- Article 5 provides for measures ranging from suspension of the right to take part in Mercosur bodies to suspension of the rights and obligations arising from integration.

### AfCFTA: the largest test ahead

The AfCFTA's projected effects on poverty are set out in Section 1 [19][20]. Most of the projected gains depend on border and customs reforms rather than tariff cuts. Whether the agreement also reduces conflict is an open empirical question. The PTA literature [10] suggests it could, if the institutions are real and trade between members actually grows.

## 4. Design features

### Labour clauses

- **How common they are.** The ILO's 2016 assessment found that nearly half of the trade agreements concluded in the previous five years contained a labour chapter or provision referring to international labour standards [22].
- **What they achieve.** The same assessment found evidence that labour provisions raise awareness, improve labour laws, involve stakeholders, and build domestic institutions for monitoring and enforcement. It also found remaining challenges in sustaining impacts, coherence and cooperation [22].
- **Hard versus soft standards.** A study of 177 states from 1972 to 2002 found that PTAs with "hard" human-rights standards, which tie market benefits to compliance, improved government human-rights behaviour. "Soft" standards and standalone human-rights treaties did not systematically do so [11].
- **The Cambodia model.** Market-access rewards, credible public monitoring by a neutral body, and buyer pressure together achieved more than any one of them would have alone [16][21].

### Development and special-and-differential provisions

WTO rules already give developing and least-developed countries differential treatment. Examples include longer timeframes for least-developed countries to implement the TRIPS intellectual-property agreement, technical assistance, and a waiver allowing preferential tariffs for least-developed countries' products, now extended to 30 June 2029 [23]. The AfCFTA projections suggest that the most valuable development provisions may be trade-facilitation support and adjustment help, not only tariff preferences [20].

### Dispute settlement

The WTO Appellate Body is a standing body of seven members that hears appeals from panel reports. It is currently unable to review appeals because its seats are vacant; the last sitting member's term expired on 30 November 2020 [26]. Without a working appeal stage, a losing party can appeal "into the void", and rulings stay unenforced. A cooperative deal needs a dispute system that both large and small members trust, because the peace benefits of PTAs depend on the institution being credible [10].

## 5. Proposal: what a more cooperative global trade deal could include

This sketches the elements of a "cooperative trade compact", each tied to the evidence above. It is a framework for discussion, not a legal text.

1. **Market access with facilitation finance.** Duty-free, quota-free access for least-developed countries, plus funded trade-facilitation programmes (customs, border infrastructure, digital paperwork). This draws on the AfCFTA finding that most gains come from trade facilitation [20] and the Vietnam evidence on market access and poverty [17].
2. **Incentive-based labour chapters with neutral monitoring.** Reward verified improvement with extra access, as in the Cambodia model [16]. Publish factory-level monitoring by a neutral body such as the ILO. Make core labour standards enforceable rather than aspirational [11][22].
3. **A forced-labour pillar.** Forced labour in the private economy generates an estimated US$236 billion in illegal profits a year, and 27.6 million people were in forced labour on any given day in 2021 [27]. The compact would require supply-chain due diligence, joint labour inspection in high-risk sectors, and remedies for victims, including back wages. Import restrictions would be a last resort, paired with remediation so that workers are not simply dismissed.
4. **An adjustment fund.** A pooled fund for the regions and workers who lose from liberalisation, financed by the winners. It would offer wage insurance, retraining and mobility support. The ECSC's restructuring fund [24] is the precedent, and the evidence from India and the United States [15][18] shows why it is needed.
5. **A graduated rights and democracy clause.** Consultations first, then proportionate suspension, modelled on the Ushuaia Protocol [25]. It would be applied by consensus, with clear criteria for lifting measures.
6. **Restored two-tier dispute settlement.** A working appeal stage with fixed timelines, plus a fast-track mechanism for labour and forced-labour complaints [26].
7. **Safeguards against weaponisation.** Diversify critical chokepoints, give notice and set time limits for economic coercion, and protect humanitarian trade from sanctions. This responds to the risks identified in the weaponised-interdependence work [9].
8. **Time-bound special treatment with review.** Differential provisions [23] tied to development benchmarks and reviewed regularly, rather than permanent categories.

### Trade-offs

- **Standards versus access.** Strict labour or rights conditions can become disguised protectionism, shutting poor countries out of the markets that reduce poverty [17]. Incentives and capacity-building are safer than bans alone.
- **Depth versus breadth.** Deep institutions such as PTAs with credible dispute settlement appear to carry the peace effect [10]. Deep commitments are harder to agree globally, so a compact may need a common core plus optional deeper modules.
- **Global openness versus bilateral ties.** Wider multilateral openness can weaken the pacifying effect of any single bilateral relationship [4]. This argues for building dense regional ties inside the global framework.
- **Who bears adjustment costs.** Gains are spread widely, while losses are concentrated in particular places and among the least mobile workers [15][17][18]. Without compensation, the losers can be large and politically decisive.

### Ethical risks

- **Harm to the workers meant to be protected.** A sudden import ban on goods linked to forced labour can push victims into worse work or debt bondage elsewhere. Remediation, back pay and protection for victims must come first [27].
- **Survivor safety and data.** Supply-chain audits and grievance channels collect sensitive information on workers. Disclosure must not expose them to retaliation by traffickers or employers.
- **Sovereignty and coercion.** Rights conditions can be applied selectively by powerful states. The same mechanism that pressures abusers can be used to coerce rivals [9].
- **Uneven power at the table.** Large economies write most of the rules. Without development provisions and a fair dispute system, a "cooperative" deal can lock in existing advantages.
- **Measurement.** Forced labour is hidden. Estimates rely on surveys and models [27], so measuring progress is hard and open to manipulation.

## 6. Limitations and open questions

- **Causation is difficult.** Peaceful pairs of countries may trade more and sign agreements because they are already at peace. Strong studies use instruments and simultaneous models to address this [4][6], but the debate continues [7][8].
- **Case selection.** The ECSC, Cambodia and Vietnam are well-known successes. Failures are less studied, so the record may look better than it is.
- **Projections are not outcomes.** The AfCFTA poverty figures are model estimates that depend on reforms that have not yet been made [19][20].
- **Self-reported programme results.** Some Better Factories Cambodia figures come from the programme itself [21] and should be independently confirmed.
- **ASEAN and Mercosur cut against a simple trade story.** In both, political settlement and shared identity came before, or alongside, trade integration [12][13].
- **Enforcement under geopolitical rivalry.** Can a global compact work while the WTO appeal stage is paralysed [26] and major powers use trade networks for coercion [9]?
- **Open questions.** Do labour chapters reduce forced labour specifically, beyond improving general labour law [22]? How should adjustment funds be financed and governed across borders? Which institutions would workers and survivors themselves trust to monitor compliance?
