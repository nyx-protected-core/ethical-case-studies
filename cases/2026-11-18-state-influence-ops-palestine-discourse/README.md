# State influence operations and the pro-Palestinian movement: what Iran, Russia and China did, and what the record shows

Status date: 18 November 2026. Every factual claim points to a source in SOURCES.md that was opened for this study.

**How to read it**
- Numbers in square brackets point to the source list, for example [S18].
- **Documented** means a government, platform or company publicly attributed the activity, or the activity is on the public record.
- **Assessment** means a researcher's or analyst's judgement.
- **Inference** means a conclusion this study draws from the documented record. It is labelled every time.
- **Hypothesis** means a claim that is tested here but not established.

## 1. Summary

The pro-Palestinian, or "Free Palestine", movement in Western countries is decades old. Its best-known current campaign, the call for boycott, divestment and sanctions (BDS), was issued by Palestinian civil-society groups on 9 July 2005 [S1]. Hostile states did not create the movement.

What the record does show is that Iran, Russia and China each used the Gaza war and the protests around it for their own ends:

- **Iran:** the clearest case. In July 2024 the US Director of National Intelligence said that actors tied to Iran's government had posed as activists online, sought to encourage Gaza protests in the United States and "even provid[ed] financial support to protesters" [S18]. Microsoft, OpenAI and Meta each documented and removed Iranian influence operations aimed at Israel and its supporters after 7 October 2023 [S11, S13, S14, S16, S17].
- **Russia:** used the war to push its campaign against Western support for Ukraine. France attributed the online amplification of the Paris Star of David graffiti in October 2023 to a bot network tied to Russia's RRN/Doppelganger operation [S20]. Researchers documented further Doppelganger activity built around the Israel-Hamas war [S21, S22], and the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) assessed that the Kremlin was exploiting the attack to reduce Western attention to Ukraine [S40].
- **China:** the record is mostly overt. Chinese state media and diplomats amplified anti-US and anti-Israel narratives, and one state broadcaster account spread antisemitic tropes [S19, S23]. The allegation that a China-linked donor network funds US protest groups is serious but disputed, and it was not established in court in any source opened [S24].

The evidence supports a narrower claim than "the movement is a misinformation campaign". A real movement was exploited, amplified and in places steered by hostile states, mainly after 7 October 2023. Their documented aims included dividing Western publics and weakening support for Israel and Ukraine. Most participants are not state actors; the US intelligence community itself said it had no indication that American protesters were acting in bad faith [S18]. Evidence that these operations actually changed Western policy is weak, and several of the companies that removed them found little authentic engagement [S14, S15, S16, S17].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The movement before 2019

- The BDS call is dated 9 July 2005 and was signed by Palestinian civil-society organisations [S1].
- The organised Palestinian national movement is older still. The Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) was already active in Jordan, where it clashed with the Jordanian military in September 1970 [S48] (section 2.2).

The movement therefore cannot have begun in 2019. The 2019–2020 period matters for a different reason: it is when Western governments began documenting larger state information operations, discussed in section 3.4.

### 2.2 The PLO's record of terrorism

- **Munich, 5–6 September 1972.** Eight Black September gunmen attacked the Israeli Olympic team. Eleven Israelis and a West German police officer were killed [S2, S48]. Britannica describes Black September as a breakaway militant faction of Fatah, "the PLO group led by Yasir Arafat", that apparently received its orders from Fatah's security apparatus; some scholars contest how close that link was [S48]. The same source ascribes other attacks to the group, including the 1973 killing of the US ambassador to Sudan [S48].
- **Achille Lauro, 1985.** In the Anti-Terrorism Act of 1987, Congress found that the PLO "was directly responsible for the murder of an American citizen on the Achille Lauro cruise liner in 1985" [S3]. The Palestine Liberation Front, the group behind the hijacking, was among the first organisations designated as Foreign Terrorist Organizations on 8 October 1997 [S6].
- **US designation, 1987.** The same Act states that "the PLO and its affiliates are a terrorist organization and a threat to the interests of the United States" [S3].
- **Oslo, 1993.** In a letter dated 9 September 1993, Arafat wrote to Israeli Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin that "the PLO renounces the use of terrorism and other acts of violence" [S4]. Israel recognised the PLO, and the Oslo Declaration of Principles was signed at the White House on 13 September 1993 [S5].
- **Hamas** is a separate organisation from the PLO. It was designated a Foreign Terrorist Organization on 8 October 1997 [S6]. The US State Department reported in 2021 that Iran continued to provide "weapons systems and other support to Hamas" and other designated Palestinian groups [S7].

Note: the PLO's history is relevant context, but it does not establish who is behind present-day protests. That has to be shown separately, operation by operation, as the sections below try to do.

## 3. Operations by state

### 3.1 Iran (strongest evidence)

**Documented**
- **US intelligence, 9 July 2024.** DNI Avril Haines: "Iranian government actors have sought to opportunistically take advantage of ongoing protests regarding the war in Gaza... We have observed actors tied to Iran's government posing as activists online, seeking to encourage protests, and even providing financial support to protesters." She added that American protesters were acting "in good faith" and "may not be aware that they are interacting with or receiving support from a foreign government" [S18].
- **Microsoft Threat Analysis Center, February 2024.**
  - Iranian groups active against Israel rose from nine in the first week of the war to 14 by day 15.
  - Cyber-enabled influence operations rose from roughly one every other month in 2021 to 11 in October 2023.
  - Traffic to Iranian state-affiliated news sites rose 42% in the first week of the war.
  - Microsoft listed four Iranian objectives: destabilisation through polarisation, retaliation, intimidation, and "undermining international support for Israel".
  - From late November 2023, Iranian groups targeted countries that Iran saw as supporting Israel [S11, S12].
- **Microsoft, August 2024 (Storm-2035).** Microsoft found an Iranian network of covert "news" sites aimed at US voters on both left and right, including on the Israel-Hamas conflict. One site, Nio Thinker, began publishing in late October 2023 with an initial focus on the war. Microsoft said the wider campaign had operated since at least 2020 [S13].
- **OpenAI, August 2024.** OpenAI banned ChatGPT accounts used by Storm-2035, whose main topics included "the conflict in Gaza" and "Israel's presence at the Olympic Games". Its fake personas posed as both progressives and conservatives [S14].
- **OpenAI, May 2024.** OpenAI disrupted the Iranian International Union of Virtual Media (IUVM), which published "pro-Iran, anti-Israel and anti-US" web content [S15].
- **Meta, Q1 2024.** Meta removed an Iranian network that targeted Israel with fake Israeli personas in Hebrew, playing on divisions over the government's war response [S16].
- **Meta, Q3 2024.** Meta removed an Iranian network running fake "hacktivist" brands, one of which called for banning Israeli athletes from the Paris Olympics. Meta linked it to Cotton Sandstorm, which Microsoft had connected to the IRGC [S17].
- **Overt messaging.** In the first days of the war, ISD found that Iranian state-affiliated accounts "glorif[ied]" the 7 October attack and framed it within a "Resistance Axis" narrative [S19].

**Limits**
- OpenAI rated Storm-2035 at the low end of Category 2 on the Brookings Breakout Scale, with "no evidence that real people picked up or widely shared their content" [S14].
- Meta removed both 2024 networks before they built authentic audiences [S16, S17].
- The DNI statement does not name groups, amounts or the scale of the funding [S18].

### 3.2 Russia

**Documented**
- **Paris graffiti, 2023.** France's agency for countering foreign digital interference, VIGINUM, found 1,095 bots on X that published 2,589 posts about the Stars of David stencilled in Paris. It linked them "with a high degree of confidence" to Russia's RRN/Doppelganger network. The bots posted photos from 28 October 2023, about 48 hours before the first apparently authentic post. Who made the graffiti was left to a judicial investigation [S20].
- **Facebook ads, 2023–2024.** ISD found 192 pro-Kremlin Facebook ads, likely tied to Doppelganger, that used the Israel-Hamas war to criticise military support and humanitarian aid for Ukraine in France and Germany [S21].
- **Israel, 2024.** INSS documented Doppelganger content in Hebrew that imitated Israeli news sites. It argued that Israel should not help Ukraine and sought to weaken confidence in the IDF and in ties with the United States [S22].
- **State media.** In October 2023, Russian state media spread the unfounded claim that Ukraine had supplied weapons to Hamas [S19].

**Assessment.** ISW assessed on 7 October 2023 that the Kremlin "will likely continue to exploit the Hamas attacks in Israel to advance several information operations intended to reduce US and Western support and attention to Ukraine" [S40].

Note: Russia's main documented aim is Ukraine, not Palestinian advocacy as such. Gaza is used as a lever.

### 3.3 China

**Documented (overt)**
- **State media and diplomats.** In October 2023, ISD found that Chinese state media and diplomatic accounts on X blamed the United States for the escalation, accused Western media of bias, and attacked Western leaders. China Daily's EU bureau chief wrote that Western politicians had "blood of Palestinian children on their hands" [S19].
- **Antisemitic content.** Brookings reported that a CCTV social media account reposted a clip with antisemitic conspiracy theories, including the hashtag "Jews represent 3% of the U.S. population but control 70% of the U.S. wealth" [S23].
- **Limits on China's role.** Brookings also concluded that China "remains reluctant to assume a substantive role" in the conflict [S23].

**Alleged, not established**
- A July 2025 report from George Washington University's Program on Extremism argues that a network funded by Neville Roy Singham, a US businessman based in Shanghai, supports groups that organised "Shut It Down for Palestine" protests, and that its messaging aligns with Beijing [S24].
- The report relies partly on a 2023 New York Times investigation and on a letter in which two US senators asked the Justice Department whether these groups had breached the Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA) [S24].
- CodePink "firmly denies" receiving money from the CCP or any foreign government [S24].
- The sources opened for this study contain no charge or court finding on these allegations.
- No platform takedown attributing a covert Chinese network specifically to Gaza protest discourse was found in the sources opened. The OpenAI Spamouflage case concerned general pro-China content [S15].

### 3.4 The 2019–2020 period

- **COVID-era disinformation.** In 2020 the EU's diplomatic service documented Russian and Chinese state disinformation around COVID-19 [S8].
- **Iranian domains seized.** In October 2020 the US government seized 92 domains it said were used by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) for a global disinformation campaign [S9]. More domains were seized in June 2021 [S10].
- **Long-running infrastructure.** Microsoft dates the Storm-2035 network to at least 2020 [S13].

These sources show that state disinformation operations grew and drew enforcement action in 2019–2020. No source opened shows that these operations created or directed Palestine activism in 2019–2020. The documented surge in Gaza-related operations comes after 7 October 2023.

## 4. Aims, and the link to Iran's nuclear programme

**Stated or attributed aims**
- **Iran:** "undermining international support for Israel" [S11, S12]; stoking discord in the United States [S18].
- **Russia:** reducing Western support for Ukraine [S21, S40].
- **China:** presenting itself as a peacemaker while blaming the United States [S19, S23].

**Iran's nuclear progress over the same period**
| Date | Uranium enriched up to 60% | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 28 Oct 2023, three weeks after 7 October | 128.3 kg | [S25] |
| 13 Jun 2025, day the military attacks of 13–24 June began | 440.9 kg | [S26, S27] |

- 60% enrichment is a short technical step from the roughly 90% used in weapons, but it is not itself a weapon [S29].
- In September 2025 the IAEA said it could not verify the stockpile's current size or location after the June attacks [S26, S27].
- In 2026 it still could not verify the stockpile. On 18 November 2026, IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi told the Board of Governors that the agency could not confirm that declared material had not been diverted [S28, S29].

**Inference.** Iran had a clear interest in reducing Western political and military backing for Israel while its stockpile grew, and Microsoft documents that Iran pursued that aim through influence operations [S11]. No source opened shows that any influence operation was planned around the nuclear timeline. That link is inferred from motive and timing, not documented.

## 5. Two fronts: Ukraine, 7 October and divided Western resources

### 5.1 Timeline
- **24 February 2022:** Russia announced its "special military operation" against Ukraine [S30].
- **7 October 2023:** Hamas attacked Israel, about 20 months later.

The two wars did not start together. What overlapped is that, from October 2023, Western governments had to supply both at once from the same limited stocks.

### 5.2 Russia and Iran: a two-way military partnership
- **Weapons both ways.** In December 2022, senior US officials said Russia was giving Iran an "unprecedented level" of military and technical support in exchange for Iranian weapons for the war in Ukraine. They said weapons and expertise were "flowing in both directions" and that Iranian pilots had trained on Russia's Su-35 fighter [S31].
- **Sanctions and deliveries.** In December 2022 and October 2023, the US designated Iranian and Russian entities for transferring Iranian drones for use against Ukraine, and Russian weapons systems to Iran, "including the Yak-130 combat aircraft" [S32].
- **Drone factory.** The same US release describes efforts to build a factory in Russia to make Shahed-136 attack drones (the Russian version is called Geran-2) [S32].

### 5.3 Hamas in Moscow
- **26 October 2023:** a Hamas delegation led by Musa Abu Marzouk met Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Mikhail Bogdanov in Moscow [S33, S34].
- **Russia's account:** the foreign ministry spokeswoman, Maria Zakharova, confirmed the visit [S33].
- **Hamas's account:** the delegation "praised the position of Russian President Vladimir Putin". According to the same statement, Bogdanov reviewed Russia's efforts toward a ceasefire, opening the crossings and bringing in humanitarian aid [S34].
- **Iran:** Iran's deputy foreign minister, Ali Bagheri Kani, was in Moscow the same day [S33].

### 5.4 Competing for the same munitions
- **155mm shells to Ukraine, 2022–2023.** The Pentagon drew on a US stockpile kept in Israel to supply Ukraine with 155mm artillery shells, up to about 300,000 according to reports, with half already in Europe by January 2023. Israeli defence officials judged at the time that Israel had no immediate need for the shells [S35, S36].
- **Shells back to Israel, October 2023.** After 7 October, Israel asked for the shells back. A US defence official confirmed that some 155mm shells withdrawn from the Israel stockpile before the attack to replenish US stocks in Europe had been sent to Israel instead: "Much of that has been redirected and provided to the Israel Defence Forces for their use" [S38]. US officials suggested the diversion would have "no immediate impact" on Ukraine's ability to fight [S37].
- **Air defence.**
  - On 14 April 2024, Iran launched 170 drones, 30 cruise missiles and 120 ballistic missiles at Israel [S39]. The United States and its allies helped intercept the attack.
  - President Zelensky asked allies to defend Ukraine the same way [S39]. The White House answered that they were "two different conflicts" [S45].
  - In January 2025, about 90 Patriot interceptors that had been stored in Israel were sent to Ukraine [S41].

### 5.5 One bill for both wars, and the delay
- **Request.** On 20 October 2023, the White House asked Congress for emergency funding for Ukraine and Israel together: about $61.4 billion for Ukraine and $14.3 billion for Israel [S42, S43].
- **Delay.** The package was signed on 24 April 2024, about six months later, as a $95.3 billion bill [S44].
- **Effect.** Roll Call reported that the Pentagon's ability to support Ukraine had been "hampered since December" by the lack of funds [S44].

### 5.6 Analysis: did Russia gain?
- **ISW, 7 October 2023:** the Kremlin was presenting attention to the Middle East and to Ukraine as a zero-sum choice. Russian commentators claimed the West's attention had shifted away from Ukraine [S40].
- **ISD, 2023–2024:** Doppelganger ads used the Gaza war to argue against aid to Ukraine [S21].

### 5.7 Hypothesis: were the two conflicts deliberately coordinated as a misdirection?

**Hypothesis.** The 7 October attack was coordinated with Russia, or between Russia and Iran, to split Western attention and resources between Ukraine and Israel while Iran's nuclear programme advanced.

**Evidence consistent with it**
- Russia and Iran had a documented two-way military partnership before 7 October [S31, S32].
- Russia hosted Hamas 19 days after the attack, the same day an Iranian deputy foreign minister was in Moscow [S33, S34].
- Russia openly used the war to argue for less Western attention to and aid for Ukraine [S21, S40].
- Western munitions really were pulled between the two theatres [S35–S38, S41].
- Iran's 60% stockpile kept growing over the same period [S25–S27].

**What is missing or points the other way**
- On 12 October 2023, ISW said it had "not observed any evidence – and does not assess – that the Kremlin supported, directed, or is involved in the Hamas attacks" [S46].
- On 12 October 2023, Secretary of State Blinken said the United States had no "direct evidence that Iran was involved in the attack, either in planning it or carrying it out", while calling Iran complicit through its long support for Hamas [S47].
- Microsoft found that Iran's own early influence operations after 7 October were "hasty and chaotic", indicating "little or no coordination with Hamas" [S11].
- The Ukraine war began 20 months earlier, so it could not have been launched as cover for 7 October.

**Finding.** The record documents opportunism and partnership: hostile states exploited both wars once they were under way. It does not document a coordinated plan. The hypothesis remains unproven. New evidence, for example declassified intelligence, could change that.

## 6. What the evidence does and does not show

**Shows**
- Iran posed as activists, encouraged protests and funded some protesters in the United States (per US intelligence) [S18].
- Iran, Russia and China ran or amplified influence content about the Gaza war, and Iran and Russia did so through covert networks that have been publicly attributed [S11–S17, S19–S22].
- Their stated or attributed aims included weakening support for Israel (Iran) and for Ukraine (Russia).

**Does not show**
- That the movement was created by these states, or began in 2019.
- That most protesters are directed or paid by foreign states.
- That these operations measurably changed Western arms supplies or policy. Removed networks often had little reach [S14–S17], and no source opened measures their policy impact.
- That 7 October and the Ukraine war were coordinated.

## 7. Ethical questions

1. **Real people and fake amplification.** How can societies expose foreign manipulation without treating genuine protesters as agents? The DNI statement tried to do both [S18].
2. **Disclosure.** Should groups that receive foreign-linked funding have to disclose it, as FARA requires for agents of foreign principals [S24]? Where is the line between disclosure and suppressing protest?
3. **Platforms.** Companies removed these networks, often before they grew [S14–S17]. How much public reporting should platforms owe?
4. **Moral consistency.** Influence operations were not limited to hostile states. OpenAI and Meta also removed an Israel-based commercial operation (STOIC) that targeted audiences in the United States and Canada [S15, S16]. A consistent standard applies to every state and firm that does this.
5. **Scarce resources.** When two democracies at war need the same shells and interceptors, how should allies decide who gets them, and how much should adversaries' information campaigns shape that debate?
