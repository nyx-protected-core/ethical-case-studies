# What a more cooperative world without war could look like

*A research case. Every figure and quotation below was checked against the source's own text, and every link and DOI was resolved live on 18 November 2026. The Sources section records how each item was checked. Anything not confirmed at the source is marked **UNVERIFIED** or has been left out.*

---

## Summary

War is costly for everyone who fights it. Bargaining theory therefore asks why states fail to find a deal they would both prefer to fighting. Its answer points to three problems: missing or hidden information, promises that cannot be trusted, and stakes that look impossible to divide. That answer is useful because each problem can, in principle, be designed against.

The world is currently moving the wrong way. In 2025 the Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded **65 state-based conflicts, the most since its records begin in 1946**, and the number of interstate conflicts doubled for the second year running. Even so, there is evidence that some institutions reduce war or the risks of war: European integration, ASEAN's norms, the African Union's peace and security architecture, Latin America's nuclear-weapon-free zone, well-resourced UN peacekeeping, and verified arms control. This case sets out what those institutions show, including their limits, a staged pathway built on them, and the strongest realist objections with a response.

---

## 1. Why wars happen, and why they end

**The bargaining puzzle.** Fearon (1995) showed that "under very broad conditions there will exist negotiated settlements that genuinely rational states would mutually prefer to a risky and costly fight". If fighting destroys value, some division of the stakes leaves both sides better off than war. So the real question is what stops leaders from finding that division. Fearon identifies:

1. **Information problems.** "States have both private information about capabilities and resolve and the incentive to misrepresent it." Each side may bluff, so each may misjudge what the other will accept, and war becomes the way the truth comes out.
2. **Commitment problems.** "In specific strategic contexts states may be unable credibly to commit to uphold a mutually preferable bargain." The clearest case is a shifting balance of power. A declining state may fight now rather than trust a rising state's promises later. Fearon treats preventive war as a commitment problem.
3. **Issue indivisibility.** If an issue "allows only a finite number of resolutions, it might be that none falls within the range that both prefer to fighting." Fearon is sceptical that this is common. Real disputes are usually multidimensional, and "side-payments or linkages with other issues typically are possible". He argues that where issues *are* effectively indivisible, "the cause of this indivisibility lies in domestic political and other mechanisms rather than in the nature of the issues themselves."

**How wars end.** The commitment problem also explains why civil wars are hard to settle. Walter (1997) reports that between 1940 and 1990, "55 percent of interstate wars were resolved at the bargaining table, whereas only 20 percent of civil wars" were. She found that if "a third party agreed to enforce the terms of a peace treaty, negotiations always succeeded". If a third party did not intervene, "these talks usually failed".

**What this means for design.** Anything that makes information credible (verification, transparency, inspection), makes commitments enforceable (third-party guarantees, binding institutions, costly-to-reverse integration) or makes stakes divisible (linkage, side payments, shared sovereignty) widens the range of deals both sides prefer to war.

## 2. The democratic peace and the commercial peace

### 2.1 Democratic peace

**Supporting evidence.**
- Maoz and Russett (1993) find that democratic states are "in general about as conflict- and war-prone as nondemocracies, but democracies have rarely clashed with one another". They report that "democracy, in and of itself, has a consistent and robust negative effect on the likelihood of conflict or escalation in a dyad". (A dyad is a pair of states.) Both normative and structural explanations are supported, and the normative one more robustly.
- Oneal and Russett (1997), studying the Cold War era, report "clear evidence that democracies are relatively unlikely to become involved in militarized disputes with other democracies".

**Critiques.**
- Rosato (2003) accepts that "there is certainly peace among democracies" but argues that the proposed causal logics do not hold. Democracies "do not reliably externalize their domestic norms" and "do not trust or respect one another when their interests clash". The peace "may not be caused by the democratic nature of those states."
- Gartzke (2007) argues for a "capitalist peace": economic development, free markets and similar interstate interests account for "the effect commonly attributed to regime type".
- Waltz (2000) calls it the democratic peace "thesis", not "theory", and argues that "inside of, as well as outside of, the circle of democratic states, peace depends on a precarious balance of forces."

### 2.2 Commercial (trade) peace

**Supporting evidence.** Oneal and Russett (1997) find that "the pacific benefits of trade, both total and dyadic, have not been sufficiently appreciated."

**Critiques.**
- Barbieri (1996) studied 1870–1938 (14,341 dyad-years, 270 militarized disputes, 14 wars). She found that "extensive economic interdependence increases the likelihood that dyads will engage in militarized interstate disputes", with "no influence on the occurrence of wars". Peace through trade is most likely "among dyads composed of mutually dependent trading partners".
- Waltz (2000) includes a section titled "The Weak Effects of Interdependence".

**Where this leaves us.** The finding that pairs of democracies rarely fight is robust. Why they don't, and whether development or markets do the real work, is still contested. Trade helps most when dependence is mutual and balanced; lopsided dependence can become a source of coercion. A cooperative world therefore cannot rest on spreading democracy or trade alone. It also needs institutions that tackle the information and commitment problems directly.

## 3. Trend evidence: where the world is now

**UCDP (Uppsala), data for 2025, published June 2026** (Davies, Pettersson and Öberg 2026, and the Uppsala University press release of 9 June 2026):

| Indicator (2025) | Value |
|---|---|
| State-based conflicts | **65**, "a record high", the most since 1946 |
| Interstate conflicts | **8**, "doubled for the second year in a row, from two in 2023 to eight in 2025", the highest since 1946 |
| Conflicts at war level (≥1,000 battle deaths in the year) | **13**, "the highest number since 1992" |
| Total fatalities in organized violence | **244,600**, up from about 187,000 in 2024 |
| State-based fatalities | **153,600**, "slightly lower than in previous years" but still very high |
| One-sided violence (against civilians) | rose fivefold, largely driven by Sudan |
| Non-state violence | fell, with fatalities at their lowest since 2013 |

**PRIO, *Conflict Trends: A Global Overview, 1946–2025* (2026):** the 65 conflicts were spread across **35 countries**, and "only 16 out of the 35 conflict countries have only one conflict." PRIO notes that the past five years account for more battle-related deaths than the entire twenty-year period before 2021, and that the rise in interstate conflict "has continued in 2026".

*Note on sources.* The two sources rank 2025's death toll differently. The UCDP article says 2025 was second only to 2022 since 1994. The PRIO paper calls 2025 "the third most violent year since 1989", behind 2021 and 1994. Both are quoted as published, and the difference is not resolved here.

**Spending and weapons:**
- World military expenditure rose by 2.9% in real terms to **US$2,887 billion in 2025**, "the 11th consecutive year of growth", and is up 41% over 2016–25 (SIPRI, April 2026).
- The global nuclear inventory is about **12,187 warheads (January 2026)**, of which about **9,745** are in military stockpiles (SIPRI Yearbook 2026 and FAS). The total is down from a peak of approximately **70,300 in 1986** (FAS). The SIPRI Yearbook notes that the total is falling only because Russia and the USA are dismantling retired warheads, and that military stockpiles are growing again. *(The FAS page gives both 12,187 and 12,321 for early 2026; this case uses the 12,187 figure that both FAS and SIPRI report.)*
- **New START expired in February 2026 without a replacement**, which "has eliminated" treaty-based official data on US and Russian strategic deployments (SIPRI Yearbook 2026).

**What the trends show.** Armed conflict is rising, and the rise is concentrated in state-based and especially interstate conflict. That is exactly where bargaining failures between governments matter most. The arms-control framework that once supplied verified information between the two largest nuclear powers has lapsed.

## 4. Institutions that have reduced war, or the risks of war

**European integration.** The 1950 Schuman Declaration, according to the EU's official history, proposed pooling coal and steel production to make war between France and Germany "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible". In bargaining terms, this is commitment through interdependence that is costly to reverse, combined with shared institutions that make information visible. The fact that France and Germany have not fought since is consistent with the mechanism. It does not by itself prove the mechanism caused the peace, because shared alliances, democracy and wealth all coincided.

**ASEAN.** The 1976 Treaty of Amity and Cooperation in Southeast Asia, signed at Denpasar, Bali, commits its parties to the "settlement of differences or disputes by peaceful means" and the "renunciation of the threat or use of force". Kivimäki (2001) argues that the "long peace" among ASEAN members (1968–94) arose from an "intersubjective consensus about the common interests, norms, and identity" even among "illiberal non-democracies". That matters because it suggests peace does not require democracy first. The record since then is not only peaceful. Armed clashes between two ASEAN members, Cambodia and Thailand, near the Temple of Preah Vihear led the International Court of Justice, in an order of 18 July 2011, to require both parties to withdraw their military personnel from a "provisional demilitarized zone" surrounding the temple (source 25). The episode shows that the non-use-of-force norm did not always prevent fighting between members.

**UN peacekeeping.** The quantitative evidence is consistent across several studies, with important limits:
- Fortna (2004) finds "that peacekeeping after civil wars does indeed make an important contribution to the stability of peace", after controlling for where peacekeepers tend to be sent.
- Hultman, Kathman and Shannon (2013) studied monthly data for conflicts in Africa from 1991 to 2008. They found that "as the UN commits more military and police forces to a peacekeeping mission, fewer civilians are targeted with violence", and that "deploying several thousand troops and several hundred police dramatically reduces civilian killings."
- Hultman, Kathman and Shannon (2014) studied civil wars in Africa from 1992 to 2011. They found that "increasing numbers of armed military troops are associated with reduced battlefield deaths, while police and observers are not". Their conclusion: "if appropriately composed, UN peacekeeping missions reduce violent conflict."
- Walter, Howard and Fortna (2021) review the field and describe "an extraordinary relationship between peacekeepers and peace, notwithstanding a number of well-known problems."

These studies are observational. The two Hultman et al. papers cover only Africa. They measure effects in civil wars, mostly in weaker states, not bargaining among great powers. The "well-known problems" include missions that failed to protect civilians. In *The fall of Srebrenica* (A/54/549, 15 November 1999), the UN Secretary-General's report on the 1995 massacre in a UN-declared safe area concluded that "the tragedy of Srebrenica will haunt our history forever," and that when the international community "makes a solemn promise to safeguard and protect innocent civilians from massacre, then it must be willing to back its promise with the necessary means" (source 26).

**Arms control.** Verified limits supply the kind of credible information that bargaining theory says is otherwise missing, and they have been accompanied by a large fall in declared stockpiles: from about 70,300 warheads in 1986 to about 12,187 in 2026 (FAS and SIPRI). That is a reduction in arsenals, not by itself evidence of less war. FAS adds a caution: "the overwhelming portion of the reduction happened in the 1990s", and official claims that credit current agreements overstate their role.

**The African Union.** The Protocol Relating to the Establishment of the Peace and Security Council of the African Union creates a "standing decision-making organ for the prevention, management and resolution of conflicts," described as "a collective security and early-warning arrangement to facilitate timely and efficient response to conflict and crisis situations in Africa." It is to be supported by the AU Commission, a Panel of the Wise, a Continental Early Warning System, an African Standby Force and a Peace Fund, with objectives that include anticipating and preventing conflicts and undertaking peace-making and peace-building where conflicts have occurred (source 27). This is Africa's own continental architecture, not an extension of European or UN models, though it is designed to work with the UN.

**Latin America's nuclear-weapon-free zone.** The Treaty for the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean (Treaty of Tlatelolco, opened for signature in 1967) requires its parties "to use exclusively for peaceful purposes the nuclear material and facilities which are under their jurisdiction" and to prohibit, in their territories, the testing, use, manufacture, production, acquisition, receipt, storage, installation, deployment or possession of nuclear weapons (source 28). It is a regional arms-control institution created by Latin American states, and a model later followed in other regions.

**Mediation by states outside the great-power circle.** The pathway in section 5 is not only for the permanent members of the Security Council. Official texts already in this case show regional bodies taking on peace-making (the AU Peace and Security Council; ASEAN's Treaty of Amity and Cooperation). In the Israel–Hamas war, the UAE has officially commended mediation by Qatar and Egypt (source 29). Those examples are cited as stated, official roles, not as a measured record of success.

**A common thread.** Each of these institutions either makes information credible, makes commitments enforceable, or ties interests together. None of them has ended war, and several have failed in specific cases.

## 5. A realistic step-by-step pathway

This sequence is designed to be within reach of current governments, ordered from least to most demanding. It is a proposal, not a forecast, and it has to take major states' stated objections into account rather than assume they will join.

**Stage 1 (0–3 years): stop the slide in information and trust.**
1. Restore US–Russia strategic transparency even without a new treaty. That could mean reciprocal data exchanges and notifications that replace what lapsed with New START.
2. Seek multilateral risk-reduction talks that include China — crisis hotlines, missile-launch notifications, and agreements on incidents at sea and in the air — while recognising China's stated position. On 6 February 2026, Shen Jian, China's ambassador for disarmament affairs, said: "China will not take part in nuclear disarmament negotiations at this stage," repeating the long-standing position that Russia and the United States have "special and primary responsibilities" to make cuts to their larger arsenals first (source 30). A pathway that simply assumes China will join talks on the same terms as the two largest arsenals is not realistic until that position changes.
3. Fund UN and regional peacekeeping so missions have the large, properly composed military and police contingents the evidence supports, rather than observers alone.

**Stage 2 (3–10 years): make commitments enforceable.**
4. Make third-party guarantees standard in peace agreements, including monitoring, phased implementation and guarantors with real capacity, as Walter's findings suggest.
5. Spread ASEAN- and Helsinki-style regional codes against the threat or use of force, each with a standing mediation body.
6. Grow *mutual*, balanced economic interdependence, such as joint energy or water infrastructure between rivals, and avoid lopsided dependence that can be used for coercion.

**Stage 3 (10–25 years): make cooperation the default.**
7. Pursue a new generation of verified arms reductions that include all nuclear-armed states, with confidence-building limits on new domains such as space, cyber and autonomous weapons.
8. Pool sovereignty step by step in specific sectors, as the coal-and-steel model did, among former rivals in regions currently at risk.
9. Shift military spending toward the institutions above. The approved UN peacekeeping budget for the fiscal year 1 July 2024 to 30 June 2025 was US$5.6 billion (source 31). World military expenditure in 2025 was US$2,887 billion (SIPRI, source 11). The peacekeeping figure is for 2024–25, not a verified 2025–26 budget, but the orders of magnitude are clear: the official peacekeeping budget was a small fraction of one year's military spending.

**How to tell whether it's working:** fewer state-based and interstate conflicts in the UCDP series; more conflicts ending in negotiated settlements that last; lower civilian deaths where peacekeepers are deployed; and restored, verified data on warhead deployments.

## 6. The realist objection, and a response

**The objection, stated fairly.**
- Waltz (2000) argues that international institutions "are created and maintained by stronger states to serve their perceived or misperceived interests", and gives a section to "The Limited Role of International Institutions". He holds that "unipolarity appears as the least durable of international configurations": concentrated power provokes balancing. On this view the post-Cold War calm was a phase of the distribution of power, not a permanent change.
- Mearsheimer (1994/95), as quoted in Keohane and Martin (1995), argues that "institutions have minimal influence on state behavior and thus hold little prospect for promoting stability in a post-Cold War world." The same author developed this "offensive realist" view at book length in *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (2001). The book is cited by title only here; its contents were not checked for this case.
- **The current evidence partly supports the realists.** Interstate conflicts are at their highest since 1946, military spending has risen for 11 straight years, and New START has lapsed. Institutions did not prevent these developments.

**A response.**
1. **The realist predictions were sometimes wrong about institutions.** Keohane and Martin (1995) point out that Mearsheimer had predicted NATO would weaken and the European Community would "grow weaker, not stronger", but both were expanding. They argue that "institutions can provide information, reduce transaction costs, make commitments more credible, establish focal points for coordination". Those are the same failures Fearon identifies as causes of war.
2. **Realism and bargaining theory are compatible.** Shifts in power do create commitment problems, as realists say. That is an argument for institutions designed to manage power transitions, such as verification and guarantees, not against them.
3. **Some effects are measured, not just argued — but in a different setting from the one realists emphasise.** The peacekeeping studies show measurable falls in civilian and battlefield deaths when missions are properly resourced. Those results are about civil wars, mostly in weaker states, and mainly in Africa in the Hultman et al. papers. Mearsheimer's claim is about the behaviour of great powers. A realist can accept the peacekeeping evidence without giving up that core claim. The fair conclusion is narrower: institutions have had a measurable influence on civil-war violence when they are properly composed and resourced; that finding does not, by itself, refute the argument that institutions have only a limited effect on great-power war.
4. **What realism gets right should be built in.** The pathway above starts with information and verification between rivals, which realists themselves endorse as prudent. It does not depend on trust, shared values or the end of competition. A cooperative world in this sense is not one without rivalry. It is one where rivalry is channelled away from war.
