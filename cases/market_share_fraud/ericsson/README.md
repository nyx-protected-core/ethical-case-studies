# Ericsson (Telefonaktiebolaget LM Ericsson): a 2019 DPA, then a 2023 guilty plea after breach

Date: 2026-11-19

**How to read the figures.** Every money amount or count below that comes from a government agency or court is marked **[⚑ agency figure]**. That mark means: this is an agency-reported figure; agencies have an incentive to report headline totals; credits and offsets between jurisdictions can mean double-counting; and what was found depends on what investigators looked for (detection bias), so it is not a measure of all the bribery that happened.

## 1. Summary

- **What was found.** On 6 December 2019, Ericsson entered a deferred prosecution agreement (DPA) with the US Department of Justice (DOJ) in the Southern District of New York. The DPA covered conspiracies to violate the FCPA's anti-bribery, books-and-records and internal-controls provisions. Its subsidiary Ericsson Egypt Ltd pleaded guilty to an anti-bribery conspiracy. Ericsson also settled SEC charges. Combined payments were more than $1 billion **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **The breach.** The DOJ determined that Ericsson breached the DPA's cooperation and disclosure obligations. On 21 March 2023, Ericsson pleaded guilty to the charges that had been deferred. It paid a further $206,728,848 **[⚑ agency figure]**, which removed the cooperation credit it had originally received, and accepted organisational probation and a one-year extension of the monitor.
- **Status.** The plea is an **adjudicated** finding (a guilty plea entered in federal court). The 2019 facts are **admitted** in a stipulated statement of facts. According to the company, the monitorship and the plea term ended on 2 June 2024 after the monitor certified its compliance programme.

**Countries and contracts named in the record:**
- **Djibouti** (DOJ): about $2.1m in bribes (2010–2014) to high-ranking officials to win a contract worth about €20.3m to modernise the state-owned operator's mobile network **[⚑ agency figure]**. A sham consulting contract and fake invoices were used. A draft due-diligence report left out the spousal relationship between the consultant's owner and an official.
- **China** (DOJ): tens of millions of dollars paid (2000–2016) to agents, consultants and service providers. Part of this funded a travel expense account used for gifts, travel and entertainment for officials, including customers at state-owned operators. Separately, about $31.5m was paid (2013–2016) under sham contracts for services never performed **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Vietnam** (DOJ): about $4.8m (2012–2015) paid to a consultant to create off-book slush funds **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Indonesia** (DOJ): about $45m (2012–2015) paid to a consultant to create off-book slush funds **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Kuwait** (DOJ): about $450,000 promised to a consultant at a sales agent's request. The agent supplied inside tender information, and Ericsson won a contract worth about $182m **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Saudi Arabia** (SEC): the SEC complaint covers bribery through third parties in Saudi Arabia, China and Djibouti (2011–2017), winning business worth about $427m **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Iraq:** the DOJ's 2 March 2023 release says Ericsson failed to promptly report evidence and allegations about its business in Iraq that may constitute an FCPA violation. That is part of the breach finding (a failure to disclose), not a finding of bribery in Iraq. The 2023 plea agreement also keeps the DOJ free to act on "certain historical conduct in Iraq described in a 2019 internal investigation report". No finding about Iraq appears in the records opened. This is **not an adjudicated finding** and is mentioned only because the court filing refers to it.

No individual is named in this case. The records anonymise employees, and individual results were not checked (see *Unverified or uncertain items*).

## 4. How the scheme worked (as found in the record)

- **Third-party agents and consultants** engaged through sham contracts and paid on false invoices, with the payments mis-recorded (DOJ).
- **Off-book slush funds** held at consultants (Vietnam, Indonesia), used to pay third parties "who would not be able to pass Ericsson's due diligence processes" (DOJ).
- **Gifts, travel and entertainment** for officials, funded through agent payments (China) (DOJ; SEC).
- **Concealed conflicts.** Due diligence that hid a family relationship between a consultant and an official (Djibouti) (DOJ).
- **Inside tender information** bought through an agent (Kuwait) (DOJ).
- **Weak controls.** The DPA records that Ericsson had "inadequate anti-corruption controls and an inadequate anti-corruption compliance program" during the period.

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath, including the breach

- **Original credit.** The DPA gave no voluntary-disclosure credit, because Ericsson did not self-report. It gave only partial cooperation credit, because Ericsson "did not disclose allegations of corruption with respect to two relevant matters, produced certain relevant materials in an untimely manner, and did not timely and fully remediate", including inadequate discipline of certain executives (DPA para 4).
- **Breach.** The DOJ determined that Ericsson failed to meet DPA paragraphs 5 and 6 (cooperation and disclosure). The plea agreement says Ericsson failed "to disclose key factual evidence and information". As a result, the cooperation credit was eliminated, giving the additional $206,728,848 **[⚑ agency figure]**. The company describes the breaches as non-criminal (company statement). That is the company's own characterisation; the DOJ record calls them a breach of the agreement.
- **Monitor.** Appointed in June 2020 and extended by one year. The company says the monitor certified the programme on 28 March 2024 and the term ended on 2 June 2024. The monitor's reports are not public. The plea agreement, a court record, sets the end of the term at 2 June 2024.
- **Home-country enforcement.** The OECD Working Group on Bribery adopted a Phase 4 evaluation of Sweden in December 2024 (an intergovernmental report **[⚑ agency figure where cited]**). Its findings on this case were not read (not verified; see *Unverified or uncertain items*).

## 7. Fair counterarguments

- **Do DPAs work if a firm can breach one and still settle?** Ericsson is a rare case where a DPA breach led to a guilty plea. One view is that this shows DPAs have teeth. Søreide and Vagle (2022) and De Vita and Vozza (2024) argue that negotiated resolutions generally under-deter and give "minimal retribution". Parker and Dodge (2022), drawing on interviews with 24 practitioners and scholars, set out both the advantages and shortcomings of DPAs.
- **Cooperation credit as a lever.** The breach penalty consisted of removing the cooperation credit. Hock and Dávid-Barrett (2022) find that compliance and cooperation signals strongly shape settlement results. This raises the question of whether credit given at the start of a case should be conditional and claw-back-able by default.
- **Monitors and transparency.** The monitor's certification is known only from the company's statement. Whether monitorships achieve lasting change is contested, and monitor reports are generally not published.
- **Extraterritorial reach.** A Swedish company with subsidiaries worldwide, whose payments ran through Africa, Asia and the Middle East, had its case resolved in a US court. Bu (2022) discusses the sovereignty and double-jeopardy tensions of multijurisdictional enforcement.
- **No Ericsson-specific peer-reviewed study** of adequate quality was found. The analysis here draws on the general DPA and settlement literature.

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that Ericsson admitted bribery in several countries and later breached its DPA, because both rest on court filings: the 2019 statement of facts and the 2023 guilty plea. It accepts that the breach shows both sides of the DPA debate, since an agreement was enforced but the firm still settled. Iraq is kept out of the findings because no record opened contains a finding of bribery there.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Make cooperation credit conditional by default.** The Ericsson plea shows that credit can be clawed back after a breach. Agreements should say from the start that credit is provisional until the term ends (Hock and Dávid-Barrett 2022 on over-crediting). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative, derived from the record; related qualitative evidence in Hock and Dávid-Barrett (2022).*
2. **Require disclosure of all known allegations at signing.** The DPA notes two undisclosed corruption matters. Agreements should require a certified list of all known allegations, with automatic penalties for omissions. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative, derived from the record.*
3. **Publish monitor certifications and summary findings.** The public currently relies on the company's own account of the monitor's conclusion. A redacted public summary would allow independent assessment. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative, derived from the record.*
4. **Tie executive accountability to remediation credit.** The DPA criticised inadequate discipline of executives. Credit for remediation should depend on documented consequences for responsible managers (De Vita and Vozza 2024). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative, derived from the record.*
5. **Strengthen home-country enforcement.** Resolutions led by a foreign enforcer should be matched by home-country review. The OECD Phase 4 process is one channel for this, and its findings should be read alongside the US record. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative, derived from the record.*
