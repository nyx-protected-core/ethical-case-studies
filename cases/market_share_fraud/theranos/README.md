# Theranos: Investor Fraud Convictions, Laboratory Sanctions, and the Regulation of Unproven Diagnostic Tests

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Theranos is included because findings were made against the company itself: a settled SEC fraud action (no admission), federal laboratory-regulator sanctions, and a state consumer consent judgment. The criminal convictions were of two former executives, not of the company, and are reported by role. **Found** means decided by a court or regulator or agreed in a settlement (labelled as such); **Alleged** means charged or claimed. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies have reasons to present large numbers.*

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers Theranos, Inc., a privately held U.S. blood-testing company that raised more than $700 million from investors on claims about a finger-stick analyser, and the criminal, securities, laboratory and consumer outcomes. Theranos was never publicly listed; the case is included because it is adjudicated securities and wire fraud against investors in a private market, with patient-safety findings by a health regulator.

- **Findings versus claims.** The Findings section lists only verdicts, sentences, appellate rulings, settled regulator actions (with "neither admitted nor denied" stated where it applies) and regulator sanctions. Counts on which a defendant was acquitted, or on which the jury could not agree, are **not** findings and are labelled.
- **Source tags.** **[CT]** court or prosecutor record; **[REG]** regulator; **[LAW]** statute; **[PR]** peer-reviewed research article; **[PR-comm]** peer-reviewed journal commentary or opinion piece.
- **Basis for inclusion.** The company-level findings are the SEC's settled action (1.1, no admission), the laboratory regulator's sanctions (1.5) and the Arizona consent judgment (1.6, a settlement). No criminal charge was brought against the company; 1.2 to 1.4 concern individuals.
- **Government figures are marked ⚑** with notes on what they include.
- **People.** No individual is named; roles only ("the founder and chief executive", "the former president and chief operating officer"). Court records are cited by court and docket number, not by the names in their captions.
- Peer-reviewed sources were checked against Crossref for title, authors and year.

## Findings (adjudicated facts first)

**1.1 SEC settlements (March 2018; no admission).** The SEC charged the company, its founder and chief executive, and its former president with raising more than $700 million ⚑ through a years-long fraud that exaggerated or misstated the technology, business and financial performance [1]. The company and founder settled, *neither admitting nor denying* the allegations. The founder agreed to a $500,000 ⚑ penalty, a ten-year bar from serving as an officer or director of a public company, return of 18.9 million shares and loss of voting control [1]. The SEC's release states that the settlements with the company and the founder were subject to court approval [1]. *Status: settled civil action against the company, without admission; the SEC's description of the conduct is an allegation, later largely matched by criminal findings against the two executives (1.2–1.4). The final judgment entered against the company in N.D. Cal. No. 5:18-cv-01602 could not be opened (UNVERIFIED).*

**1.2 Criminal verdict against the founder (January 2022).** A jury convicted the founder of one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud against investors and three counts of wire fraud against individual investors, involving wire transfers totalling more than $140 million ⚑ [2]. *Not findings:* the jury **acquitted** her of the patient-fraud conspiracy count and three counts of fraud against individual patients, and could not agree on three further investor counts; one patient count was dismissed during trial [2]. She was sentenced to 135 months [2][3].

**1.3 Criminal verdict against the former president and chief operating officer.** Tried separately, he was convicted on all twelve counts, including conspiracy and wire fraud against **patients** as well as investors, and sentenced to 155 months [2][4][5].

**1.4 Appeal (final at the circuit level).** The Ninth Circuit affirmed both defendants' convictions and sentences and the $452 million ⚑ restitution order, holding them jointly and severally liable; the order comprised $397 million for twelve investor victims identified at sentencing and $54.5 million for two corporate partners [4]. The court found that some former employees' testimony strayed into expert opinion but that any error was harmless [4]. The opinion was amended in December 2025 [4].

**1.5 Laboratory regulator sanctions (July 2016).** After a 2015 inspection, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services found the company's Newark laboratory out of compliance with five condition-level requirements of the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA), with a finding of **immediate jeopardy to patient health and safety**, and imposed sanctions including revocation of the laboratory's CLIA certificate (effective 5 September 2016 unless a timely appeal was filed), a limitation of the certificate for haematology, a civil money penalty of $10,000 ⚑ per day of non-compliance from 12 July 2016, and suspension and cancellation of the laboratory's approval to receive Medicare and Medicaid payments [6][7]. *Status: sanctions imposed by the regulator's letter; the outcome of any appeal is not confirmed here (Unverified or uncertain items).*

**1.6 State consumer settlement (April 2017).** Under a consent judgment with the Arizona Attorney General, the company paid $4.65 million ⚑ in consumer restitution, a full refund to every Arizonan who bought a Theranos blood test; payments were distributed in December 2017 [8].

## How the fraud worked

- **Misstating what the device could do.** The SEC alleged, and the criminal juries found as to investors, that the company told investors its proprietary analyser could run comprehensive tests from a finger-stick sample, when it ran only a small number of tests and most patient tests were run on modified commercial analysers made by others [1][2].
- **Inflated revenue and deployment claims.** The SEC stated that the company claimed more than $100 million in 2014 revenue and military battlefield deployment; in fact it earned a little over $100,000 from operations in 2014 and was never deployed by the Department of Defense [1].
- **Secrecy in place of evidence.** Peer-reviewed commentary described the company's claims as "stealth research", made without peer-reviewed validation [9][10]. A later study, titled for its subject, examines the lack of peer-reviewed evidence from high-valuation healthcare start-ups [11].
- **Regulatory gaps.** Commentary argues that the company reached patients without pre-market review or peer-reviewed validation by exploiting regulatory gaps [12]; a 2015 commentary addresses FDA regulation of laboratory-developed tests, the category through which in-house tests could reach patients without FDA review [13].
- **Organisational design.** A design-management study treats the company as a case in "designing a dangerous unicorn" [14].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

- **Investors.** $452 million ⚑ in restitution affirmed on appeal [4]; the SEC's figure for funds raised is more than $700 million ⚑ [1]. *Reporting note:* restitution is limited to identified victims and losses proven at sentencing; funds raised is a gross figure, not a loss figure.
- **Patients.** The health regulator's finding of immediate jeopardy to patient health and safety [6] and the former president's convictions on patient-fraud counts [4] establish that patients were exposed to unreliable testing. The founder was **acquitted** of the patient counts [2]; the two verdicts are not inconsistent, because different juries heard different evidence about different defendants. No count of individuals harmed by wrong results was located in a primary record.
- **Consumers.** About $4.65 million ⚑ refunded to Arizona buyers [8].
- **Trust in diagnostics.** Laboratory-medicine commentators argue the case damaged public trust in diagnostic innovation [12].

## Fair counterarguments

**4.1 Patients versus investors.** The founder's jury convicted only on investor counts [2]. Some read this as the law protecting money better than health; others note that the separate jury convicted the former president on patient counts [4], and that the regulator's own sanctions addressed patient safety [6].

**4.2 Private companies and securities fraud.** The SEC stressed that there is no exemption from anti-fraud rules for private, development-stage companies [1]. Critics argue private markets still lack disclosure duties that would have exposed the problems earlier.

**4.3 Was this fraud or failed innovation?** A common public framing is a start-up that over-promised rather than lied (this framing is not drawn from a cited record). The appellate court upheld convictions for intentional fraud on investors [4]. Peer-reviewed case work draws lessons for innovation culture and the structure of biomedical innovation [12][14]. The adjudicated record answers the legal question; the cultural question remains debated.

**4.4 Should the FDA regulate laboratory-developed tests?** FDA oversight of laboratory-developed tests was debated in peer-reviewed commentary by 2015 [13]. The FDA adopted a rule in 2024, but in March 2025 a federal court entered final judgment for the laboratory plaintiffs and against the FDA [15]. CLIA certification governs laboratory quality and testing standards [7]; whether that is enough without review of a test's clinical validity is the point in dispute (characterisation, not a finding).

**4.5 Detection.** Here, the regulator's inspection and published peer-reviewed commentary questioning the company's undisclosed evidence [9][10] preceded the legal actions.

**How the conclusion handles these points.** The case keeps the company-level record (settled SEC action, laboratory sanctions, consumer consent judgment) separate from the criminal verdicts against individuals, reports the founder's acquittals on patient counts as faithfully as the convictions, and treats the laboratory-test regulation question as open.

## Values (labelled section: these are judgements, not findings)

- **Health claims need evidence before use, not after.** Patients cannot audit a black box. Secrecy that might be acceptable for a consumer gadget is not acceptable for a medical test.
- **Boards and investors are gatekeepers too.** Prestige and valuation are not evidence.
- **Equal weight for patient harm.** A legal system that convicts readily for investor losses should take equal care with harms to patients.
- **Fair process.** Acquittals are part of the record and must be reported as faithfully as convictions.

## Proposed solutions (each sourced and rated for evidence)

Ratings: **strong** means consistent causal evidence from more than one careful study; **moderate** means one careful study or consistent observational evidence; **weak** means indirect or single-case support; **UNTESTED** means no evaluation of effect was located.

**6.1 Evidence and validation before clinical use.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED.** Commentators call for peer-reviewed validation of new diagnostic platforms before patient use [9]. *Evidence of effect:* none located; the 2019 study addresses the evidence gap in healthcare start-ups, not the effect of an intervention [11].

**6.2 Close the laboratory-developed-test gap.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED (the 2024 rule was set aside before it could be evaluated).** FDA review of laboratory-developed tests is the reform discussed in the 2015 commentary [13]. *Status and evidence:* the FDA's 2024 rule was set aside by the March 2025 judgment [15], so any reform now needs legislation; no outcome evidence exists.

**6.3 Stronger CLIA inspection and enforcement.** **Evidence rating: weak (one case shows the mechanism works once inspection occurs).** CLIA certification and inspection produced the 2016 sanctions [6][7]. *Evidence:* this case shows the mechanism worked once inspection occurred; it does not show how quickly problems are detected in general.

**6.4 Investor due diligence and disclosure in private markets.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED.** The SEC's settlement terms (penalty, officer-and-director bar, loss of control) are its tools after the fact [1]. *Evidence of effect:* not located for private-company enforcement.

**6.5 Whistleblower channels for scientific staff.** **Evidence rating: moderate for accounting fraud in listed firms; UNTESTED for laboratory staff.** *Evidence:* in U.S. listed firms, exposure to the Dodd-Frank whistleblower reward programme was followed by a 12–22% lower likelihood of accounting fraud relative to controls [16]. Evidence specific to laboratory staff or to private companies was not located.

## What this does not replace

This case does not replace medical advice about any blood test, a laboratory's own validation and accreditation records, or the court records of the individual criminal cases. It is not legal advice for investors or patients and does not assess any person's guilt beyond the verdicts reported.
