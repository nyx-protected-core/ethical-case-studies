# Copyright infringement and technology theft in engineering, 1996–2026: who was caught, what the law allows, and whether a long market ban would work

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study. Research and writing only; this is not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026.*

*Three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Found** means a court, jury, trade tribunal, regulator or the party's own plea decided or admitted it. **Alleged** means a prosecutor charged it or a party claimed it; an indictment, complaint or settlement is not a finding, and every person and company charged is presumed innocent unless proven guilty. **Reported** means it is known only from news coverage, which is labelled SECONDARY and used only for dates and sequence. **[GF]** marks a government figure or a government claim of scale (a loss estimate, a fine total announced in a release, or a statement that something is a *record* or a *first*). Agencies and prosecutors announcing results have reasons to present large numbers and firsts, so these figures are reported as the government's own statements. Private individuals are not named; a person is described by role.*

## The ethical question

Engineering firms compete on know-how that takes years and large sums to build: a turbine controller's source code, a fibre-spinning process, a chip design, a casting recipe. When a rival takes that know-how instead of building it, the prize is often not the information itself but a place in a market. This case asks three questions. First, over the thirty years since the United States made trade-secret theft a federal crime, who has actually been caught, in the sense of a conviction, guilty plea, final civil judgment or regulator sanction? Second, what can the law in the United States, in Canada and under international agreements do about it today? Third, would a much heavier sanction, a ban of up to 25 years from the market where the fraud happened, work better than what exists, and what would it cost?

## How the evidence was gathered

- **Scope.** Semiconductors, aerospace, automotive, energy and wind, telecommunications, rail, industrial and chemical engineering, and software engineering, from 1996 to 2026.
- **Inclusion rule.** A row in the master table needs a finding: a conviction, a guilty or no-contest plea, a deferred or non-prosecution agreement in which the company accepted responsibility, a final civil judgment or jury verdict that was entered as a judgment, or a trade-tribunal or procurement sanction. First-instance, appealable, liability-only or partly-remanded findings are included where the opened record supports them, and the status is labelled in the row; they are not treated as final unless the row says so. Charges, pending suits and settlements without a finding go to the *Alleged, not adjudicated* list. Patent and copyright infringement are called infringement. The word theft is used only where a court or a plea found or admitted taking trade secrets or unauthorised access.
- **Records.** Each row links a primary record: a court opinion or judgment, a plea agreement, a Federal Register notice of the US International Trade Commission (ITC), a court's own release, or a Justice Department release announcing a verdict, plea or sentence. Where only a release was opened, the column says so. Court records are preferred to company releases; a company filing is used only where no court record was opened, and is labelled.
- **Not exhaustive.** The search covered the Justice Department's release archive for titles containing *trade secret* and *espionage* (about 350 releases from 2009 to 2026), ITC trade-secret and patent notices already known from companion cases, and targeted searches for leading civil judgments and non-US decisions. Most adjudicated cases in this field are US cases because the US publishes its criminal outcomes in a searchable form; this is a measurement bias, not evidence that theft happens mainly there (see *Unverified or uncertain items*).
- **Verification.** The *Record* column is marked **Verified** where the cited passage was found in the opened record, **Verified (release)** where the finding was read in a government release rather than the judgment, and **UNVERIFIED** where the record did not open.

### 4.2 Canada

Canadian law and policy appear only in this case's law sections: this section and the comparison table in section 5.1. No Canadian data, studies or institutions are used.

- **Copyright Act, s. 42.** Knowing infringement for sale or trade is an offence; on indictment the penalty is a fine of up to $1,000,000, up to five years' prison, or both [102].
- **Patent Act, s. 55.** An infringer is liable to the patentee for all damage sustained after grant, with reasonable compensation for a period before grant [103]. The remedy is civil.
- **Criminal Code, s. 391 (trade secrets).** The offence of knowingly obtaining, communicating or making available a trade secret by deceit, falsehood or other fraudulent means, and of knowingly receiving one so obtained, carries up to 14 years on indictment. Independent development and reverse engineering are expressly not offences. The section in its present form was added by the 2020 amending statute, c. 1, s. 37 [104].
- **Security of Information Act, s. 19 (economic espionage).** Fraudulently communicating, obtaining, retaining, altering or destroying a trade secret at the direction of, for the benefit of, or in association with a foreign economic entity, to the detriment of the country's economic interests, international relations or security, carries up to 10 years. Independent development, reverse engineering and ordinary gains in personal skill are defences [105].
- **Breach of confidence (common law).** The Supreme Court set a three-part test: the information was confidential, it was communicated in confidence, and it was misused by the recipient to the confider's detriment; the remedy can include a constructive trust over what was gained [106] (headnote only).
- **Integrity Regime: Ineligibility and Suspension Policy (2024 version).** An independent Registrar may declare a supplier ineligible for federal contracts for a period ending no more than 10 years after the notice. The Registrar weighs seriousness, including gains realised and repeat violations, against remedial steps. An ineligible supplier may ask for an administrative agreement after 36 months, and the Registrar may accept a criminal remediation agreement as such an agreement [107].
- **Remediation agreements (Criminal Code Part XXII.1).** The purposes include denouncing wrongdoing, accountability through "effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties", reparations to victims, and "to reduce the negative consequences of the wrongdoing for persons — employees, customers, pensioners and others — who did not engage in the wrongdoing" [108]. The trade-secret offence in s. 391 is on the schedule of offences eligible for such an agreement [109].

### 4.3 International

- **TRIPS, Article 39.** Members must let holders prevent secret, commercially valuable information from being disclosed, acquired or used without consent "in a manner contrary to honest commercial practices" [110]. **Article 61** requires criminal procedures at least for wilful trademark counterfeiting and copyright piracy on a commercial scale, with penalties "sufficient to provide a deterrent" [111]. TRIPS does not require criminal penalties for trade-secret theft.
- **Multilateral development bank cross-debarment (2010).** The African, Asian, European (EBRD), Inter-American and World Bank groups agreed to enforce one another's debarments where the decision is public, is for fraud, corruption, coercion or collusion, and the initial debarment exceeds one year [112]. Technology theft is not itself a listed ground. At the World Bank the baseline sanction is three years' debarment with conditional release; in exceptional cases with no realistic prospect of rehabilitation a party may be debarred permanently [113].
- **EU Procurement Directive 2014/24/EU, Article 57.** Conviction by final judgment for listed offences (including participation in a criminal organisation, corruption, fraud, terrorist offences, money laundering, child labour and trafficking) is a mandatory exclusion ground; grave professional misconduct and agreements distorting competition are discretionary grounds. Where a final judgment does not set the period, exclusion may not exceed five years from conviction for mandatory grounds and three years from the relevant event for discretionary grounds. A firm can avoid exclusion by proving self-cleaning: compensation, cooperation with investigators and concrete preventive measures [114]. Technology theft would normally fall under the discretionary grounds unless it is prosecuted as fraud.

## 5. Solutions for the world

Each proposal is rated for the strength of evidence that it would reduce theft without disproportionate harm: **strong** (consistent results from several careful studies), **moderate** (some careful studies, or strong theory with partial evidence), **weak** (theory or indirect evidence only) or **untested** (no direct evidence).

### 5.1 The headline proposal: a market ban of up to 25 years for market share fraud — UNTESTED

**The proposal.** A firm found to have gained market position by fraud, including theft of trade secrets, wilful copyright infringement, bid manipulation or bribery, could be banned for up to 25 years from selling in the market where it gained that position. No jurisdiction surveyed for this case has such a sanction, and no study tests it. It is presented here as a proposal, not as a finding.

**How it compares with existing maximums.**

| Regime | Ordinary maximum | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US federal contracts (FAR) | Generally 3 years | Covers government purchasing only, not private markets [100] |
| EU public procurement | 5 years (mandatory grounds); 3 years (discretionary) | Self-cleaning ends exclusion early [114] |
| World Bank | 3-year baseline; permanent in exceptional cases | Cross-debarred by other development banks if over one year [113][112] |
| Canada (federal contracts) | Up to 10 years, set out in section 4.2 | Administrative agreement possible after 36 months [107] |
| US trade tribunal (section 337) | No fixed maximum; 10-year orders used | Applies to imported products made with the stolen secret, not to the firm as a whole [33][34] |
| **Proposal** | **Up to 25 years** | **Whole-market ban; untested** |

The proposal goes beyond all of these in two ways: length, and scope (a whole market, including private buyers, rather than public contracts or one product line).

**What the deterrence evidence says.** In the economic model of crime, the expected sanction is the probability of being caught times the size of the penalty [115][116]. That model suggests a larger penalty can make up for a low chance of detection. The empirical evidence points the other way. Reviews find that certainty of apprehension deters more consistently than severity [117], and that there is "far less evidence that crime responds to the severity of criminal sanctions" [118]. These reviews are mostly about individual offenders. For companies, a meta-analysis of 58 studies found that punitive sanctions alone did not produce a consistent deterrent effect, while combinations of interventions did [119]. Reputation already punishes firms caught in fraud: for financial misrepresentation, the estimated loss from lost reputation was more than 7.5 times all legal penalties [120]. The evidence therefore supports raising the chance of being caught more strongly than it supports a 25-year ban. **Rating: untested** for the ban itself; **moderate** for the general claim that heavier corporate penalties alone add little.

**Design if it is tried.** A sanction of this weight needs safeguards, or it will fail in court or harm the wrong people.

1. **Graded tiers.** Reserve the longest bans for adjudicated, wilful, senior-management conduct with a large gain; use product-specific exclusions (like the ten-year section 337 orders) for lesser cases [33][34]. The World Bank sets a baseline and adjusts it for aggravating and mitigating factors [113].
2. **An independent tribunal and appeal.** The development-bank agreement requires notice, an opportunity to respond, a decision-maker separate from the investigator, and proportionate sanctions [112]. A market ban should require at least that, with full appeal to a court.
3. **Reinstatement through self-cleaning or a compliance monitor.** EU law ends exclusion when a firm proves compensation, cooperation and preventive measures [114]; the World Bank uses conditional release [113]. A 25-year maximum should be a ceiling that compliance can shorten, not a fixed term.
4. **Protection for workers, customers and suppliers.** The SK Innovation orders allowed imports for two carmakers' programmes for two and four years so they could move to new suppliers [46]. A ban should include transition periods, continued service and spare parts for existing customers, and ring-fencing of the culpable unit where the evidence allows.
5. **Proof standard.** Use the ban only after a criminal conviction, a plea or a final civil judgment of wilful conduct, never on charges alone. Row 3 in the alleged list shows why: a company placed under export controls was later acquitted [79][81].

**Trade-law limits.** A ban that falls on foreign firms more heavily than domestic ones for the same conduct risks breaching national treatment, which requires imported products to receive "treatment no less favourable than that accorded to like products of national origin" [121]. In covered government procurement, the Government Procurement Agreement requires non-discrimination [122] but expressly allows excluding a supplier for "final judgments in respect of serious crimes or other serious offences" or "professional misconduct or acts or omissions that adversely reflect on the commercial integrity of the supplier" [122]. A procurement ban based on a final judgment therefore fits the agreement; a ban from private markets would have to be applied to domestic and foreign firms alike and would rest on general exceptions whose fit was not analysed here. Section 337 orders have been upheld for conduct abroad that injures a domestic industry [32]. **Rating: weak** evidence on how trade tribunals would treat a whole-market ban.

**Competition effects.** Removing a firm reduces the number of competitors. A theoretical analysis of debarment found it makes little difference in highly competitive markets, may deter corruption in concentrated ones, but, when it works, reduces competition and can make collusion among the remaining suppliers easier; it recommends targeting one firm, such as the ring-leader or the beneficiary, rather than all [123]. Empirical work on higher concentration after a large appliance merger found price rises for some products (dishwashers, dryers) and none for others [124]. Engineering markets for wind turbines, memory chips or gas turbines have few suppliers, so a 25-year ban in such a market could raise prices for buyers who did nothing wrong. **Rating: moderate** that removal raises prices in concentrated markets; the size is case-specific.

**Fair counterarguments to the headline proposal.**

- **Severity has weak support.** The strongest evidence says certainty, not severity, deters [117][118]. A 25-year ban that is rarely imposed may deter less than a three-year ban that is reliably imposed.
- **Over-deterrence of legitimate mobility.** Stronger employer-friendly trade-secret rules were associated with less innovation by individual inventors [125], and intellectual-property rights that are too broad reduced follow-on research by 20–30 percent in one well-studied setting [126]. A very heavy sanction invites defensive overreach: firms may refuse to hire experienced engineers from rivals.
- **Litigation as a weapon.** Firms targeted by patent-assertion suits cut their own research after settling or losing [127]. A sanction this large gives claimants a strong reason to bring weak cases.
- **Enforcement is selective.** The adjudicated record is dominated by US prosecutions shaped by US priorities (section 2). A market ban built on that record could look like trade policy to other governments.
- **Protection can help or hurt innovation.** Stronger trade-secret law was associated with 3.2 percent more R&D in pharmaceuticals and 3.1 percent in computers, but no significant change in industrial machinery [128]. Gains from heavier protection are not uniform across engineering sectors.

**How the case handles them.** The proposal is kept as an untested option with a cap, tiers, appeal, reinstatement and protection for third parties, and is ranked below the measures that raise the chance of detection.

## Fair counterarguments

- **Copying versus theft.** TRIPS protects information against acquisition "in a manner contrary to honest commercial practices" [110]. Hiring experienced engineers, reverse engineering and learning from public products are not, in themselves, contrary to honest commercial practices in the regimes reviewed. Some rows in the master table are patent infringement (rows 18, 21 and 37), which requires no intent and no taking. Treating them as theft would be wrong.
- **Selection bias in the record.** The table is built mostly from US releases and US court records. Government releases have reasons to stress firsts and large sums [GF]. Adjudicated non-US court findings are now included from China, Taiwan, Japan, Korea and the United Kingdom. No completed primary record was opened from India, Israel or continental Europe.
- **Juries and appeals cut back many findings.** Kolon's $919.9 million verdict was vacated [17]; Syntel's avoided-cost award was vacated [131]; TCS's punitive damages were capped [37]; Huawei's jury rejected wilful and malicious misappropriation [22]; a defendant charged with UMC was acquitted [79]. A sanction regime must assume that first findings are often reduced.
- **Strong rights have costs.** Evidence that stronger secrecy and broader IP rights can reduce follow-on innovation [125][126] means heavier penalties are not free even when aimed at real wrongdoing.

These points are why the case ranks detection, gain-based remedies and targeted exclusion above a whole-market ban.

## What this does not replace

- **Legal advice.** Nothing here says how any pending or future dispute should be decided. Anyone involved in a trade-secret, patent or copyright matter needs a qualified lawyer in the relevant country.
- **The court record.** Summaries are shortened. Read the cited judgment, plea, order or notice before relying on any figure.
- **A view of any company's present conduct.** A finding describes past conduct at a past date. It is not evidence about a company's current practices, products or staff, and must not be used to judge individual employees.
- **Due diligence, sanctions screening or an investment view.** This case is not a supplier-risk score or a trade-control list.
- **Policy impact assessment.** The 25-year proposal has not been modelled for any market. It must not be used in place of a competition and trade-law analysis of a specific market.
