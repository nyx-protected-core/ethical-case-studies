# Telia (2017): coordinated U.S. and Netherlands resolution of bribes paid to enter the Uzbek telecom market, and a Swedish acquittal

Date: 2026-11-19

**Key to flags.** ⚑ marks a figure published by a government, court or intergovernmental body. These are negotiated amounts and agreed estimates of gain, not measures of harm. Agencies have reasons to present coordinated resolutions as large, and the figures cover only conduct that was detected (detection bias).

---

## 1. Summary

On 21 September 2017, **Telia Company AB** (formerly TeliaSonera AB), the Stockholm-based telecom group, and its Uzbek subsidiary **Coscom LLC** resolved charges that they paid about **$331 million ⚑** in bribes between 2007 and 2010. The bribes went to an Uzbek government official who, according to the DOJ, had influence over the Uzbek body that regulated telecoms, in order to enter and operate in Uzbekistan's mobile market [R1], [R3], [R4].

The resolution had four parts:
- Telia entered a **deferred prosecution agreement (DPA)** with the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ).
- Coscom **pleaded guilty** in the Southern District of New York.
- Telia **consented to an SEC cease-and-desist order**.
- Three Netherlands subsidiaries accepted an **out-of-court settlement (transactie)** with the Netherlands Public Prosecution Service (OM).

The combined amount was **$965,773,949 ⚑** [R1], [R2], [R5].

In **Sweden**, criminal charges against three former executives ended in **acquittal**, in the district court in 2019 and on appeal in 2021. A **forfeiture claim of $208.5 million ⚑** against Telia was **dismissed**. The reason was not that no payments were made. The courts found it **not proven** that the recipient fell within the circle of persons who could take bribes under the Swedish law then in force [R6], [R7]. That $208.5 million was then paid to the Netherlands OM in March 2019 under the 2017 settlement terms [R8].

**Evidence strength:** **strong** for the U.S. and Netherlands admissions and sanctions. For Sweden, the record is an **acquittal**, which this case reports as such without naming the individuals.

**Countries and contracts.** All the conduct concerned Uzbekistan: mobile licences, frequencies, channels and number blocks. The SEC order notes that private parties "could not sell or purchase" these in Uzbekistan, which gave a government-linked official control over market entry [R4, ¶¶14–15]. The recipient is not named in this case.

- **2007–2010:** Bribe payments [R3], [R4], [R9].
- **2012:** Swedish media report the payments. A Swedish criminal investigation and a Telia internal investigation begin. Further payments are contemplated [R2], [R10].

- **5 Apr 2021:** after the DPA term ends, the US information is dismissed on the government's motion [R1].

## 4. How the scheme worked, as found in the record

- **Payments to a front company.** Bribes were paid to a front company "under the guise of payments for lobbying and consulting services that never actually occurred" [R5]. The SEC order describes "sham lobbying and consulting services" [R4, ¶2].
- **A regulated asset as the bribe channel.** In a July 2007 agreement, the official's company was to contribute "licenses, frequencies, and" related regulated assets, which in Uzbekistan only the state could allocate [R4, ¶¶19–20].
- **Management knowledge.** "Certain Telia management" knew the front company was beneficially owned by the official [R2], [R3].
- **Same official, other firms.** The DOJ's 2016 VimpelCom resolution concerns bribes to the same kind of official in the same market, and says three telecom companies together paid "more than $800 million" (see vimpelcom-2016) [R11].

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **No monitor**, according to the company's statement [R10]. The U.S. DPA (term not extracted from the scanned PDF) relied on self-reporting and cooperation.
- **Remediation the company reported:** dismissal of all individuals involved, a new compliance function, a new anti-corruption programme and a governance overhaul [R10] (company statement, not opened; **UNVERIFIED**). The OM's 2017 release records a new CEO and CFO and an internal whistleblower scheme [R9].
- **Peer-reviewed context.** Cooley & Sharman (2015) analyse transnational corruption networks in Central Asia. They argue these networks join multinational companies, host-country elites, offshore vehicles, brokers and destination banks, and they include a case on telecommunications contracts in Uzbekistan [P1]. Cooley & Sharman (2017) argue that professionals in major financial centres "lower the transaction costs of transnational corruption by senior foreign officials" [P2].

## 7. Fair counterarguments

1. **Admitted abroad, acquitted at home.** Telia admitted bribery in the U.S. and accepted a Netherlands bribery settlement. Yet Swedish courts acquitted the executives and dismissed the forfeiture claim, because under the Swedish law of the time it was not proven that the recipient was a person who could legally receive a bribe [R6], [R7]. The two results do not contradict each other on the facts. The Swedish court found the transactions "objectively" occurred [R6]. They differ on legal definitions and burdens of proof. Readers should not treat the Swedish acquittal as a finding that no bribery happened, or the U.S. admission as a finding against any individual.
2. **Negotiated settlements versus trials.** The OM notes that Sweden has no out-of-court settlement like the Netherlands, so it had to go to court [R8]. Negotiated settlements deliver sanctions faster. Trials test evidence against individuals. Lord (2022) argues that non-trial resolutions can leave corporations "exempt, not just deferred" [P3].
3. **Who should receive the money?** The money went to the U.S., the Netherlands and, potentially, Sweden. None of it went to Uzbekistan, the country whose market was distorted [R2], [R8]. The OM states that it makes no difference to the Netherlands which country receives the proceeds, as long as it is taken away [R8]. Others argue the harmed population should benefit. **Open question:** whether later asset-return arrangements to Uzbekistan involved Telia-linked funds was not verified.
4. **Double-counting.** Headline coverage cited "$965 million" and sometimes "over $1 billion". The DOJ explains that the gross amount exceeded $1bn but crediting reduced the net to $965.8m [R2]. Bu (2022) argues for predictable global crediting [P4].

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that Telia and Coscom admitted bribery, because that rests on a DPA, a guilty plea and an SEC order, with a parallel Netherlands settlement. It gives the Swedish acquittals full weight: no Swedish court found that the individuals committed bribery, and the case does not suggest otherwise. The difference is explained by the legal test in Sweden, not by different facts.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Close definitional gaps in who can be bribed.** The Swedish result turned on whether the recipient fell within a legally defined circle [R6], [R7]. Laws should cover persons who exercise *de facto* control over state decisions, such as a person operating through front companies. *Basis:* [R6], [R7]; Cooley & Sharman on how influence is exercised through networks [P1]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Rests on a legal gap shown by the case, not on an evaluated reform.*
2. **Make beneficial-ownership checks mandatory before buying regulated assets.** Regulated assets such as licences and spectrum should be bought from or through private parties only after the beneficial owner is verified and disclosed to regulators. *Basis:* the admitted scheme used a front company to channel regulated assets [R4]; Cooley & Sharman on professional enablers [P2]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
3. **Create a restitution path for the country whose market was distorted**, with independent oversight so funds do not return to the people who took the bribes. *Basis:* [R8]; §7.3. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
4. **Publish crediting tables.** *Basis:* [R2]; Bu [P4]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
5. **Require monitors to be decided and disclosed by authorities, not announced by companies.** *Basis:* the only statement found that no monitor was imposed is the company's own [R10]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*

## 10. Recommendation (normative)

Use Telia to show readers that **the same facts can produce an admission in one system and an acquittal in another**. Present both results side by side, name only the companies, and explain which legal element (who counts as a bribeable official) made the difference.

## 12. What this does not replace

This case is not a finding against any individual; the Swedish courts acquitted the former executives who were tried. It does not replace the DPA, the plea agreement, the SEC order, the Netherlands settlement or the Swedish judgments. It is not legal advice on Swedish bribery law or the FCPA.
