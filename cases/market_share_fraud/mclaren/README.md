# McLaren and Ferrari's technical data: a sport's regulator excludes a team and fines it US$100 million

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study. Research and writing only; this is not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Found**: what a court, jury, regulator or plea record decided or admitted. **Alleged**: what a prosecutor charged or a party claimed; an indictment or complaint is not a finding. **Reported**: facts known only from news coverage, labelled SECONDARY. Figures are quoted as the source gives them. Government figures (prosecution estimates, agency loss figures) are flagged, because agencies and prosecutors have reasons to present large numbers when announcing results. Individuals are not named except where a public office or a court record makes the name part of the public finding; private individuals are not named.*

## The ethical question

In motor racing, the market is a championship, and with it sponsorship, prize money and prestige. When a team's staff receive a rival's confidential technical data, is the team responsible even if senior management did not plan it? The sport's regulator answered that the team was responsible, and that the penalty should take away the season's constructors' result, not only charge a fine.

## Penalty against market effect

- **Sanction:** exclusion from the 2007 Constructors' Championship and a US$100 million fine (net of withheld payments) [1].
- **Market effect.** The Council found "some degree" of sporting advantage but did not put a figure on it [1]. This case makes no estimate of the commercial value of the information.

## Analysis

The McLaren decision is unusual in this category in two ways. First, the regulator removed the **prize itself** (the constructors' result) rather than only charging money. That is the clearest example here of a remedy that takes away the market position the conduct could have helped win, which is what proposal 3 below recommends.

Second, the decision rejected a "rogue employee" defence as a reason for no penalty once dissemination within the team was shown [1]. The Council said it need not show that the information produced any specific sporting advantage before imposing a penalty [1]. That lowers the evidential bar for organisational responsibility, which strengthens deterrence but also raises fairness questions (see below).

The economic model of deterrence suggests that a sanction which removes the gain and is likely to be imposed deters more than a larger but uncertain fine [2][3]. A sport's governing body can act faster than courts, and here it did so within weeks of new evidence.

## Fair counterarguments

- **Legitimate protection versus litigation as a weapon.** Suing a rival over intellectual property is often legitimate protection of research. But litigation can also be used to raise a competitor's costs; research on suits by patent holders that do not make products found that targeted firms substantially reduce their innovative activity after settling or losing [4]. A finding in court shows that a line was crossed; it does not show that every claim the winning side made was right.
- **Learning is not taking.** The international standard protects undisclosed information only against acquisition "contrary to honest commercial practices" [5], which is narrower than all copying. Learning from public products and hiring experienced people are lawful and are how skills spread. This case concerns confidential technical information that a sport's governing body found was held and used by a rival team, not learning from public observation of rivals' cars or hiring experienced engineers.
- **The costs of litigation.** Long cases consume money and management attention on both sides, and delay can hurt the winner as much as the loser. Remedies that depend on ever-longer litigation are a weak answer to a problem of speed.
- **Collective punishment.** Exclusion affected employees who had nothing to do with the information. The Council answered in part by not sanctioning the drivers, but the team's staff bore the result.
- **A low bar for a heavy sanction.** The Council did not require proof of a specific advantage [1]. A private regulator applying a lower standard than a court, with a very large fine, can be criticised as disproportionate.
- **The first decision imposed no penalty.** The same body reached the opposite result on more limited evidence in July [1]. Results that move so sharply with new evidence show how much depends on detection.

## Proposed solutions

The proposals below are this case's own reasoning, built on the cited evidence. Each carries an evidence rating for how well research supports the measure itself: **strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **UNTESTED** (no study cited here tests whether the measure works).

1. **Make the expected penalty larger than the gain, mainly by raising the chance of being caught.** In the standard economic model, a firm deterred only by money compares the gain with the probability of a sanction times its size [2][6]. Reviews of the evidence find that certainty of apprehension deters more consistently than severity [3], and that single punitive sanctions against companies do not deter consistently while combinations of measures (regulation, inspection, sanctions together) do [7]. Detection funding, forensic review of departing-employee transfers in litigation, and protected reporting channels therefore matter more than headline fines. *Evidence rating: moderate. The reviews cited are broad; none tests trade-secret or infringement enforcement specifically.*
2. **Measure the remedy by the gain, not only the victim's provable loss.** US civil law already allows recovery of unjust enrichment and up to double exemplary damages for wilful and malicious trade-secret theft [8], and criminal fines for organisations can reach three times the value of the stolen secret, including avoided research and design costs [9]. Courts and prosecutors should use the gain-based measures the law already allows, and report the gain they found, so that the public can compare penalty with benefit. *Evidence rating: weak. Supported by enforcement theory [2][6]; UNTESTED as a measured change in behaviour.*
3. **Take away the product advantage, not the whole firm.** Where products are made with misappropriated technology, a product-specific exclusion, verified by an independent technical expert and lasting at least as long as the head start gained, removes the market benefit while sparing customers and workers who did nothing wrong. A ten-year US exclusion order for products made with misappropriated trade secrets is an existing precedent [10][11]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. A legal precedent exists; no study cited here measures its deterrent effect.*
4. **Protect the people who report it.** US law gives immunity to a person who discloses a trade secret in confidence to officials or to a lawyer in order to report a suspected violation [12]. Similar protection, and a clear duty to report in engineering codes, should be standard. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. The legal protection exists; no study cited here measures its effect on detection.*
5. **Do not over-correct into employer-friendly secrecy.** Stronger trade-secret law was associated with more R&D in some industries [13], but stronger employer-friendly secrecy rules were associated with less innovation by individual inventors [14], and intellectual-property rights that are too broad can reduce follow-on research [15]. Remedies should target taking and using confidential material, not ordinary job mobility or learning. *Evidence rating: moderate. Three peer-reviewed empirical studies, with mixed directions by industry and by inventor.*
6. **Remove the prize, not only charge a fee.** In competitions with a defined prize, the sanction should withdraw the result the misconduct could have affected, as here [1]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. One precedent; no study cited here measures its deterrent effect.*

## What this does not replace

- **Legal advice or a legal opinion.** Nothing here says how any pending or future dispute should be decided. Anyone involved in a trade-secret, patent, copyright or related matter needs a qualified lawyer in the relevant country.
- **The court record itself.** Summaries here are shortened. Where a figure or finding matters, read the cited judgment, plea or notice.
- **A company's current conduct or compliance standing.** A finding describes past conduct at a past date. It is not evidence about the company's present practices, products or staff, and must not be used to judge individual employees.
- **Due diligence or a sanctions screen.** This case is not a supplier-risk score, a trade-control list or an investment recommendation.
