# Airbus SE (2020): coordinated French, UK and US bribery resolutions

Date: 2026-11-19

**How to read the figures.** Every money amount or count below that comes from a government agency or court is marked **[⚑ agency figure]**. That mark means: this is an agency-reported figure; agencies have an incentive to report headline totals; credits and offsets between jurisdictions can mean double-counting; and what was found depends on what investigators looked for (detection bias), so it is not a measure of all the bribery that happened.

## 1. Summary

- **What was found.** Between 28 and 31 January 2020, Airbus SE concluded three coordinated resolutions (the CJIP was signed on 29 January and validated on 31 January; the UK DPA was approved on 31 January; the US DPA is dated 31 January):
  - a French *convention judiciaire d'intérêt public* (CJIP) with the Parquet National Financier (PNF), validated by the President of the Paris *tribunal judiciaire*;
  - a UK deferred prosecution agreement (DPA) with the Serious Fraud Office (SFO), approved by the Crown Court in a public judgment;
  - a US DPA with the Department of Justice (DOJ) covering FCPA bribery conspiracy and arms-export (AECA/ITAR) conspiracy.

  None of these is a conviction. Each is a court-approved negotiated resolution resting on an agreed statement of facts, so the conduct is **admitted and court-approved**, not proven at trial.
- **Scale.** The DOJ called it the largest global foreign-bribery resolution to date, at more than $3.9 billion **[⚑ agency figure]**. That total includes the US export-control part. The French and UK parts were €2,083,137,455 and €990,963,712 (including costs) **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Result.** Airbus complied. The UK DPA expired on 31 January 2023 and the SFO discontinued the case on 13 February 2023. The DOJ moved on 28 July 2023 to dismiss the US charges with prejudice.

**Countries and contracts named in the record:**
- **UK counts** (approved judgment):
  - **Malaysia:** a $50m payment, presented as sponsorship of a sports team, to directors or employees of AirAsia and AirAsia X, with another $55m offered but not paid **[⚑ agency figure]**. Those airlines ordered 180 aircraft during the indictment period.
  - **Sri Lanka:** up to $16.84m offered through a straw company, of which $2m was paid, to influence SriLankan Airlines' purchase of 10 aircraft and lease of 4 **[⚑ agency figure]**. Airbus employees also gave misleading answers to UK Export Finance.
  - **Taiwan:** $2,432,500 and $11,902,500 paid to two intermediary companies, for a TransAsia Airways director and employee, while TransAsia bought 20 aircraft **[⚑ agency figure]**.
  - **Indonesia:** more than $3.3m paid to or for employees of Garuda Indonesia and Citilink, linked to the purchase of 55 aircraft **[⚑ agency figure]**.
  - **Ghana:** about €5m in success commissions promised or paid to a close relative of a senior elected official, in connection with the proposed sale of three C-295 military transport aircraft to the Government of Ghana **[⚑ agency figure]**.

  The airlines are named only as the purchasing customers in the record. The bribes went to individual employees or directors, and the record makes no finding against the airlines themselves.
- **French investigation scope** (CJIP para 43): United Arab Emirates, China, South Korea, Nepal, India, Taiwan, Russia, Saudi Arabia, Vietnam, Japan, Turkey, Mexico, Thailand, Brazil, Kuwait and Colombia. Examples set out in the CJIP include:
  - a China Aviation Cooperation Fund;
  - a Nepal Airlines campaign with a payment commitment of $1.8m **[⚑ agency figure]**;
  - a China Airlines (Taiwan) A350 campaign;
  - a Russian state satellite operator;
  - an Arabsat transaction;
  - a sales campaign involving a Colombian intermediary.
- **US** (DOJ): bribes to Chinese officials through a business partner, with payments sent to a Hong Kong account in the name of another partner's company (2013–2015). Separately, false or missing ITAR Part 130 reports of commissions on defence sales (December 2011 to December 2016).
- **Joint investigation** (CJIP para 42): the Franco-British Joint Investigation Team reviewed more than 1,750 intermediaries engaged up to 2016 and concentrated on 110 with red flags **[⚑ agency figure]**.

No individual is named in this case. The court documents anonymise employees and intermediaries. Whether any individual has been convicted was not checked (see *Unverified or uncertain items*).

## 3. Timeline (news-sourced items are labelled SECONDARY)

- 24 Apr 2015: UK Export Finance writes to Airbus about anti-bribery due diligence and asks about a Sri Lankan business partner (judgment para 31).
- October 2014 to 2015: Airbus freezes payments to business partners. The judgment says this freeze prevented some intended corrupt payments.
- Mar to Apr 2016: Airbus makes disclosures to UK Export Finance. UK Export Finance and Airbus both report to the SFO on 1 April 2016 (judgment para 32).
- 15 Jul 2016: the SFO opens a criminal investigation. 20 Jul 2016: the PNF opens a preliminary inquiry (PNF press release).
- 28 to 31 Jan 2020: the US information is filed, the CJIP and both DPAs are concluded and approved.
- 31 Jan 2023: the UK DPA expires. 13 Feb 2023: the SFO discontinues (Details of compliance).
- 28 Jul 2023: the DOJ files an unopposed motion to dismiss with prejudice. No dismissal order was opened (**UNVERIFIED**).

## 4. How the scheme worked (as found in the record)

- **Business partners as conduits.** The UK judgment and the CJIP describe a central "Strategy and Marketing Organisation" unit (closed 1 March 2016) that engaged intermediaries ("business partners"). Payments to them were passed on to people who influenced purchasing decisions.
- **Disguised payments.** The record describes sponsorship of a sports team (Malaysia), a straw company fronting for a decision-maker's spouse (Sri Lanka), split payments through two intermediaries described in coded email language (Taiwan), and false documentation supporting success commissions to a relative of a decision-maker (Ghana).
- **Misleading export-credit and export-control declarations.** False answers were given to UK Export Finance (Sri Lanka), and commissions were under-reported to the US State Department under ITAR Part 130 (DOJ).
- **Third-country routing.** Payments for China went to a Hong Kong account controlled by a different partner (DOJ).

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **No external monitor in the UK.** The SFO did not ask for an external monitor because of the board's remediation and because the AFA was given a three-year compliance-checking role under the CJIP (judgment para 81).
- **France.** AFA checks for three years, reporting at least once a year to the PNF (CJIP paras 179–180).
- **Compliance confirmed.** The SFO's "Details of compliance" records that Airbus co-operated fully and paid the disgorgement and penalty. The DOJ motion records that the DPA required dismissal within six months of expiry if Airbus complied, and the DOJ moved to dismiss.
- **Breaches.** None found in the records opened.

## 6. Evidence strength

- **Strong** for the instruments, dates and amounts: an approved public judgment, the CJIP and validation materials, the DOJ DPA and the dismissal motion were all opened.
- **Moderate-to-strong** for the facts of the scheme. They come from agreed statements of facts that the company accepted and a judge reviewed, but no trial tested them. The English CJIP is a translation "for information purposes", and only the French text is binding.
- **Unknown** for the full extent. The French and UK teams each "selected a representative sample" of markets (CJIP para 44), so the record is a sample, not a census.

## 7. Fair counterarguments

- **Are DPAs justice?** Lord (2022, *British Journal of Criminology*) analyses how judges in England and Wales justify approving DPAs. He argues that the conditions for using DPAs have produced an infrastructure in which companies are "exempt, not just deferred," from prosecution. De Vita and Vozza (2024) conclude that DPAs "do not foster ethical behavior". Bu (2021) argues that DPAs can deter, punish and reshape corporate behaviour while avoiding collateral harm. The Airbus judgment itself weighs that collateral harm to employees and the wider industry.
- **Self-report or caught?** The judgment notes that the "true catalyst" was the watchfulness of UK Export Finance, not an unprompted self-report. Airbus still received credit for its later disclosure and co-operation. This is a live debate about how much credit firms should get when disclosure follows outside pressure.
- **Double-counting and allocation.** The DOJ credited about $1.8bn of its own penalty against the French fine **[⚑ agency figure]**, and the French CJIP deducted the €265.95m paid to the DOJ. Headline totals ($3.9bn, or €3.6bn) can therefore be mis-added. Hock (2020) describes how settlements "bundle" allegations and enforcers, which brings efficiency but also discounts. Bu (2022) discusses double jeopardy across sovereigns.
- **Organisational explanation.** Boakye, Siaw and Sarpong (2022) argue that executives institutionalised bribery through "collective myopia" and that compliance policies served as "smokescreens". Their data are court-approved documents, company material and news.
- **Compliance as signalling.** Hock and Dávid-Barrett (2022) find in interviews that companies use compliance programmes to signal good character, and that enforcers accept these signals as grounds for settling or leniency. This is relevant to the weight the Airbus judgment placed on remediation.

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its finding that Airbus admitted bribery through business partners, because it rests on agreed statements of facts approved by a French and an English court. It takes the DPA critique seriously: the conduct was never tested at trial, and the UK judgment itself says outside pressure from UK Export Finance, not a self-report, started the case. Those points limit how far the record shows the full extent of the conduct, which is why the case calls the record a sample and not a census.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Make "self-report" credit depend on timing.** The judgment shows that disclosure followed pressure from an export-credit agency. Credit should scale with how early and how voluntary disclosure was. This is consistent with the concerns of Søreide and Vagle (2022, DOI 10.1007/s10657-022-09726-9) that large settlement discounts weaken deterrence. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative, derived from the record.*
2. **Use export-credit agencies as detection points.** UK Export Finance's questions about an agent's background triggered this case (judgment paras 31–32). Export-credit and arms-export agencies should require and verify disclosure of intermediaries and commissions (compare ITAR Part 130 in the DOJ record). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative, derived from the record.*
3. **Publish one net global figure.** Agencies should state the amount actually retained by each jurisdiction after credits. The DOJ, PNF and SFO numbers here overlap through credits (Bu 2022; Hock 2020). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). A reporting reform; its effect has not been measured.*
4. **Check that compliance is real, not assumed.** Where a monitor is waived (as in the UK here), require independent testing with published summaries, such as the AFA checks under the CJIP. Hock and Dávid-Barrett (2022) show that compliance "symbols" can be over-credited. *Evidence rating: weak. Hock and Dávid-Barrett (2022) give qualitative interview evidence that compliance symbols are over-credited; the remedy itself is untested.*
5. **Track individual accountability publicly.** Record what happened to the individuals behind each corporate resolution, so that corporate payments do not quietly stand in for individual responsibility (Lord 2022; De Vita and Vozza 2024). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative; no study of public tracking was found.*

## 11. What this does not replace

This case does not replace the CJIP, the UK approved judgment or the US DPA, which hold the binding text (only the French CJIP is authoritative; the English version is a translation). It is not a compliance assessment of Airbus or of the airlines named as customers, which the record does not accuse. It is not legal advice on the Bribery Act 2010, French CJIP law or the FCPA and ITAR.
