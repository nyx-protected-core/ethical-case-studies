# The Forex-Rigging Banks: the 2015 Guilty Pleas, the Regulator Orders and the EU Cartel Decisions

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. The banks covered here (Citigroup, JPMorgan, Barclays, RBS, UBS, HSBC and others) face findings against the institutions themselves: US guilty pleas, CFTC orders, UK final notices, Federal Reserve orders and European Commission cartel decisions. A 2018 jury acquittal of three former traders is reported as an outcome about individuals. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies sum amounts across bodies.*

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers collusion and attempted manipulation in the spot foreign exchange (FX) market by large dealer banks between about 2007 and 2013. The centre of the case is the May 2015 parent-level guilty pleas by Citicorp, JPMorgan Chase & Co., Barclays PLC and The Royal Bank of Scotland plc to a Sherman Act conspiracy in the euro/US dollar market, and UBS AG's guilty plea in the same announcement. It also covers the November 2014 US and UK regulator orders, the May 2015 Federal Reserve orders, the European Commission's 2019 and 2021 cartel decisions, the reforms that followed, and the 2018 acquittal of three individual traders.

- **Findings first.** The Findings section lists only adjudicated or admitted findings (pleas, regulator orders and final notices, Commission decisions), each with its primary record. The 2018 trial outcome is reported as a court outcome concerning individuals, not as a finding about the banks.
- **Source tags** are defined at the top of Sources. One news report is used, labelled **[SECONDARY — news]**, for a timeline point only.
- **Government figures are marked ⚑.** FX penalty totals are routinely added across agencies and countries ("nearly $9 billion"), mix criminal fines with civil penalties and disgorgement, and include settlement discounts and leniency reductions. They are enforcement outcomes, not measures of customer loss.
- **People.** No individual is named. Chatroom names appear because they are the names the Commission gave its decisions; personal names inside them are omitted.
- **Times of day.** Benchmark fixes are referred to by name (the WM/Reuters London closing fix, the European Central Bank reference fix), not by clock time.
- **Evidence strength.** Findings are rated **Strong**, **Moderate**, **Limited** or **Very limited** for how firmly the record supports them. Proposed solutions carry a separate evidence rating: strong, moderate, weak or UNTESTED.

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts, each with its primary record)

### 1.1 US criminal pleas (May 2015)

- Citicorp, JPMorgan Chase & Co., Barclays PLC and The Royal Bank of Scotland plc agreed to plead guilty to a one-count felony charge of conspiring to fix prices and rig bids for US dollars and euros in the FX spot market [1]. The DOJ set fines "proportional to its involvement": Citicorp $925 million; Barclays $650 million; JPMorgan $550 million; RBS $395 million ⚑ [1].
- According to the plea agreements, between December 2007 and January 2013, euro/dollar traders at the four banks, "self-described members of 'The Cartel'", used an exclusive chatroom and coded language to coordinate trading around the two major daily fixes and to agree to withhold bids or offers so as to protect each other's positions [1].
- **Barclays** also agreed that its FX conduct violated its June 2012 LIBOR non-prosecution agreement and agreed to pay a further $60 million ⚑ [1].
- **UBS AG** agreed to plead guilty to the LIBOR-related wire fraud charge it had avoided in 2012 and pay $203 million ⚑, because the DOJ found it had breached its 2012 NPA. The factual statement of breach describes undisclosed markups added to customers' FX transactions, misrepresentations that no markups were being added, and the use of hand signals to conceal them [1].
- All five banks agreed to three years of corporate probation, subject to court approval [1].

*Status: admitted (plea agreements). Strength: Strong. The Justice Department release could not be opened in this check, so the amounts and quoted wording in this subsection are UNVERIFIED against it; the underlying conduct is also found in the opened CFTC, FCA, Federal Reserve and Commission records [2; 3; 5; 6].*

### 1.2 US and UK regulator orders (November 2014)

- **CFTC:** orders against Citibank, HSBC, JPMorgan, RBS and UBS for attempted manipulation of FX benchmark rates, with civil penalties of $310 million each for Citibank and JPMorgan, $290 million each for RBS and UBS, and $275 million for HSBC (over $1.4 billion ⚑) [2]. The orders find that traders used private, sometimes invitation-only, chatrooms to disclose confidential customer order information and positions and to agree trading strategies to attempt to manipulate benchmark rates, with conduct for some banks starting in 2009 and continuing into 2012 [2].
- **UK FCA:** fines totalling £1,114,918,000 ⚑ on Citibank, HSBC, JPMorgan, RBS and UBS for failing to control G10 spot FX trading. Without settlement discounts the total would have been £1,592,740,000 ⚑ [3]. The FCA found that traders shared confidential client information, "attempted to manipulate G10 spot FX currency rates, including in collusion with traders at other firms", and tried to trigger client stop-loss orders [3].
- The FCA release also records FINMA disgorgement of CHF 134 million from UBS and an OCC penalty of $700 million ⚑ [3].
- **Barclays (May 2015):** the FCA imposed £284,432,000 ⚑ for failing to control its London FX business [4].

*Status: regulator sanctions on settled facts. Strength: Strong.*

### 1.3 Federal Reserve orders (May 2015)

The Federal Reserve imposed fines of more than $1.8 billion ⚑ for "unsafe and unsound practices" in FX: $342 million each for UBS, Barclays, Citigroup and JPMorgan Chase; $274 million for RBS; $205 million for Bank of America [5]. Bank of America's order concerns failure to detect and address traders discussing similar agreements; the Fed notes Bank of America was not charged by the DOJ [5]. The Fed barred the banks from re-employing individuals involved [5].

*Strength: Strong.*

### 1.4 European Commission cartel decisions (2019 and 2021)

- **May 2019 (settlement):** fines totalling about €1.07 billion ⚑ for two cartels in spot trading of 11 currencies: "Three Way Banana Split" (Barclays, RBS, Citigroup and JPMorgan; €811,197,000; 18 December 2007 – 31 January 2013) and "Essex Express" (Barclays, RBS and MUFG Bank; €257,682,000) [6]. Traders exchanged outstanding customer orders, bid-ask spreads, open risk positions and trading plans, and occasionally coordinated, for example by "standing down" to avoid interfering with one another [6]. UBS received full immunity [6].
- **December 2021 (Sterling Lads):** fines of €344 million ⚑: Barclays €54,348,000; RBS €32,472,000; HSBC €174,281,000 (all settled); Credit Suisse €83,294,000 (ordinary procedure; no leniency or settlement reduction, but a 4% reduction because it was not held liable for all aspects of the case); UBS €0 (immunity) [7]. Whether the Credit Suisse decision was appealed, and with what result, was not checked (UNVERIFIED).

*Strength: Strong (settlement decisions with admitted liability; Credit Suisse decision adopted under the ordinary procedure).*

### 1.5 Individual prosecution in the United States: acquittal (2018)

In October 2018 a New York federal jury acquitted three former London-based traders charged with using "The Cartel" chatroom to rig euro/dollar rates [12, SECONDARY]. The court docket (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:17-cr-00019) could not be opened, so the verdict is UNVERIFIED against a primary record. This is a court outcome concerning those individuals. It does not undo the four banks' pleas, which rest on corporate admissions [1].

## How the scheme worked

1. **The fixes.** Many large customers trade at a published benchmark ("fix") rather than a negotiated price. The WM/Reuters London closing fix was then computed from trades and quotes in a one-minute window [8]. Dealers receiving large fix orders from customers had to buy or sell around that window.
2. **Information sharing.** Traders at competing banks pooled information on their net customer orders at the fix and their positions [2; 3; 6]. Knowing the combined order flow, they could coordinate who traded and when, including "standing down", to move the fix in a direction that benefited them [1; 6].
3. **Withholding supply or demand.** The DOJ record describes agreements not to buy or sell at certain times to protect co-conspirators' positions, a classic cartel restriction of competition [1].
4. **Customer-facing deception.** Separate from collusion, UBS added undisclosed markups and misrepresented them to customers [1]; the FCA found attempts to trigger client stop-loss orders [3].
5. **Weak controls.** Regulators found inadequate policies on chatroom use and on trading around fixes [2; 3]. A crime-script study of the benchmark cases found that organisational systems acted both as guardians and as facilitators of misconduct [20].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

- **Prices around the fix behaved abnormally.** Peer-reviewed analysis of 21 currencies over a decade found "extraordinary volatility and negative serial correlation" in prices around the WM/Reuters fix, contrary to a model of competitive trading [14]. A model of dealer conduct at a fix shows that front-running, information sharing and collusion generate high pre-fix volatility and partial reversals, and tests on FX data support it [15].
- **Customers paid hidden costs.** The admitted markup scheme at UBS [1] and the stop-loss conduct found by the FCA [3] harmed identifiable customers, but no adjudicated aggregate loss figure was located.

*Strength: Moderate that the conduct distorted prices near the fix; Very limited for any total loss figure.*

## Fair counterarguments

1. **Is there a price footprint at all?** One working paper found "little evidence of volatile movement (or spikes)" around the London fix and noted that liquidity at the fix is unusually deep, so any single trade has less impact [19, WP]. Peer-reviewed work finds the opposite pattern in a longer, broader sample [14]. The two may differ because of data, window and currency coverage.
2. **Innocent explanations for fix patterns.** Price movements around a fix can have explanations other than collusion, such as dealers trading ahead of large customer orders they must fill, and studies differ on whether there is a price footprint at all (Fair counterarguments, item 1). A price pattern alone does not prove collusion; the chatroom evidence and admissions do [1; 2; 6].
3. **Did the reforms fix it?** After the 2015 change to a five-minute window, the volume spike in the fixing window disappeared, but price anomalies remained, and banks now incur higher execution costs to avoid even the appearance of collusion [18, WP]. A pre-registered peer-reviewed study assesses whether the revised methodology is representative and robust [17].
4. **Criminal law and individuals.** The 2018 acquittal [12, SECONDARY] shows that juries can distinguish between chatroom information sharing and proven agreement to fix prices. Some argue that competition law alone cannot prevent recurrence and that ex ante market-structure regulation is needed [16].
5. **Headline totals ⚑.** "Nearly $9 billion" [1] combines amounts from several US and foreign agencies; Commission fines include settlement and leniency reductions and an immune firm pays nothing [6; 7]. The penalty ranking therefore does not rank wrongdoing.
6. **Recidivism.** The same institutions appear in LIBOR and FX resolutions, and Barclays and UBS breached LIBOR agreements through FX conduct [1]. JPMorgan's later 2020 spoofing resolution lists the 2015 FX plea as prior criminal history [13].

**How the conclusion handles these points.** Each bank's entry rests on its own plea, order or settlement decision, not on price studies or individual trials. The case does not estimate customer losses, and it treats the acquittal as a limit on what can be proved against individuals rather than as a reversal of the corporate findings.

## Values (labelled section: judgement, not fact)

*This section states value positions. It is not a finding.*

- **Fiduciary-like trust.** Customers who give a bank a fix order are trusting it to execute at a fair benchmark. Using that order flow against them, or sharing it with rivals, breaks a trust the customer cannot monitor.
- **Competition is a duty, not a courtesy.** "Standing down" and withholding orders are polite words for not competing. Markets rely on rivals actually competing.
- **Proportion and fairness to individuals.** The acquittal [12, SECONDARY] is a reminder that conduct that looks bad in chat logs must still be proved to a criminal standard.
- **Repeat offending.** When the same institutions return under new agreements, the public interest in credible escalation (monitors, probation, licence consequences) grows.

## Proposed solutions (each sourced and rated for evidence)
