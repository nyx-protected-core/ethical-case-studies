# WorldCom: Capitalised Line Costs, a Record Penalty Paid in Bankruptcy, and Court-Ordered Governance Reform

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. WorldCom is included because a federal court entered a final judgment against the company itself in the SEC's fraud case, with a civil penalty of $2.25 billion that was satisfied in bankruptcy by $750 million in cash and stock paid to victims. Criminal verdicts concern former officers and are reported by role. **Found** means decided by a court or regulator or admitted in a plea; **Alleged** means charged or claimed. Government and court-approved figures carry a ⚑ flag.*

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers the accounting fraud at WorldCom, Inc., a U.S. long-distance and data telecommunications company that disclosed false accounting in June 2002 and filed for bankruptcy the following month. It covers the SEC case and penalty, the criminal convictions, the board's investigation, the court-appointed monitor's governance report, and the evidence on remedies.

- **Findings versus claims.** The Findings section lists only convictions, pleas, court judgments and the board committee's conclusions (marked as the company's own). The SEC's complaint is an allegation document; it is used for what the SEC alleged and was later resolved by judgment.
- **Source tags.** **[CT]** court or prosecutor record; **[REG]** regulator; **[CO/GOV]** board committee report; **[CT/GOV]** report of a court-appointed monitor; **[LAW]** statute; **[PR]** peer-reviewed research.
- **Basis for inclusion.** The company-level finding is the SEC final judgment against WorldCom, Inc. (1.3) and the court-ordered governance reform under the SEC's injunction (1.6).
- **Government and court-approved figures are marked ⚑**, with notes on how they were built.
- **People.** No individual is named; roles only. Court decisions about individuals are cited by court and docket number, not by the names in their captions.
- Peer-reviewed sources were checked against Crossref for title, authors and year. Where no abstract could be opened, a source is cited only for its stated subject.

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts first)

**1.1 The SEC's case (alleged June 2002; resolved by judgment).** The SEC charged WorldCom with overstating income before taxes and minority interests by about $3.055 billion in 2001 and $797 million in the first quarter of 2002 ⚑, by capitalising about $3.8 billion of costs that GAAP required it to expense, in order to keep earnings in line with analysts' estimates [1]. The company settled; see 1.3.

**1.2 The board's own investigation (March 2003).** A special investigative committee of the board found that "more than $9 billion in false or unsupported accounting entries" were made to reach desired reported results; that the company improperly reduced reported line costs by over $7 billion from the second quarter of 1999 to the first quarter of 2002 (about $3.3 billion in 1999–2000 through releases of accruals, then $3.8 billion from 2001 mainly by capitalisation); and that its advisers had identified a further $1.107 billion of questionable revenue items [2]. The company also wrote off about $80 billion of the stated book value of its assets [2]. *Status: the company's own investigation.* The monitor later reported that the best current estimates put the overstatement of income at "at least $11 billion" over several years [3].

**1.3 Record civil penalty, mostly satisfied in bankruptcy (final).** On 7 July 2003 the court approved a final judgment making WorldCom liable for a civil penalty of $2.25 billion ⚑, to be deemed satisfied on confirmation of a reorganisation plan by payment of $500 million in cash and $250 million in stock of the reorganised company ⚑, distributed to fraud victims under the Fair Funds provision of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act [4][5][6]. The court called the penalty "75 times greater than any prior such penalty" [4]. *Reporting note:* the $2.25 billion headline is a liability figure; the amount actually paid was $750 million, and part of it was in the company's own new stock. Because the money went to victims, it partly returned value to investors rather than punishing anyone separately from them.

**1.4 Conviction of the former chief executive (final).** A jury convicted the former chief executive on all counts in March 2005 [7][8]. He was sentenced to 25 years' imprisonment, and the Second Circuit affirmed in 2006, describing a scheme that disguised declining operating performance mainly by treating what had been recorded as operating costs as capital expenditures [8].

**1.5 The former chief financial officer.** The SEC brought proceedings against the former chief financial officer [9], who testified at the chief executive's trial; the Second Circuit records that the subordinate co-defendants it compared, including the finance chief, "cooperated and pled guilty", which it gave as a reason for their lighter sentences [8]. *Note:* the primary record of the plea itself was not located at a working address (Unverified or uncertain items).

**1.6 Court-ordered governance overhaul.** Under the SEC's permanent injunction, the court appointed a corporate monitor whose report, *Restoring Trust*, set out 78 recommendations that the company was required to implement unless the court allowed otherwise [3].

## How the fraud worked

- **Line costs moved to capital accounts.** Line costs are fees paid to other carriers to carry calls and data; they are operating expenses. Moving them to capital accounts removed them from the income statement and spread them over future years [1][2].
- **Releasing reserves.** Before capitalisation began, accruals were released to reduce reported line costs [2].
- **"Mundane" entries.** The committee stressed that the fraud was done through ordinary journal entries, not complex instruments [2]. A peer-reviewed study re-examines the fraud to design continuous-assurance monitoring that could have flagged the key transactions early, while noting the complexity created by many legacy systems [10].
- **Governance failure.** The monitor found that the board approved loans and guarantees to the chief executive that grew to more than $400 million, and that no director objected to the $408 million in loans, a $238 million retention plan or large option grants [3]. It also stated that the fraudulent accounting appeared to have involved fewer than 100 people [3].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

- **Employees.** The monitor reported that tens of thousands of employees lost their jobs when the company went into bankruptcy, and that virtually all employees lost the full value of company stock in retirement accounts and of equity compensation [3].
- **Investors.** Victims received the $750 million paid under the SEC judgment ⚑ [5]. The class-action settlements, reported in court opinions as more than $6 billion in total, are **UNVERIFIED** here (Unverified or uncertain items) [11].
- **Competitors and the industry.** Peer-reviewed studies examine contagion from the bankruptcy to other firms [12] and the product-market consequences of the fraud for U.S. telecommunications competitors [13]. Their abstracts could not be opened, so their numerical findings are not reported here.
- **Scale.** Income overstated by at least $11 billion ⚑ and asset write-offs of about $80 billion ⚑ [2][3]. These are company and monitor estimates, not adjudicated amounts.

## Fair counterarguments

**4.1 Was the penalty real?** The court defended the settlement as fair given bankruptcy realities [4]. Critics argue that penalising a bankrupt company mostly moves value between groups of investors and creditors. The $2.25 billion headline versus $750 million paid shows why agency penalty headlines need breaking down ⚑ [5].

**4.2 Sentence severity.** The former chief executive argued on appeal that his sentence was unreasonable, based on an inaccurate loss calculation and far heavier than his co-conspirators'; the Second Circuit rejected those arguments [8]. Debate over very long sentences for fraud continues; this case takes no position on the sentence length.

**4.3 Auditor and board responsibility.** The monitor placed heavy weight on board passivity [3]. The committee report discusses the external auditor (named more than 400 times) and notes that earlier reporting by staff to internal audit, the audit committee, the auditor or regulators might have shortened the fraud [2]. No auditor conviction is part of the adjudicated record located for this case.

**4.4 How much did SOX change?** Evidence is mixed: reporting quality appears to have risen after SOX, but causal attribution is weak and net welfare is unresolved [14]; internal-control audits initially improved control quality, then the gain deteriorated after the 2007 standards change [15].

**4.5 Do markets punish more than courts?** Across SEC misreporting cases, reputational losses average more than 7.5 times legal penalties [16]. WorldCom did not survive as an independent firm, so reputational penalty and bankruptcy loss cannot be separated here.

**How the conclusion handles these points.** The case reports the $2.25 billion penalty as a liability figure and the $750 million as the amount paid, notes that most of it went back to investors, and keeps the company-level judgment separate from the criminal verdicts against individuals.

## Values (labelled section: these are judgements, not findings)

- **Small entries, large lies.** Fraud does not need sophistication; it needs people willing to make entries they know are false, and superiors who ask them to.
- **Boards owe care, not deference.** Personal loyalty to a founder-chief executive is not a governance qualification.
- **Victims first.** Directing penalties to victims (Fair Funds) is a sound principle; it should be reported honestly as compensation, not as punishment.
- **Proportion.** Punishment should reflect culpability and harm; it should not be calibrated to public anger.

## Proposed solutions (each sourced and rated for evidence)

Ratings: **strong** means consistent causal evidence from more than one careful study; **moderate** means one careful study or consistent observational evidence; **weak** means feasibility or model-only support; **UNTESTED** means no evaluation of effect was located.

**6.1 Board independence and limits on insider loans.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED.** The monitor's 78 recommendations include independence standards, a non-executive chair and compensation limits [3]; SOX § 402 prohibits most personal loans to executives of listed companies [6]. *Evidence of effect:* the monitor's recommendations were not evaluated in peer-reviewed work located for this case.

**6.2 Independent audit oversight (PCAOB).** **Evidence rating: moderate.** [6] *Evidence:* higher PCAOB inspection deficiency rates led auditors to issue more adverse internal-control opinions, including to clients with real misstatements [17].

**6.3 Internal-control reporting and audit (SOX § 404).** **Evidence rating: moderate (improvement that later weakened).** [6] *Evidence:* initial improvement in internal-control quality, which weakened after the 2007 auditing-standard change [15].

**6.4 Continuous transaction-level assurance.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED (feasibility study only).** *Evidence:* a design study shows how continuous monitoring of key transactions could have detected the line-cost entries, but it is a feasibility analysis, not an outcome evaluation [10].

**6.5 Whistleblower rewards and protection.** **Evidence rating: moderate.** The Dodd-Frank reward programme reduced the likelihood of accounting fraud by 12–22% in exposed firms relative to controls [18].

**6.6 Fair Funds for victims.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED.** SOX § 308 lets civil penalties go to harmed investors [6]; WorldCom's penalty was directed to victims under this provision [4]. *Evidence of effect* on investor recovery or deterrence was not located.

## What this does not replace

This case does not replace the court-appointed monitor's report, accounting standards on capitalising costs, or legal advice for investors. It does not assess any individual's guilt beyond the verdicts and pleas reported.
