# Hytera and Motorola's digital radio code: a civil judgment and a criminal plea for the same theft

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study. Research and writing only; this is not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Found**: what a court, jury, regulator or plea record decided or admitted. **Alleged**: what a prosecutor charged or a party claimed; an indictment or complaint is not a finding. **Reported**: facts known only from news coverage, labelled SECONDARY. Figures are quoted as the source gives them. Government figures (prosecution estimates, agency loss figures) are flagged, because agencies and prosecutors have reasons to present large numbers when announcing results. ## The ethical question

A company behind in a new market recruits engineers from the leader, the engineers bring source code and technical documents, and the company launches a competing product years earlier than it could have on its own. When the courts finally act, more than a decade later, does the penalty take away what the theft gained?

## Analysis

Hytera is the strongest record in this category for the claim that theft was used to **enter a market early**. In the Seventh Circuit's account, Hytera was struggling to develop its own DMR radios, decided to take Motorola's trade secrets and copyrighted code, and aimed to "leapfrog Motorola" to become the leading DMR provider; it hired three engineers from Motorola in Malaysia with offers of high-paying jobs [2]. The prosecution described Hytera as "behind in the market" and said it launched a competing DMR product in June 2010, years before it could have without the stolen material [4]. That is a party's description, but the core facts were admitted in the plea [1], and the civil courts found liability [2].

Unlike several other cases in this category, the money here was large relative to the firm. The civil award exceeded the profits the court found, and Hytera told the criminal court that the obligations were about equal to a year's revenue [5][4]. Even so, the remedy came about a decade after market entry. A firm that gains a decade of customers, dealers and installed base keeps some of that advantage even after paying. The enforcement literature predicts this: long delay reduces the weight of a sanction in the offender's calculation [6][7].

## Fair counterarguments

- **Legitimate protection versus litigation as a weapon.** Suing a rival over intellectual property is often legitimate protection of research. But litigation can also be used to raise a competitor's costs; research on suits by patent holders that do not make products found that targeted firms substantially reduce their innovative activity after settling or losing [8]. A finding in court shows that a line was crossed; it does not show that every claim the winning side made was right.
- **Learning is not taking.** The international standard protects undisclosed information only against acquisition "contrary to honest commercial practices" [9], which is narrower than all copying. Learning from public products and hiring experienced people are lawful and are how skills spread. This case concerns confidential material that a court, regulator or plea record found was taken or misused, not learning from public products or hiring experienced people.
- **The costs of litigation.** Long cases consume money and management attention on both sides, and delay can hurt the winner as much as the loser. Remedies that depend on ever-longer litigation are a weak answer to a problem of speed.
- **The penalty may now be too heavy for workers and customers.** If the combined obligations threaten the firm's survival, as Hytera argued [5], employees and radio users who had no part in the theft bear part of the cost. That is an argument for product-specific remedies and for speed, not for leniency on the finding.
- **Overlap of civil and criminal penalties.** Paying restitution, fines and civil damages for the same conduct raises fairness questions; the reported offset of restitution against civil payments is one answer [3].

## Proposed solutions

The proposals below are this case's own reasoning, built on the cited evidence. Each carries an evidence rating for how well research supports the measure itself: **strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **UNTESTED** (no study cited here tests whether the measure works).

1. **Make the expected penalty larger than the gain, mainly by raising the chance of being caught.** In the standard economic model, a firm deterred only by money compares the gain with the probability of a sanction times its size [6][7]. Reviews of the evidence find that certainty of apprehension deters more consistently than severity [10], and that single punitive sanctions against companies do not deter consistently while combinations of measures (regulation, inspection, sanctions together) do [11]. Detection funding, forensic review of departing-employee transfers in litigation, and protected reporting channels therefore matter more than headline fines. *Evidence rating: moderate. The reviews cited are broad; none tests trade-secret or infringement enforcement specifically.*
2. **Measure the remedy by the gain, not only the victim's provable loss.** US civil law already allows recovery of unjust enrichment and up to double exemplary damages for wilful and malicious trade-secret theft [12], and criminal fines for organisations can reach three times the value of the stolen secret, including avoided research and design costs [13]. Courts and prosecutors should use the gain-based measures the law already allows, and report the gain they found, so that the public can compare penalty with benefit. *Evidence rating: weak. Supported by enforcement theory [6][7]; UNTESTED as a measured change in behaviour.*
3. **Take away the product advantage, not the whole firm.** Where products are made with misappropriated technology, a product-specific exclusion, verified by an independent technical expert and lasting at least as long as the head start gained, removes the market benefit while sparing customers and workers who did nothing wrong. A ten-year US exclusion order for products made with misappropriated trade secrets is an existing precedent [14][15]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. A legal precedent exists; no study cited here measures its deterrent effect.*
4. **Protect the people who report it.** US law gives immunity to a person who discloses a trade secret in confidence to officials or to a lawyer in order to report a suspected violation [16]. Similar protection, and a clear duty to report in engineering codes, should be standard. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. The legal protection exists; no study cited here measures its effect on detection.*
5. **Do not over-correct into employer-friendly secrecy.** Stronger trade-secret law was associated with more R&D in some industries [17], but stronger employer-friendly secrecy rules were associated with less innovation by individual inventors [18], and intellectual-property rights that are too broad can reduce follow-on research [19]. Remedies should target taking and using confidential material, not ordinary job mobility or learning. *Evidence rating: moderate. Three peer-reviewed empirical studies, with mixed directions by industry and by inventor.*
6. **Head-start remedies.** Where the record shows how many years of development were skipped (here, avoided R&D was found), exclusion of the specific products should run at least that long, with independent verification that later products are clean. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. This is a design proposal; no study cited here tests it.*

## What this does not replace

- **Legal advice or a legal opinion.** Nothing here says how any pending or future dispute should be decided. Anyone involved in a trade-secret, patent, copyright or related matter needs a qualified lawyer in the relevant country.
- **The court record itself.** Summaries here are shortened. Where a figure or finding matters, read the cited judgment, plea or notice.
- **A company's current conduct or compliance standing.** A finding describes past conduct at a past date. It is not evidence about the company's present practices, products or staff, and must not be used to judge individual employees.
- **Due diligence or a sanctions screen.** This case is not a supplier-risk score, a trade-control list or an investment recommendation.
