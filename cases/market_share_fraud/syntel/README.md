# Syntel and TriZetto's Facets software: misappropriation affirmed, a $285 million gain-based award thrown out

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study. Research and writing only; this is not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Found**: what a court, jury, regulator or plea record decided or admitted. **Alleged**: what a prosecutor charged or a party claimed; an indictment or complaint is not a finding. **Reported**: facts known only from news coverage, labelled SECONDARY. Figures are quoted as the source gives them. Government figures (prosecution estimates, agency loss figures) are flagged, because agencies and prosecutors have reasons to present large numbers when announcing results. Individuals are not named except where a public office or a court record makes the name part of the public finding; private individuals are not named.*

## The ethical question

A subcontractor that was trusted with a software maker's secrets, in return for a guaranteed payment and an agreement not to compete, later competed using those secrets. A jury awarded the avoided development costs; the appeal court removed that award and left the victim with lost profits of $8.5 million. When the law measures by the victim's loss rather than the taker's gain, who keeps the difference?

## Damages against market effect

- **Gain claimed.** TriZetto's expert testified that Syntel avoided roughly $285 million in research and development costs, a part of TriZetto's more than $500 million development spend on Facets [1].
- **Loss proved.** $8.5 million in lost profits on one software upgrade [1].
- **Market effect.** No record opened measures Syntel's market gain. This case makes no estimate.

## Analysis

Syntel is the opposite of the Epic case. There, the measured benefit was awarded; here, the gain-based measure was removed on appeal [1]. The legal reasoning has force: an injunction had already stopped the use, and the law does not always allow a windfall to the victim. But the result shows the gap this category is about. A firm that took secrets it was trusted with, and was found liable, faces a damages exposure tied to what the victim can prove it lost, which can be a small fraction of what the firm saved.

In deterrence terms, the expected cost of taking falls when remedies track provable loss rather than gain [2][3]. The injunction is the stronger remedy here: it took the product advantage away [1].

## Fair counterarguments

- **Legitimate protection versus litigation as a weapon.** Suing a rival over intellectual property is often legitimate protection of research. But litigation can also be used to raise a competitor's costs; research on suits by patent holders that do not make products found that targeted firms substantially reduce their innovative activity after settling or losing [4]. A finding in court shows that a line was crossed; it does not show that every claim the winning side made was right.
- **Learning is not taking.** The international standard protects undisclosed information only against acquisition "contrary to honest commercial practices" [5], which is narrower than all copying. Learning from public products and hiring experienced people are lawful and are how skills spread. This case concerns confidential material that a court, regulator or plea record found was taken or misused, not learning from public products or hiring experienced people.
- **The costs of litigation.** Long cases consume money and management attention on both sides, and delay can hurt the winner as much as the loser. Remedies that depend on ever-longer litigation are a weak answer to a problem of speed.
- **Avoided-cost awards can over-compensate.** Awarding a victim the full development cost of a product it still owns and still sells can be a windfall; the appeal court's concern is legitimate [1]. This case's proposal is to use gain-based measures where the gain is still being enjoyed, and injunctions where it is not.

## Proposed solutions

The proposals below are this case's own reasoning, built on the cited evidence. Each carries an evidence rating for how well research supports the measure itself: **strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **UNTESTED** (no study cited here tests whether the measure works).

1. **Make the expected penalty larger than the gain, mainly by raising the chance of being caught.** In the standard economic model, a firm deterred only by money compares the gain with the probability of a sanction times its size [2][3]. Reviews of the evidence find that certainty of apprehension deters more consistently than severity [6], and that single punitive sanctions against companies do not deter consistently while combinations of measures (regulation, inspection, sanctions together) do [7]. Detection funding, forensic review of departing-employee transfers in litigation, and protected reporting channels therefore matter more than headline fines. *Evidence rating: moderate. The reviews cited are broad; none tests trade-secret or infringement enforcement specifically.*
2. **Measure the remedy by the gain, not only the victim's provable loss.** US civil law already allows recovery of unjust enrichment and up to double exemplary damages for wilful and malicious trade-secret theft [8], and criminal fines for organisations can reach three times the value of the stolen secret, including avoided research and design costs [9]. Courts and prosecutors should use the gain-based measures the law already allows, and report the gain they found, so that the public can compare penalty with benefit. *Evidence rating: weak. Supported by enforcement theory [2][3]; UNTESTED as a measured change in behaviour.*
3. **Take away the product advantage, not the whole firm.** Where products are made with misappropriated technology, a product-specific exclusion, verified by an independent technical expert and lasting at least as long as the head start gained, removes the market benefit while sparing customers and workers who did nothing wrong. A ten-year US exclusion order for products made with misappropriated trade secrets is an existing precedent [10][11]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. A legal precedent exists; no study cited here measures its deterrent effect.*
4. **Protect the people who report it.** US law gives immunity to a person who discloses a trade secret in confidence to officials or to a lawyer in order to report a suspected violation [12]. Similar protection, and a clear duty to report in engineering codes, should be standard. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. The legal protection exists; no study cited here measures its effect on detection.*
5. **Do not over-correct into employer-friendly secrecy.** Stronger trade-secret law was associated with more R&D in some industries [13], but stronger employer-friendly secrecy rules were associated with less innovation by individual inventors [14], and intellectual-property rights that are too broad can reduce follow-on research [15]. Remedies should target taking and using confidential material, not ordinary job mobility or learning. *Evidence rating: moderate. Three peer-reviewed empirical studies, with mixed directions by industry and by inventor.*

## What this does not replace

- **Legal advice or a legal opinion.** Nothing here says how any pending or future dispute should be decided. Anyone involved in a trade-secret, patent, copyright or related matter needs a qualified lawyer in the relevant country.
- **The court record itself.** Summaries here are shortened. Where a figure or finding matters, read the cited judgment, plea or notice.
- **A company's current conduct or compliance standing.** A finding describes past conduct at a past date. It is not evidence about the company's present practices, products or staff, and must not be used to judge individual employees.
- **Due diligence or a sanctions screen.** This case is not a supplier-risk score, a trade-control list or an investment recommendation.
