# United Microelectronics (UMC) and Micron's memory-chip secrets: a guilty plea, and an acquittal for the state-owned partner

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study. Research and writing only; this is not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Found**: what a court, jury, regulator or plea record decided or admitted. **Alleged**: what a prosecutor charged or a party claimed; an indictment or complaint is not a finding. **Reported**: facts known only from news coverage, labelled SECONDARY. Figures are quoted as the source gives them. Government figures (prosecution estimates, agency loss figures) are flagged, because agencies and prosecutors have reasons to present large numbers when announcing results. Individuals are not named except where a public office or a court record makes the name part of the public finding; private individuals are not named.*

## The ethical question

A contract manufacturer with no memory-chip business agreed to develop memory technology for a new state-owned producer. Engineers hired from the market leader brought that leader's confidential files with them. What does it mean for fairness when one company in the chain admits theft, and the company that was to use the technology is acquitted?

## Findings at a glance

| Step | Status | What the record says | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| September 2018 | Alleged | A federal grand jury indicted UMC, Fujian Jinhua Integrated Circuit Co. (a PRC state-owned enterprise) and three individuals for conspiracy to steal Micron's trade secrets for Fujian Jinhua's benefit | [1] |
| 30 October 2018 | Administrative measure | The US Commerce Department added Fujian Jinhua to the Entity List, on a finding that it posed "a significant risk" of acting contrary to US national-security interests; this is an export-control listing, not a finding of theft | [2] |

## What UMC admitted

According to the facts admitted with the plea, as summarised by the Justice Department: UMC hired three people from Micron's Taiwan subsidiary and put one in charge of negotiating a memory-development agreement with Fujian Jinhua; two of them brought Micron confidential information to UMC; after UMC's own IT department found Micron material on one computer, two "off network" laptops were issued that allowed continued access without detection; one Micron file was used to adjust UMC's design rules; and during a search by Taiwan authorities, materials were hidden and one laptop's drive was reformatted [1]. Individuals are not named here.

## Damages against market effect

- **Penalty.** $60 million fine, probation, cooperation [1].
- **Market effect.** No record opened gives a figure for the value of the Micron secrets or for any market gain. The Justice Department's statement that the scheme served a strategic aim of "self-sufficiency in computer memory production without spending its own time or money to earn it" is a prosecutor's characterisation (government statement; reporting incentive), not a measured effect [1]. This case makes no estimate.
- **Statutory ceiling, for scale.** The organisational fine for this offence can reach three times the value of the stolen secret, including avoided research and design costs [4]. No such valuation was opened.

## Analysis

This is the clearest record in this category of the **internal controls failing on purpose**: the company's IT department found the material, and the response recorded in the admitted facts was a workaround, not a stop [1]. That is the pattern the deterrence literature warns about: when management condones the conduct, compliance systems become decoration [5].

The acquittal of Fujian Jinhua matters just as much. The firm that was meant to benefit was listed for export controls in 2018 [2] and acquitted in 2024 [3]. A measure imposed before trial landed on a firm that a court later did not convict. Any remedy proposed for market-share theft must survive that test: penalties that bite before a finding can hit the wrong party.

## Fair counterarguments

- **Legitimate protection versus litigation as a weapon.** Suing a rival over intellectual property is often legitimate protection of research. But litigation can also be used to raise a competitor's costs; research on suits by patent holders that do not make products found that targeted firms substantially reduce their innovative activity after settling or losing [6]. A finding in court shows that a line was crossed; it does not show that every claim the winning side made was right.
- **Learning is not taking.** The international standard protects undisclosed information only against acquisition "contrary to honest commercial practices" [7], which is narrower than all copying. Learning from public products and hiring experienced people are lawful and are how skills spread. This case concerns confidential material that a court, regulator or plea record found was taken or misused, not learning from public products or hiring experienced people.
- **The costs of litigation.** Long cases consume money and management attention on both sides, and delay can hurt the winner as much as the loser. Remedies that depend on ever-longer litigation are a weak answer to a problem of speed.
- **Plea bargains trade findings for cooperation.** UMC's fine was set in exchange for cooperation against a co-defendant [1]. The plea is a real admission, but its size reflects a bargain, not a full measure of harm.
- **National-security framing.** Government releases in this case stress strategic competition with China. That framing has its own incentives and is flagged accordingly.

## Proposed solutions

The proposals below are this case's own reasoning, built on the cited evidence. Each carries an evidence rating for how well research supports the measure itself: **strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **UNTESTED** (no study cited here tests whether the measure works).

1. **Make the expected penalty larger than the gain, mainly by raising the chance of being caught.** In the standard economic model, a firm deterred only by money compares the gain with the probability of a sanction times its size [8][9]. Reviews of the evidence find that certainty of apprehension deters more consistently than severity [10], and that single punitive sanctions against companies do not deter consistently while combinations of measures (regulation, inspection, sanctions together) do [5]. Detection funding, forensic review of departing-employee transfers in litigation, and protected reporting channels therefore matter more than headline fines. *Evidence rating: moderate. The reviews cited are broad; none tests trade-secret or infringement enforcement specifically.*
2. **Measure the remedy by the gain, not only the victim's provable loss.** US civil law already allows recovery of unjust enrichment and up to double exemplary damages for wilful and malicious trade-secret theft [11], and criminal fines for organisations can reach three times the value of the stolen secret, including avoided research and design costs [4]. Courts and prosecutors should use the gain-based measures the law already allows, and report the gain they found, so that the public can compare penalty with benefit. *Evidence rating: weak. Supported by enforcement theory [8][9]; UNTESTED as a measured change in behaviour.*
3. **Take away the product advantage, not the whole firm.** Where products are made with misappropriated technology, a product-specific exclusion, verified by an independent technical expert and lasting at least as long as the head start gained, removes the market benefit while sparing customers and workers who did nothing wrong. A ten-year US exclusion order for products made with misappropriated trade secrets is an existing precedent [12][13]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. A legal precedent exists; no study cited here measures its deterrent effect.*
4. **Protect the people who report it.** US law gives immunity to a person who discloses a trade secret in confidence to officials or to a lawyer in order to report a suspected violation [14]. Similar protection, and a clear duty to report in engineering codes, should be standard. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. The legal protection exists; no study cited here measures its effect on detection.*
5. **Do not over-correct into employer-friendly secrecy.** Stronger trade-secret law was associated with more R&D in some industries [15], but stronger employer-friendly secrecy rules were associated with less innovation by individual inventors [16], and intellectual-property rights that are too broad can reduce follow-on research [17]. Remedies should target taking and using confidential material, not ordinary job mobility or learning. *Evidence rating: moderate. Three peer-reviewed empirical studies, with mixed directions by industry and by inventor.*
6. **No market bans before a finding.** Product or market exclusion should follow an adjudicated or admitted finding, not precede it. Pre-trial measures, where needed for security, should be labelled as such and reviewed if the target is acquitted. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. This is a design proposal; no study cited here tests it.*

## What this does not replace

- **Legal advice or a legal opinion.** Nothing here says how any pending or future dispute should be decided. Anyone involved in a trade-secret, patent, copyright or related matter needs a qualified lawyer in the relevant country.
- **The court record itself.** Summaries here are shortened. Where a figure or finding matters, read the cited judgment, plea or notice.
- **A company's current conduct or compliance standing.** A finding describes past conduct at a past date. It is not evidence about the company's present practices, products or staff, and must not be used to judge individual employees.
- **Due diligence or a sanctions screen.** This case is not a supplier-risk score, a trade-control list or an investment recommendation.
