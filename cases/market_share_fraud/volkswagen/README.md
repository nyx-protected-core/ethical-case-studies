# Volkswagen Diesel Emissions Fraud ("Dieselgate"): Adjudicated Findings, Harms, Disputes and Reforms

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Volkswagen AG is included because it pleaded guilty in the United States to conspiracy, obstruction of justice and entry of goods by false statements over diesel defeat devices, and accepted a €1 billion administrative fine order in Germany; a separate EU cartel decision on emissions-cleaning technology is reported as a different matter. **Found** means decided by a court or regulator or admitted by the company; **Alleged** means charged or claimed. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies tend to announce the largest defensible total.*

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers the Volkswagen group's use of software "defeat devices" in diesel passenger cars (roughly model years 2009–2015), the criminal, civil and regulatory outcomes, and a related but separate EU cartel decision on emissions-cleaning technology. It works at the level of institutions, records and evidence.

- **Findings versus claims.** The Findings section lists only adjudicated or admitted findings: convictions, guilty pleas, administrative fine orders that the company accepted, final civil judgments, settled regulator decisions. Each is tied to its primary record. Anything that is an allegation, a first-instance ruling still under appeal, or an agency's description of its own result is labelled as such.
- **Source tags.** **[CT]** court record or prosecutor's case page; **[REG]** regulator or agency record; **[CO]** company disclosure (used only as an admission against interest); **[LAW]** statute or regulation; **[PR]** peer-reviewed research; **[SECONDARY — news, timeline only]** news report used only to date an event.
- **Government figures are marked ⚑.** Penalty totals, "up to" settlement values and loss estimates published by agencies are reporting choices as well as facts. Headline totals often add together different legal instruments (criminal fines, civil penalties, consumer compensation, disgorgement), and agencies have an incentive to present the largest defensible total. Each ⚑ figure is broken down where the record allows.
- **People.** No individual is named. People appear by role only. Cases against companies keep their published captions; cases against individuals are cited by court and case number only.
- **Peer-reviewed sources** were checked against Crossref for title, authors and year. Claims taken from them are limited to what the abstract or summary states unless noted.

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts first)

**1.1 United States: corporate guilty plea and sentence (criminal, final).** On 11 January 2017 Volkswagen AG was charged by criminal information in the Eastern District of Michigan with conspiracy to defraud the United States, to commit wire fraud and to violate the Clean Air Act; obstruction of justice; and entry of goods by false statements [1]. The charges concerned the sale of approximately 590,000 diesel vehicles in the United States using a defeat device [1]. The company pleaded guilty on 10 March 2017, and on 21 April 2017 the court accepted the plea agreement and imposed a criminal penalty of $2.8 billion ⚑, three years of probation, full cooperation and an independent compliance monitor for at least three years [1][2]. *Status: final conviction of the company.*

**1.2 United States: the agency headline total.** The Justice Department announced the plea agreement together with civil resolutions as $4.3 billion in criminal and civil penalties ⚑ [3] (the release could not be opened in this check, so this wording is UNVERIFIED). *Reporting note:* this headline adds the $2.8 billion criminal penalty [1] to separate civil resolutions of roughly $1.5 billion. The exact civil breakdown could not be confirmed because the primary page could not be opened (Unverified or uncertain items).

**1.3 United States: civil consent settlements (2.0-litre vehicles).** In June 2016 the Justice Department announced that the company would spend up to $14.7 billion ⚑ under consent decrees and related settlements for nearly 500,000 2.0-litre diesel vehicles: up to about $10.03 billion for buybacks, lease terminations or approved emissions modifications, $2.7 billion for a NOx-mitigation trust, and $2 billion for zero-emission-vehicle investment [4]. *Reporting note:* the consumer element is an "up to" ceiling that depends on take-up, and part of it is a purchase price paid for returned vehicles that the company receives back. It is not a penalty and should not be added to fines. The release could not be opened in this check, so the component amounts are UNVERIFIED against it.

**1.4 United States: the starting allegation.** The Environmental Protection Agency's Notice of Violation of 18 September 2015 alleged that software in 2.0-litre diesel cars detected official testing and reduced emission controls in normal driving [5]. A notice of violation is an allegation. It is listed here because the company later admitted the underlying conduct in its plea [2].

**1.5 United States: securities case (resolved without admission).** The Securities and Exchange Commission sued in March 2019, alleging false statements in more than $8 billion of U.S. bond offerings in 2014–2015. On 3 April 2024 the U.S. finance subsidiary consented, *without admitting or denying* the allegations, to a final judgment ordering $34.35 million in disgorgement and $14.4 million in prejudgment interest ⚑; the SEC then dismissed its remaining claims against the parent company and its former chief executive [6]. *Status: final judgment by consent; no adjudicated finding of fraud by the parent.*

**1.6 Germany: administrative fine order accepted by the company (final).** On 13 June 2018 the Braunschweig public prosecutor issued a fine order of €1 billion ⚑ against Volkswagen AG for negligent breach of supervisory duties (§§ 30, 130 of the Administrative Offences Act) in connection with the EA 189 and EA 288 (Gen3) diesel engines [7]. The prosecutor states that the company admitted organisational failings [7], and the company announced that it accepted the order and would not appeal [8]. *Reporting note:* only €5 million is the punitive element (the statutory maximum for negligent breach); the other €995 million is disgorgement of economic benefit (saved costs) [7]. The prosecutor also says it set the amount so as not to endanger buyers' civil claims [7].

**1.7 Germany: civil liability to buyers (final).** On 25 May 2020 the Federal Court of Justice held (VI ZR 252/19) that a buyer of a car fitted with the prohibited EA 189 shut-off device has a damages claim against the manufacturer: refund of the purchase price, less a deduction for use, against return of the car [9].

**1.8 Germany: first-instance criminal convictions of former employees (not final).** On 26 May 2025 the Braunschweig Regional Court (6 KLs 411 Js 49032/15) gave a first-instance verdict in the fraud trial of four current or former employees [10]. The date and the fact of convictions are known only from a secondary report [11, SECONDARY] and are UNVERIFIED against a court record; sentences and the court's reasoning are not relied on. *Status: first instance; reported as under appeal, so this is not a final finding.*

**1.9 European Union: technical-development cartel (final, settled).** On 8 July 2021 the European Commission found that Daimler, BMW and the Volkswagen group (Volkswagen, Audi, Porsche) had breached Article 101 TFEU by colluding, from 25 June 2009 to 1 October 2014, on AdBlue tank sizes, refill ranges and assumed consumption for selective catalytic reduction systems [12][13]. The Commission described this as a restriction of technical development, its first cartel decision based on that ground alone rather than on price-fixing [12]. Fines: Volkswagen group €502,362,000 ⚑ and BMW €372,827,000 ⚑; Daimler received immunity as the leniency applicant [12]. All parties acknowledged involvement and settled [12]. *Scope note:* this decision is separate from the defeat-device fraud. It concerns collusion on how far to clean exhaust beyond legal minimums, not on cheating tests.

## How the fraud worked

- **Mechanism (admitted in the U.S. plea; described in German rulings).** Engine-control software recognised the standard laboratory test cycle and ran a low-NOx calibration only in that mode; in normal road driving it switched to a calibration with less exhaust-gas recirculation and higher NOx output [2][9].
- **Technical forensics.** A peer-reviewed firmware analysis described two families of diesel defeat-device software, including the one used by the Volkswagen group in the United States and Europe, and developed static-analysis methods that flagged a potential defeat device in more than 400 of about 900 firmware images spanning eight years [14]. The authors argue that standardised, black-box laboratory testing created the opportunity for evasion [14].
- **Why it paid.** Peer-reviewed ethics analysis notes that the U.S. models were marketed as "clean diesel" that met strict NOx limits with a cheaper lean-NOx-trap design instead of urea-based selective catalytic reduction, and that later models with urea systems kept the software so that owners needed fewer refills [15]. The German prosecutor valued the costs saved by the company at the core of its €995 million disgorgement [7].
- **How it was detected.** The violation came to light through on-road testing outside the official test cycle, followed by the EPA notice [5][15].
- **Organisational failure.** The German fine order rests on a finding of breach of supervisory duty in the engine-development department [7].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

**3.1 Health (modelled estimates, peer-reviewed).** These are model-based estimates, not counts of deaths.
- United States: excess emissions over 2008–2015 estimated to cause 59 early deaths (95% CI 10–150), with social cost of about $450 million; returning the fleet to compliance by end-2016 was estimated to avert about 130 further early deaths [16].
- United States, separate model: excess damages of about $430 million (range $350–500 million) and 46 expected deaths (range 40–52) for 2009–2015 vehicles [17].
- Germany-registered vehicles: about 1,200 premature deaths across Europe and €1.9 billion in life-years-lost costs over 2008–2015 (median), about 60% of it borne outside Germany [18]. The same study reports mean on-road NOx of 0.85 g/km for affected cars against a 0.18 g/km limit [18].

**3.2 Investors and the wider market (peer-reviewed).** An event study estimates that the company lost €27.4 billion in equity market value over the first five event days, almost all of it abnormal loss; competitors and suppliers lost a further €18.3 billion and €12.6 billion respectively in abnormal value, and bond markets added losses on top [19].

**3.3 Buyers.** The German Federal Court of Justice treated the purchase itself as a loss that the buyer can unwind [9]. U.S. buyers were compensated under the civil settlements, which is why the criminal court did not order restitution: the prosecutors cited the very large number of victims and the existing civil remedy [1].

**3.4 Competition.** The EU decision found that the cartel limited competition on cleaning performance beyond legal requirements and on AdBlue refill ranges, which limited customer choice [12][13]. The decision does not quantify an emissions or price effect.

## Fair counterarguments

**4.1 Are the penalties large or small?** Agency totals mix instruments (Findings 1.2, 1.3, 1.6). Put together they look very large; split apart, the punitive part is smaller than the headline. In Germany only €5 million of €1 billion was punitive [7]. Against this, a peer-reviewed study of SEC financial-misrepresentation cases found that market-imposed reputational losses averaged more than 7.5 times all legal penalties [20], and the Volkswagen event study found equity losses far above its fines in the first week alone [19]. Whether legal penalties deter, or whether markets do most of the work, remains contested.

**4.2 Did the company recover too easily?** A peer-reviewed review notes that the share price fell about 40% in two weeks but that sales rebounded and the group became the world's largest carmaker by 2017, while also arguing that recovery was incomplete [21]. Readers should not take sales recovery as proof that sanctions failed or worked; no causal test is offered.

**4.3 Individual versus corporate responsibility.** The company is a convicted defendant in the United States [2]. In Germany, corporate liability is administrative, not criminal, which is why the €1 billion came as a fine order [7]. The first-instance Braunschweig verdict against individuals is reported as under appeal [11, SECONDARY]. The SEC's claims against the parent and its former chief executive were dismissed after a subsidiary consented without admission [6]. Where liability for senior leadership has not been adjudicated, it must be treated as unproven.

**4.4 Regulator failure versus company fraud.** The firmware study argues that predictable, standardised lab tests invited evasion [14]. This is a design weakness in the testing regime; it does not reduce the company's adjudicated responsibility.

**4.5 Contested health numbers.** The three health studies use different emission assumptions, concentration-response functions and valuation methods, and they give wide intervals (for example, 10–150 early deaths in the U.S. estimate) [16][17][18]. They should be cited as ranges.

**4.6 The cartel decision is not the fraud.** Treating the 2021 EU decision as proof of the defeat-device scheme would be an error. The Commission's finding concerns coordination on AdBlue technology, and actual tank sizes stayed above the volumes discussed, according to the decision summary [13].

**How the conclusion handles these points.** The case separates the criminal plea and German fine order (findings against the company) from consumer buy-back ceilings (not penalties), from first-instance verdicts against former employees (not final), and from the 2021 cartel decision (a different infringement).

## Values (labelled section: these are judgements, not findings)

- **Honesty toward regulators as a public duty.** Type approval is a trust system: the state relies on manufacturers' test behaviour. A car that behaves differently when tested breaks that trust even if no single sale looks harmful.
- **Diffuse harm.** The main harm is spread thinly across many people breathing polluted air, which makes it easy to under-weight. Modelled deaths are uncertain, but uncertainty is not zero.
- **Proportion and fairness.** Disgorging saved costs, compensating buyers and funding mitigation repairs harm; punitive fines signal blame. Both have a place, and conflating them hides which one was actually used.
- **Responsibility inside organisations.** Pressure from above and silence below are both failures. Fair process still requires that individual guilt be proved, not assumed from rank.

## Proposed solutions (each sourced and rated for evidence)

Ratings: **strong** means consistent causal evidence from more than one careful study; **moderate** means one careful study or consistent observational evidence; **weak** means feasibility or single-case support; **UNTESTED** means no evaluation of effect was located.

**6.1 Test what cars do on the road, not only in the lab.** **Evidence rating: moderate for the post-scandal fixes; UNTESTED for the on-road test rule itself.** The EU added a Real Driving Emissions test to type approval in 2016 [22]. *Evidence:* a peer-reviewed UK remote-sensing study of 23,000 on-road measurements found that the post-scandal hardware and software fixes cut NOx from affected Volkswagen group 1.6-litre cars by 36% and from 2.0-litre cars by 30% [23]. That study measures the effect of the fixes, not the RDE rule itself; peer-reviewed evidence isolating the RDE rule's effect was not located (Unverified or uncertain items).

**6.2 Independent market surveillance and EU-level oversight of type approval.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED.** Regulation (EU) 2018/858 gives the Commission and member states powers to test vehicles already on the market and to impose penalties for non-compliance [24]. *Evidence of effect:* not yet established in the peer-reviewed literature located for this case.

**6.3 Software audits for regulated products.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED (feasibility shown; no programme evaluation).** Static firmware analysis can detect known defeat-device patterns at scale [14]. *Evidence:* the method found candidate defeat devices across hundreds of firmware images, which shows feasibility; it has not been evaluated as a regulatory programme.

**6.4 Protect and reward insiders who report.** **Evidence rating: moderate (U.S. accounting-fraud evidence; transfer untested).** *Evidence:* in U.S. listed firms, exposure to the Dodd-Frank whistleblower reward programme was followed by a 12–22% lower likelihood of accounting fraud relative to controls [25]; transfer to emissions compliance and to automotive engineering cultures is untested.

**6.5 Leniency for cartels.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED (single case; deterrence not measured).** The EU leniency programme produced the AdBlue cartel case: the immunity applicant revealed the cartel and avoided about €727 million in fines ⚑ [12] (the Commission's notional figure for a fine that was never imposed). *Evidence of effect:* this single case shows the mechanism working; it does not measure deterrence.

## What this does not replace

This case does not replace vehicle emissions testing, type-approval or recall information for any car, legal advice on owners' claims, or the court records of individual prosecutions. Public-health estimates cited here are model estimates, not counts of harm to particular people.
