# Wirecard AG: Missing Cash, Void Accounts and Supervisory Failure — What Has and Has Not Been Adjudicated

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Wirecard is included on a narrow basis: a final civil judgment against the company itself declared its 2017 and 2018 accounts void for overstated assets, and the financial supervisor fined the company for late financial reporting. No court has yet ruled that fraud was committed at the company, and the criminal trial of former managers is listed as an allegation. **Found** means decided by a court or regulator or admitted by the company; **Alleged** means charged or claimed; **Reported** means known only from news, labelled SECONDARY. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies and claimants have reasons to present large numbers. Individuals are referred to by role.*

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers the collapse of Wirecard AG, a German payments company and member of the DAX index, which reported €1.9 billion of cash in trust accounts that its own board then said probably did not exist. It covers the company's admission, the civil, insolvency, auditor-oversight and foreign criminal outcomes, and the record on supervisory failure.

- **Status warning.** As of 19 November 2026, **no criminal court has ruled on whether fraud was committed at Wirecard AG itself.** The Munich criminal trial of the former chief executive and two co-defendants began on 8 December 2022 and had not reached a verdict as of November 2026 [1, SECONDARY]. Everything charged in that case is an **allegation**, and the defendants are presumed innocent.
- **Basis for inclusion.** The company-level findings are the final void-accounts judgment (1.3) and the supervisor's final fine (1.10). The company's 2020 statement (1.1) is an admission, not a ruling.
- **What counts as a finding here.** The company's own ad hoc admission; final or announced civil judgments (each labelled final or not final); the auditor-oversight sanction; foreign criminal convictions of persons connected to the group's Asian business; and official supervisory reviews.
- **Source tags.** **[CT]** court record; **[REG]** regulator; **[CO]** company disclosure used as an admission; **[GOV]** parliamentary report; **[LAW]** statute; **[PR]** peer-reviewed research; scholarly books and chapters whose peer-review status is not confirmed are tagged as such; **[SECONDARY — news]** news used for timeline only and paired with a primary record where one exists.
- **Government figures are marked ⚑**; see the note on reporting incentives in each case.
- **People.** No individual is named; roles only. A fugitive former board member and other persons appear by role. Court decisions are cited by court and case number.
- Peer-reviewed sources were checked against Crossref for title, authors and year.

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts first)

**1.1 The company's admission (22 June 2020).** The management board stated that "there is a prevailing likelihood that the bank trust account balances in the amount of 1.9 billion EUR do not exist", that previous descriptions of its so-called Third Party Acquiring business were "not correct", and it withdrew its preliminary 2019 and first-quarter 2020 results and its 2020 forecast [2]. *Status: admission by the issuer.* It is not a court finding that any person committed fraud.

**1.2 Insolvency (2020).** The Munich local court opened insolvency proceedings over the company's assets on 25 August 2020 [3].

**1.3 Annual accounts void (civil, final).** On 5 May 2022 the Munich Regional Court I (5 HK O 15710/20) declared the 2017 and 2018 annual accounts and the related profit-distribution resolutions void [4]. The court held that the accounts were void *either way*: if the trust-account balances did not exist, assets were overstated; if they existed in other accounts, as the former chief executive argued, the bookkeeping still breached creditor-protection rules. It found the overstatement material at about 39% and 41% of balance-sheet totals of roughly €1.9 billion and just over €2.3 billion [4]. The court expressly did not have to decide whether the bank confirmations were forged or whether the Third Party Acquiring business existed [4]. The judgment text, published in the Bavarian state court database, confirms the four declarations in its operative part (Tenor) and that the action was brought by the insolvency administrator against the company [25]. *Status: final.* The former chief executive, who had joined the case on the company's side, appealed to the Higher Regional Court of Munich (7 U 3337/22) and withdrew the appeal on 4 August 2023 after the court indicated it intended to dismiss it; the company's statutory notice states that the nullity is therefore final [26]. This is a finding that the accounts were materially wrong; it is not a finding of fraud.

**1.4 Board members liable for negligence (civil, first instance).** On 5 September 2024 the same court (5 HK O 17452/21) ordered three former management board members, jointly and severally, to pay €140 million plus interest to the insolvency administrator for at least negligent breach of duty in granting an unsecured €100 million loan through a subsidiary; it dismissed the claim against a former supervisory board member [5]. *Status: not final when announced. This is a negligence finding, not a fraud finding.*

**1.5 Shareholders rank behind creditors (final).** On 13 November 2025 the Federal Court of Justice (IX ZR 127/24) held that shareholders' capital-market damages claims are not ordinary insolvency claims and rank behind ordinary creditors [3]. The court's press release records that about 50,000 shareholders had lodged claims of about €8.5 billion ⚑, that total lodged claims were about €15.4 billion ⚑, and that the available estate was about €650 million ⚑ [3]. *Reporting note:* lodged claims are amounts asserted by claimants, not amounts proved or owed.

**1.6 No state liability of the supervisor (final).** On 10 January 2024 the Federal Court of Justice (III ZR 57/23) refused an investor leave to appeal against the rejection of a damages claim against the Federal Financial Supervisory Authority (BaFin). It stated that BaFin's market-abuse and financial-reporting measures concerning Wirecard from April 2015 to June 2020 were not open to objection and were "in any event defensible" (*vertretbar*) [6]. *Status: final as to that claim.* This is a finding about legal liability, not a finding that supervision was good (compare 1.8).

**1.7 Auditor-oversight sanction (regulator).** On 31 March 2023 the ruling chamber of Germany's auditor oversight body, APAS, found breaches of professional duty "proven" in the audits of Wirecard AG and Wirecard Bank AG for 2016–2018 and imposed sanctions on the audit firm and five individual auditors; proceedings against seven further auditors ended because they gave up their licences [7]. APAS's own statement records a fine of €500,000 ⚑ on the audit firm, a two-year ban on new statutory audits of public-interest entities running from the date the decision becomes final, and fines of €23,000 to €300,000 ⚑ on the five auditors [7]. *Status: whether the decision became final after any objection is not confirmed from a primary record (Unverified or uncertain items).*

**1.8 Official finding of supervisory deficiencies (regulator review).** The European Securities and Markets Authority's Fast Track Peer Review (3 November 2020) identified deficiencies in BaFin's and the Financial Reporting Enforcement Panel's application of EU enforcement guidelines in the Wirecard case: weak information on employees' shareholdings (raising doubts about conflict-of-interest controls), a heightened risk of influence by the finance ministry, non-selection of Wirecard's reports for examination in 2016–2018 despite risk signals, and examinations whose scope missed areas material to the business [8][9]. *Status: a supervisory peer-review finding, not a court judgment.*

**1.9 Foreign criminal convictions (Singapore, final as reported).** Singapore courts convicted: two former employees of the group's Asian holding company, of criminal breach of trust and other offences, sentenced to 21 and 10 months (20 June 2023) [10]; a company director, of criminal breach of trust and other offences in relation to the Asian holding company, sentenced to 12 months (27 June 2023) [11]; and a sole director of another company, who pleaded guilty to failing to use reasonable diligence when signing letters to Wirecard AG, sentenced to four weeks and disqualified as a director for five years (convicted 11 January 2024; sentenced 7 February 2024) [12]. *Scope note:* these convictions concern specific persons and acts in Singapore. They do not establish what senior management in Germany knew.

**1.10 Supervisor's fine against the company (final).** On 15 April 2019 the Federal Financial Supervisory Authority (BaFin) fined Wirecard AG €1.52 million ⚑ for making its 2018 half-year financial report partly late and for late publication of the notice saying where the report could be found; the finance ministry's report to the Bundestag finance committee records the fine and that the company accepted it [27]. *Status: final regulator sanction against the company. Scope note:* this fine concerns the timing of publication, not the truth of the figures.

## How the fraud worked (what is established versus alleged)

- **Established by admission:** the reported €1.9 billion in trust balances, which underpinned the Third Party Acquiring business, was probably fictitious, and the company's own descriptions of that business were wrong [2].
- **Established by a civil court:** the 2017 and 2018 accounts overstated assets by roughly two-fifths of the balance sheet, or else breached bookkeeping rules [4].
- **Alleged, not adjudicated:** former managers are on trial in Munich (4 KLs 402 Js 108194/22) over the third-party acquiring business; no verdict had been given as of November 2026 [1, SECONDARY]. No court or prosecutor record of the charges could be opened, so their content and the defences are not set out here.
- **Peer-reviewed accounts of the mechanism.** A governance study in a journal whose review standards are uncertain (low confidence) argues that pressure to meet earnings consensus, pay tied to share-price growth and weak board supervision created the opportunity for earnings manipulation that went undetected for years [13]. An early-warning study applied the Beneish M-score to the 2016–2018 annual reports to test whether standard red-flag models would have signalled risk [14]. Scholarly book-length treatments analyse the audit failures in detail [15][16].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

- **Shareholders and creditors.** Lodged claims of about €15.4 billion against an estate of about €650 million ⚑, with about €8.5 billion asserted by about 50,000 shareholders ⚑ [3]. After the 2025 ruling, shareholders rank behind ordinary creditors [3], so their realistic recovery from the estate is small.
- **Lenders and bondholders.** A bond of €500 million is among the lodged claims [3].
- **Market trust and the supervisory system.** The ESMA review and the parliamentary inquiry documented failures in the institutions meant to catch false reporting [9][17]. Germany then abolished its two-tier enforcement system and moved all financial-reporting enforcement to BaFin [18][19].

## Fair counterarguments

**4.1 The short-selling ban.** In February 2019 BaFin banned new or increased net short positions in Wirecard shares for two months under the EU Short Selling Regulation [20], and ESMA issued a positive opinion on the measure [21]. The parliamentary inquiry examined the ban and BaFin's handling of short sellers at length [17]. In hindsight, critics read the ban as the supervisor protecting the company from those who doubted it; that reading is a judgement, not a finding. The ESMA peer review later criticised BaFin's independence and monitoring [8], but the Federal Court of Justice held BaFin's measures over 2015–2020 to be "defensible" for liability purposes [6]. Both statements are official; they answer different questions (supervisory quality versus legal liability).

**4.2 Auditor versus management responsibility.** The auditor oversight body found the auditors breached professional duties [7]. The sanctions reported (a €500,000 fine for the firm) are small next to the losses ⚑ [7]. Some argue that auditors were deceived by forged confirmations; others argue that independent bank confirmations should have been obtained much earlier. The civil court did not decide whether confirmations were forged [4]. Scholarly analyses focus on the audit failures [15].

**4.3 Fraud or embezzlement?** Who did what is the question before the Munich court, which had not ruled as of November 2026 [1, SECONDARY]. Until the verdict, the established core is the company's admission and the void accounts, not a finding of who did what.

**4.4 Detection.** In Wirecard, financial press reporting questioned the accounts before the collapse, and the parliamentary inquiry report has a section on that reporting and the authorities' response to it [17]. Critics read this as showing that outsiders raised the alarm before the auditors and the supervisor acted; that reading is a judgement, not a finding.

**4.5 Figures.** The €1.9 billion is the company's own number [2]; other figures in reporting on the criminal case measure different things and are not used here.

**How the conclusion handles these points.** The case limits its company-level claims to what is final: the void accounts and the supervisor's fine. It does not treat the company's admission, press coverage or the prosecution's account as proof of fraud, and it reports the supervisor's legal vindication (4.1) alongside the ESMA criticism.

## Values (labelled section: these are judgements, not findings)

- **Gatekeepers owe the public, not the client.** Auditors and supervisors exist because investors cannot check the books themselves. When they defer to a prestigious client, the public pays.
- **National champions.** Protecting a high-profile domestic company from critics is a political temptation. Supervisory independence matters most when a company is popular.
- **Presumption of innocence.** Strong public belief in fraud does not replace a verdict. Naming roles and labelling allegations is a way to respect both victims and defendants.
- **Who bears loss.** Ranking shareholders behind creditors follows insolvency law's logic; it also means retail investors who were misled bear most of the loss.

## Proposed solutions (each sourced and rated for evidence)

Ratings: **strong** means consistent causal evidence from more than one careful study; **moderate** means one careful study or consistent observational evidence; **weak** means indirect, anecdotal or model-only support; **UNTESTED** means no evaluation of effect was located.

**6.1 Single, independent financial-reporting enforcer.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED (reform in force; no outcome evaluation yet).** Germany's FISG (2021) ended the two-tier system and gave BaFin direct forensic powers [19]. *Evidence:* ESMA's 2024 follow-up records that the two-tier system was discontinued and all financial-information supervision moved to BaFin, and assesses how the recommendations were addressed [18]. Outcome evidence on fraud detection is not yet available.

**6.2 Mandatory audit-firm rotation for public-interest entities.** **Evidence rating: moderate (changes auditor behaviour near the end of a term; no evidence it prevents large frauds).** EU Regulation 537/2014 limits audit engagements of public-interest entities [22], and FISG tightened German rules [19]. *Evidence:* in Italy, where rotation has applied for over 20 years, auditors became more conservative in the final term before mandatory rotation, and investors perceived higher audit quality in that term [23]. The evidence is that rotation changes incentives near the end of a term; it does not show rotation prevents large frauds.

**6.3 Independent inspection of auditors with real consequences.** **Evidence rating: moderate.** *Evidence:* in the United States, higher PCAOB inspection deficiency rates led auditors to issue more adverse internal-control opinions, including to clients with real misstatements, consistent with improved audit quality [24]. Germany's APAS performs a comparable role; its Wirecard sanctions show the mechanism but not its deterrent effect [7].

**6.4 Protect and reward outside and inside whistleblowers, including journalists.** **Evidence rating: moderate (U.S. evidence; transfer to Germany untested).** *Evidence:* in U.S. data, exposure to the Dodd-Frank whistleblower reward programme was followed by a 12–22% lower likelihood of accounting fraud relative to controls [28]; whether this carries over to German law and to outside reporters such as journalists is untested. The ESMA review's criticism of monitoring [8] supports giving supervisors duties to act on credible outside signals.

**6.5 Use red-flag screening in enforcement.** **Evidence rating: UNTESTED (use in enforcement is debated; no evaluation located).** *Evidence:* applying standard models such as the Beneish M-score to published accounts is feasible [14]; its predictive value for this case is debated, and false positives are a cost.

## What this does not replace

This case does not replace the Munich criminal court's verdict, which is still pending, or any judgment on individual responsibility. It is not investment advice, not a guide to claims in the Wirecard insolvency, and not a substitute for reading the court and supervisory records themselves.
