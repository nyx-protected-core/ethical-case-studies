# JPMorgan Precious Metals and Treasuries Spoofing (2008–2016): a DPA, a Record CFTC Order and Trader Convictions

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. JPMorgan Chase & Co. is included because it entered a deferred prosecution agreement with an admitted statement of facts over spoofing in precious metals and US Treasury markets, and three JPMorgan entities consented to a CFTC order with findings and $920.2 million in monetary relief. Former traders appear by role only. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies tend to announce headline totals.*

## Scope and ground rules

**Why this case was added.** The set already covers collusive benchmark manipulation (LIBOR, FX). This case adds a different, single-firm form of market manipulation, "spoofing" (placing orders intended to be cancelled before execution to mislead other traders). It is fully adjudicated at the corporate level (a criminal deferred prosecution agreement with an admitted statement of facts, and the largest monetary relief the CFTC had imposed). Individual traders were convicted by a jury. It involves an institution that had pleaded guilty in the 2015 FX case, which makes it a direct test of whether the earlier sanctions deterred. A peer-reviewed study analyses this specific case's order data.

This case covers the September 2020 resolutions with JPMorgan Chase & Co. and affiliates (DOJ, CFTC, SEC), the end of the DPA, and the 2022 jury verdict against two former precious metals traders.

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts, each with its primary record)

### 1.1 DOJ deferred prosecution agreement (29 September 2020)

- JPMorgan Chase & Co. entered a DPA resolving a criminal information filed in the District of Connecticut charging two counts of wire fraud, covering two schemes: tens of thousands of episodes of unlawful trading in precious metals futures, and thousands of episodes in US Treasury futures and cash markets [1].
- Traders on the precious metals desk placed orders "with the intent to cancel those orders before execution", including in an attempt to profit by "injecting false and misleading information concerning the existence of genuine supply and demand" [1].
- JPMorgan agreed to pay over $920 million ⚑ in a criminal penalty, criminal disgorgement and victim compensation, with parts credited against CFTC and SEC payments [1].
- The DOJ cited as factors the conduct's eight-year span, the bank's failure to fully and voluntarily self-disclose, and its prior criminal history, "including a guilty plea on May 20, 2015, for similar misconduct involving manipulative and deceptive trading practices in the foreign currency exchange spot market"; it noted that substantially all the conduct occurred before that plea [1].
- The DOJ gave credit for cooperation and remediation and decided an independent monitor was unnecessary [1].
- **Outcome of the DPA.** The three-year term expired on 29 September 2023; on 29 March 2024 the court granted the DOJ's motion to dismiss the case with prejudice because JPMorgan had met its obligations [2].

*Status: admitted (DPA with statement of facts). Strength: Strong. The Justice Department release [1] could not be opened in this check, so the quoted wording and the payment breakdown in this subsection are UNVERIFIED against it. The DPA itself, dated 29 September 2020, is recorded in the opened CFTC order [4], and its expiry and dismissal on the opened case page [2].*

### 1.2 CFTC order (29 September 2020)

- The CFTC ordered JPMorgan Chase & Co., JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. and J.P. Morgan Securities LLC to pay $920.2 million ⚑, including restitution of $311,737,008, disgorgement of $172,034,790 and a civil penalty of $436,431,811, described by the CFTC as the largest monetary relief it had ever imposed [3].
- The order finds that "from at least 2008 through 2016", traders on the precious metals and Treasuries desks, "including the heads of both desks", placed hundreds of thousands of orders in gold, silver, platinum, palladium, Treasury note and Treasury bond futures with the intent to cancel them before execution [3; 4].
- The order also finds that, during the investigation, JPMorgan responded incompletely or unsatisfactorily to some requests "in a manner that resulted in the Division being misled" [4].
- The respondents consented without admitting or denying the findings, except to the extent admitted in agreements with the DOJ or other agencies [4].

*Strength: Strong.*

### 1.3 SEC order (29 September 2020)

The DOJ release records that J.P. Morgan Securities LLC agreed with the SEC to pay $10 million in disgorgement and a $25 million civil penalty ⚑ [1]. *(Neither the SEC order nor the Justice Department release could be opened in this check, so these figures are UNVERIFIED.)*

### 1.4 Individual convictions (August 2022)

A federal jury in the Northern District of Illinois convicted two former JPMorgan precious metals traders of fraud, attempted price manipulation and spoofing after a three-week trial [5] (N.D. Ill. No. 1:19-cr-00669; the Justice Department release could not be opened in this check, so the details in this subsection are UNVERIFIED against it). The evidence at trial showed a scheme from about May 2008 to August 2016 involving thousands of deceptive trading sequences in gold, silver, platinum and palladium futures on the New York Mercantile Exchange [5]. Two other former traders had earlier pleaded guilty [5]. The jury acquitted on racketeering-related charges [11, SECONDARY].

On 20 August 2025 the Seventh Circuit affirmed the convictions (Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846 and 23-2849), holding that spoofing as charged constitutes a scheme to defraud under the wire and commodities fraud statutes [13].

*Strength: Strong (jury verdict affirmed on appeal).*

## How the scheme worked

1. **The legal definition.** The Commodity Exchange Act expressly prohibits conduct "commonly known to the trade as, 'spoofing' (bidding or offering with the intent to cancel the bid or offer before execution)" [6]. A sociological study links the prohibition to Congress's response to the global financial crisis and to the first criminal prosecutions [12].
2. **The mechanics.** Traders placed orders they intended to cancel ("spoof orders" and "layered spoof orders"), sending false signals of supply or demand designed to deceive other participants into executing against orders the traders did want filled [4]. The traders knew other participants often considered order book balance when deciding how to trade [4].
3. **What it bought.** Execution of the orders the traders wanted filled [4]. A peer-reviewed analysis of this case's exchange messages, using visualisation tools from particle physics, offers "an alternative motivation for spoofing than moving the price" [7].
4. **Research on the mechanism.** Empirical work on a Korean exchange examines the strategy and performance of spoofing traders [8].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

- **Restitution ⚑.** The CFTC ordered $311.7 million in restitution [3], and the DOJ DPA included victim compensation credited against it [1]. These are the only sourced figures for counterparty harm, and they were agreed, not measured by a court.
- **Disgorgement ⚑.** $172.0 million in disgorgement [3] indicates the agencies' view of gains, not market-wide harm.
- **Market quality.** Spoofing degrades the information in order books. Legal-economic analysis evaluates its effects on social welfare and economic efficiency and argues current enforcement is both under- and over-inclusive [9], so not every instance has the same social cost.

*Strength: Strong that the conduct occurred; Limited for size of harm (agreed figures only).*

## Fair counterarguments

1. **Is spoofing wrong in itself?** One peer-reviewed philosophical argument holds that spoofing is a form of bluffing within market practice and could benefit hedgers by frustrating high-frequency traders, and should therefore be legalised [10]. Most legal systems reject this; the jury verdict [5] and agency orders [3] reflect the prevailing view.
2. **Where is the line?** A sociological study traces how spoofing moved from a valued floor-trading skill to a crime and argues that drawing the boundary between spoofing and legitimate trading "remains work-in-progress" [12]. Legal scholars note sharp disagreement among courts and economists on when quoting activity is manipulation [9].
3. **Was the 2015 plea a deterrent?** The DOJ noted that substantially all of the spoofing occurred before the FX plea [1]. Yet the conduct ran in parallel with the FX conduct for years [1; 3], which suggests the bank's controls failed across desks.
4. **DPA versus conviction.** The DPA ended in dismissal with prejudice after the bank met its obligations [2]. Supporters see this as successful remediation; critics see repeated non-trial resolutions for repeat institutions as weak deterrence. No peer-reviewed evaluation of this DPA's effect was located.
5. **Combined totals ⚑.** "$920 million" is a combined resolution with credits [1; 3]; reporting it as DOJ plus CFTC plus SEC sums would double count.

**How the conclusion handles these points.** The bank's entry rests on the DPA and the CFTC order, which it consented to without admitting or denying the findings except as admitted to the Justice Department. The case does not adopt the argument for legalising spoofing, and it treats the 2015 forex plea as a prior record noted by the prosecutor, not as proof that the plea failed to deter, since most of the spoofing came before it.

## Values (labelled section: judgement, not fact)

*This section states value positions. It is not a finding.*

- **Truthful signals.** An order book works only if visible orders are, in general, real offers. Systematically placing orders one intends never to fill is a form of deception towards other participants.
- **Leadership responsibility.** The CFTC found that the heads of both desks took part [3]. When supervisors engage in the conduct, controls designed around supervisors cannot work.
- **Repeat institutions.** Fairness to the public suggests escalating consequences for firms with prior pleas, even where the new conduct predates the old plea.
- **Room for dissent.** Arguments that spoofing should be legal [10] deserve a hearing; the right response is evidence on who is harmed, not dismissal.
