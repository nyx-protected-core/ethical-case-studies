# The LCD Panel Cartel: AU Optronics, LG Display and Others — the "Crystal Meetings", Pleas, a Trial Conviction and EU Fines

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. The companies named here (LG Display, Sharp, Chunghwa Picture Tubes, AU Optronics, Samsung and the other panel makers listed) pleaded guilty in the United States, were convicted at trial, or were fined or granted immunity by the European Commission. Executives appear by role only. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies tend to announce cumulative totals.*

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers the price-fixing cartel among makers of thin-film transistor liquid crystal display (TFT-LCD) panels used in monitors, notebook computers, televisions and phones, which operated in the early and mid-2000s. It covers the US criminal case (guilty pleas by LG Display, Sharp and Chunghwa Picture Tubes in 2008, and AU Optronics' conviction at trial in 2012, affirmed on appeal in 2015) and the European Commission's 2010 decision.

- **Findings first.** The Findings section lists only adjudicated or admitted findings: guilty pleas, a jury verdict and sentence affirmed on appeal, and a Commission decision. Each is tied to its primary record.
- **Source tags** are defined at the top of Sources. No news reports are used.
- **Government figures are marked ⚑.** DOJ totals ("$1.39 billion") combine pleas and sentences across companies and years; the EU total includes a zero fine for the immunity applicant. The jury's "gain" finding was a sentencing threshold ("$500 million or more"), not a precise loss estimate.
- **People.** No individual is named. Executives are described by role. Court decisions are cited by court and case number; captions naming individuals are not reproduced.
- **Evidence strength.** Findings are rated **Strong**, **Moderate**, **Limited** or **Very limited** for how firmly the record supports them. Proposed solutions carry a separate evidence rating: strong, moderate, weak or UNTESTED.

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts, each with its primary record)

### 1.1 United States: guilty pleas (2008)

- LG Display, Sharp and Chunghwa Picture Tubes agreed to plead guilty and pay $585 million ⚑ in total criminal fines [1]: LG Display $400 million, which the DOJ described as then the second highest fine imposed by its Antitrust Division ⚑; Sharp $120 million; Chunghwa $65 million [1].
- LG Display agreed to plead guilty to a conspiracy from September 2001 to June 2006 to fix TFT-LCD prices sold worldwide [1]; its plea agreement was filed in the Northern District of California in December 2008 [2].
- Sharp's pleas covered separate conspiracies on panels sold to particular large customers for computer monitors, notebooks and phones [1].

*Status: admitted (pleas). Strength: Strong.*

### 1.2 United States: AU Optronics, conviction at trial (2012) and appeal (2015)

- After an eight-week trial, a jury found AU Optronics Corporation, its US subsidiary and two former senior executives guilty on 13 March 2012 [3]. The jury found that they fixed prices of LCD panels sold into the United States [3]. The indictment charged participation from 14 September 2001 to 1 December 2006 [3].
- In September 2012 the court fined AU Optronics $500 million ⚑, matching the then largest fine imposed on a company for a Sherman Act violation, placed it and its US subsidiary on three years' probation, and required an antitrust compliance programme and an independent monitor [3]. Each of the two executives received a prison sentence [3].
- The jury also found that the combined gross gains to all participants were "$500 million or more" [4].
- **Appeal.** The Ninth Circuit affirmed all convictions and AU Optronics' sentence [4]. It held that import trade falls within the Sherman Act without needing the Foreign Trade Antitrust Improvements Act's exceptions; that the evidence on import trade alone was enough to convict; and that the alternative-fine statute permits a fine based on the gross gains to all co-conspirators, not only to the defendant [4].
- **Running total ⚑.** Including the 2012 sentences, the DOJ reported eight companies convicted with fines totalling $1.39 billion, 22 executives charged and 12 convicted [3].

*Strength: Strong (jury verdict affirmed on appeal).*

### 1.3 European Union (December 2010)

- The European Commission fined six LCD panel producers a total of €648,925,000 ⚑ for a cartel from October 2001 to February 2006: Samsung Electronics and LG Display of Korea, and AU Optronics, Chimei InnoLux, Chunghwa Picture Tubes and HannStar Display of Taiwan [5]. Samsung received full immunity as the first to provide information [5].
- The members held monthly multilateral and further bilateral meetings, around 60 in total, mainly in hotels in Taiwan, which they called "the Crystal meetings", and took steps to conceal the venue and results [5].

*Strength: Strong.*

## How the scheme worked

1. **Regular meetings.** Senior staff met monthly to exchange information and agree target prices, mostly in Taiwan [3; 5]. The DOJ described meetings in hotel conference rooms, karaoke bars and tea rooms [3].
2. **Focus on high-volume products.** An economic case study of the cartel found that, rather than coordinating hundreds of product variants, members focused on the highest-selling products at an aggregated level that did not fully account for quality differences [9, WP].
3. **Concealment.** The Commission found deliberate steps to hide meeting venues and outcomes [5].
4. **Reaching downstream buyers.** Panels were sold to manufacturers of monitors, notebooks, televisions and phones, including some of the largest buyers in those markets [1]. Much of the volume was sold outside the United States and imported inside finished products, which raised the jurisdiction questions in Fair counterarguments [4].
5. **General cartel organisation.** The structure (regular meetings, price targets, monitoring) matches the patterns documented across European Commission cartel decisions [10].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

- **Market size ⚑.** The DOJ put the worldwide TFT-LCD panel market at about $70 billion in 2006 [1]. This is a measure of the market, not of harm.
- **Gains to the cartel.** The jury's finding of combined gross gains of "$500 million or more" [4] is a threshold for sentencing, not an estimate of total harm.
- **Case-specific overcharge estimate.** An economic case study estimates the overcharge at no higher than 2 percent, but notes that even 2 percent would have produced incremental revenues to the conspirators of more than $3 billion [9, WP].
- **General evidence.** Meta-analyses of other cartels find typical overcharges far above 2 percent (surveys report averages of 18 percent or more [11]). The gap between these averages and the LCD estimate is itself a point of disagreement (Fair counterarguments).
- **The DOJ's statement** that every buyer of affected products paid more is an agency claim ⚑ [3], not a measured result.

*Strength: Strong that the conduct occurred; Limited for size of harm.*

## Fair counterarguments

1. **How effective was the cartel?** The case study's low overcharge estimate (no more than 2 percent) is attributed to the difficulty of fixing prices across many products and fast technological change [9, WP]. Survey averages are much higher [11]. Both can be true: a large cartel can earn billions from a small percentage. The chapter's authors' role, if any, in the litigation was not checked (Unverified or uncertain items).
2. **Reach of US law.** The defendants argued that most panels were sold to third parties abroad, so the link to US commerce was too weak; the Ninth Circuit rejected this for the criminal case on the import-trade theory [4]. How far US civil plaintiffs can recover for panels bought abroad and imported inside finished goods was contested in later civil litigation that is not covered here (Unverified or uncertain items).
3. **Fines based on all conspirators' gains.** The Ninth Circuit allowed a fine based on gains to the whole conspiracy [4]. Critics may see this as punishing one firm for others' gains; the court read the statute that way.
4. **Overlapping punishment across jurisdictions.** The same conduct was fined in the US and the EU [1; 3; 5]. Whether parallel enforcement over-punishes is debated in the literature; peer-reviewed estimates nonetheless suggest that cartel fines are often below the gains from collusion [11].
5. **Prison for foreign executives.** US prosecutors obtained prison terms for executives of a foreign company [3]. A peer-reviewed review found that such sentences grew but that extradition remained rare and many executives who went to trial were acquitted [12].

**How the conclusion handles these points.** Company entries rest on pleas, the affirmed jury verdict against AU Optronics and the Commission decision. The case gives no single harm figure: the low case-study overcharge and the higher survey averages are both reported, and the fines are described as enforcement outcomes, not as measures of what buyers lost.

## Values (labelled section: judgement, not fact)

*This section states value positions. It is not a finding.*

- **Hidden costs spread thin.** A small overcharge on a component in nearly every screen is invisible to each buyer but adds up to large transfers. Harm that is hard to see is still harm.
- **Global firms, national laws.** Firms that sell into many countries should expect to answer to each one's competition law, but fairness requires that the combined penalty bear some relation to the harm.
- **Responsibility.** Executives who attended the meetings made choices; the companies that rewarded them shaped those choices. Both deserve scrutiny.
