# VimpelCom / VEON (2016): coordinated U.S. and Netherlands resolution of bribes for Uzbek telecom licences and frequencies

Date: 2026-11-19

**Key to flags.** ⚑ marks a figure published by a government or court body. These are negotiated amounts and agency findings, not measures of harm. Agencies have reasons to present coordinated resolutions as landmark successes ("one of the largest ... ever"), and the figures cover only detected conduct (detection bias).

---

## 1. Summary

On 18 February 2016, **VimpelCom Ltd.** (since renamed VEON Ltd.), an Amsterdam-headquartered telecom group, and its wholly owned Uzbek subsidiary **Unitel LLC** admitted to a conspiracy to pay **more than $114 million ⚑** in bribes between 2006 and 2012. The payments went to an Uzbek government official, so that the companies could enter and stay in the Uzbek telecom market [R1].

The resolution had four parts:
- **Unitel pleaded guilty** and was sentenced in the Southern District of New York.
- **VimpelCom entered a deferred prosecution agreement (DPA)**.
- The SEC obtained a **court-ordered judgment**.
- The Netherlands Public Prosecution Service (OM) concluded an **out-of-court settlement** of **$397.5 million ⚑**.

The combined U.S. and Netherlands amount was **$795,326,398.40 ⚑** [R1], [R2], [R3]. A **three-year independent monitor** was imposed [R1], [R2]. The U.S. charges were **dismissed with prejudice on 26 February 2020** after VimpelCom met its obligations [R5].

**Evidence strength: strong.** The DOJ release (PDF), SEC release, OM release and court docket order were opened. The signed DPA and Unitel plea were opened but are scanned images with little extractable text.

The OM adds that Unitel paid "over 30,000,000 US dollars in sponsorships or charitable contributions", some of which "can be linked to the acquisition of licenses, permits and number blocks" [R3]. The SEC found about half a million dollars in bribes "disguised as charitable donations" [R2]. The official is not named in this case.

## 4. How the scheme worked, as found in the record

- **Shell company owned by the official.** Payments went to a shell company that "certain VimpelCom and Unitel management knew was beneficially owned by the foreign official" [R1].
- **Disguises.** The payments were recorded as "equity transactions, consulting and repudiation agreements and reseller transactions", together with sham charitable donations [R1], [R2].
- **Defeating legal advice.** When the board sought an FCPA opinion, "certain VimpelCom management withheld crucial information from outside counsel ... rendering them worthless" [R1].
- **Laundering through global banks.** The official's associates laundered proceeds through accounts in Latvia, the UK, Hong Kong, Ireland, Belgium, Luxembourg and Switzerland, with transfers routed through U.S. financial institutions [R1].
- **Market effect.** In the OM's view, the bribery "resulted in a dominant position in the telecommunication market in Uzbekistan and significant profits" [R3].

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **Monitor.** A three-year monitor ended in October 2019. The certification is reported by the company [R7], and the DOJ's motion confirmed full compliance [R5]. The monitor's reports are not public.
- **Peer-reviewed context.** Cooley & Sharman (2015) analyse Central Asian corruption as transnational networks linking multinationals, host-country elites, offshore vehicles, brokers and destination banks. One of their illustrative cases concerns telecommunications contracts in Uzbekistan [P1]. Cooley & Sharman (2017) argue that professional intermediaries in major financial centres "lower the transaction costs of transnational corruption by senior foreign officials" [P2]. The DOJ's account of laundering through seven jurisdictions [R1] fits that argument.

## 7. Fair counterarguments

1. **A 45% discount without self-disclosure.** The DOJ gave a 45% reduction for prompt acknowledgement and cooperation, but withheld further credit because the companies "did not voluntarily self-disclose" [R1]. Supporters say fast resolution saves public resources. Critics, such as Lord (2022) writing on non-trial resolutions in general, argue that such arrangements tend to construct corporate wrongdoing as manageable and offsettable [P3].
2. **Where the money goes.** Fines went to the U.S. and the Netherlands [R1], [R3]. The forfeiture actions aim at the bribe proceeds, and the DOJ describes using recovered assets "to benefit the people harmed by corruption" where appropriate [R1]. **Open question:** final disposition and any return of the forfeited funds to Uzbekistan were not verified from primary records.
3. **Netherlands as a jurisdiction.** The OM bases its jurisdiction on VimpelCom's Amsterdam headquarters and says the company "benefits from the favorable business climate in The Netherlands" [R3]. Bu (2022) argues that overlapping sovereign claims call for a global settlement regime [P4]. Here, a 50/50 split was agreed in advance [R3].
4. **Does bribery pay?** The OM says the bribes produced market dominance and significant profits [R3]. Cheung, Rau & Stouraitis (2021) find benefits rise with bribe size, and that disclosure reduces those benefits mainly in democracies [P5]. Uzbekistan in this period was not a democracy, so the benefits were likely large.

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that VimpelCom and Unitel admitted bribery, because it rests on a DPA, a guilty plea, an SEC judgment and a Netherlands settlement. It accepts that much of the forfeiture story is still open, so it reports the forfeiture actions as claims and states the amounts sought, not amounts recovered or returned.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Allocate licences and spectrum through public auctions with beneficial-ownership disclosure.** The admitted scheme used a shell company owned by the official to control access to licences and frequencies [R1], [R2]. *Basis:* [R1]–[R3]; Cooley & Sharman [P1]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
2. **Make professional enablers accountable.** Banks, company formation agents and others in the financial centres used for laundering [R1] should face enforceable due-diligence duties. *Basis:* Cooley & Sharman [P2]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
3. **Tie charitable giving in regulated sectors to public registers.** Donations by regulated firms to entities linked to officials should be pre-cleared and published. *Basis:* sham donations [R2] and the OM's link between $30m+ in sponsorships and licence acquisitions [R3]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
4. **Protect the integrity of legal advice.** Withholding information from counsel to obtain a clean opinion [R1] should be an aggravating factor, and boards should get direct access to investigators. *Basis:* [R1]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
5. **Publish the full chain of forfeiture results**, including any returns to the harmed country. *Basis:* the open question in §7.2. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*

## 10. Recommendation (normative)

Use VimpelCom with the Telia file to show a **multi-firm pattern in one market**. The DOJ records more than $800m paid by three telecom companies to the same official [R1]. The lesson is that the market structure, a single gatekeeper for state-allocated spectrum, matters as much as any one company's conduct.

## 12. What this does not replace
