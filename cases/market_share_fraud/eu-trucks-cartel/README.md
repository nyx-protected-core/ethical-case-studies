# The European Trucks Cartel (2016–2017 Decisions): List-Price Collusion, Emissions Costs, and What the Damages Courts Found

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. The truck makers named here (MAN, Daimler, DAF, Iveco, Volvo/Renault and Scania) were found by the European Commission to have taken part in a cartel from 1997 to 2011: five admitted liability in a 2016 settlement decision and Scania was fined in 2017 under the ordinary procedure. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies tend to announce headline totals.*

## Scope and ground rules

**Why this case was added.** It is the largest cartel fine package the European Commission has imposed in a single infringement (about €2.93 billion in 2016 plus €880 million on a sixth maker in 2017). Its findings are fully adjudicated: five makers admitted liability in a settlement, and the sixth's fine was upheld on appeal (the Court of Justice judgment could not be opened; see Unverified or uncertain items). It is also one of the few cartels where a court has made a reasoned, quantified finding on overcharge in follow-on damages litigation, so it lets agency figures, court findings and peer-reviewed estimates be compared side by side. It sits in the same vehicle supply chain as the auto-parts case.

This case covers the Commission's July 2016 settlement decision against MAN, Volvo/Renault, Daimler, Iveco and DAF, its September 2017 decision against Scania, Scania's unsuccessful appeal, and the UK Competition Appeal Tribunal's 2023 damages judgment (upheld by the Court of Appeal in 2024).

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts, each with its primary record)

### 1.1 European Commission settlement decision (19 July 2016)

- The Commission found that MAN, Volvo/Renault, Daimler, Iveco and DAF broke EU antitrust rules by colluding for 14 years, from 1997 until 2011, across the whole European Economic Area [1].
- The collusion covered: coordinating "gross list" (factory) prices for medium and heavy trucks; the timing for introducing emissions technologies to meet the Euro III to Euro VI standards; and the passing on to customers of the costs of those technologies [1].
- Fines ⚑ [1]: Daimler €1,008,766,000; DAF €752,679,000; Volvo/Renault €670,448,000; Iveco €494,606,000; MAN €0 (immunity for revealing the cartel). Total €2,926,499,000. Volvo/Renault, Daimler and Iveco also received leniency reductions, and all five received a 10% settlement reduction [1].
- The five makers together accounted for around 9 of every 10 medium and heavy trucks produced in Europe [1].

*Status: settlement with acknowledged liability. Strength: Strong.*

### 1.2 Scania (27 September 2017) and appeal

- Scania did not settle. Under the standard procedure the Commission found that Scania took part in the same cartel and fined it €880,523,000 ⚑ [2].
- On 1 February 2024 the Court of Justice dismissed Scania's further appeal, leaving the fine in place [3]. *(The case page was located, but the judgment text could not be opened. The outcome is taken from the case listing and is marked UNVERIFIED against the judgment text; the Court's reasoning is not relied on. The 2017 fine itself is confirmed by the Commission release [2].)*

*Strength: Strong (decision upheld on appeal).*

### 1.3 UK follow-on damages: *Royal Mail and BT v DAF* (CAT 2023; Court of Appeal 2024)

- The Competition Appeal Tribunal found that the infringement caused loss to Royal Mail and BT through an overcharge it assessed at 5% of the value of commerce over the whole relevant period, using a "broad axe" approach [4].
- The claimants' economic expert had estimated an average overcharge of about 9.95%; the defendant's expert concluded there was no overcharge; the Tribunal's 5% is "approximately half of what they are claiming" [4].
- The Tribunal rejected DAF's arguments that the overcharge was offset by pass-on [4; 5].
- The Court of Appeal dismissed DAF's appeal in February 2024 [5].

*Strength: Strong that a court made this finding; it is one court's broad-axe estimate for two buyers, not a market-wide measure.*

## How the scheme worked

1. **List-price coordination.** Gross list prices are the starting point from which national and dealer discounts are negotiated [1]. Coordinating increases at that level could move the price base without fixing each final deal.
2. **Emissions technology.** Each new Euro standard required new technology. The makers coordinated when to introduce it and how far to pass its cost on [1]. That turned a regulatory change meant to cut pollution into an occasion for collusion.
3. **Long duration and wide reach.** Fourteen years across the whole EEA, ending with Commission inspections in January 2011 [1].
4. **Leniency trigger.** MAN's report gave it immunity [1]; others gained reductions for cooperation [1].

- **Who bears the cost.** Road hauliers and other fleet buyers; trucks carry around three quarters of inland freight in Europe according to the Commission [2], so some cost may pass through to goods prices. No sourced estimate of final-consumer pass-on was located.

*Strength: Moderate that the cartel raised prices (one court finding plus two studies using different methods); Limited on the exact size.*

## Fair counterarguments

1. **Did list-price collusion raise transaction prices?** The defence position in damages cases is that coordination at list-price level did not translate into higher prices paid, because discounts were negotiated separately; in the UK case the defendant's expert found no overcharge, but the Tribunal found 5% [4].
2. **What a non-significant estimate means.** Peer-reviewed commentary on the German Federal Court of Justice's *Trucks III* ruling argues that a precise overcharge estimate close to zero is stronger evidence of "no harm" than of harm, and urges experts and courts to be transparent about their prior beliefs [8]. This cuts against presuming harm from the existence of a cartel.
3. **Size of overcharge across estimates.** Court (5%) [4], model-based (up to 7.6%) [6] and proxy-based (18%) [7] estimates differ widely. A European sample of cartels puts the mean overcharge at about 20% [9]. Method, country, buyer size and period all matter [7].
4. **Is the fine enough?** The welfare study argues the loss "cannot be offset by fines or private damage claims" [6]. Peer-reviewed work on EU fining finds fines moderate relative to illicit gains [10] but also that the 2006 fining guidelines and 2014 Damages Directive seem to have reduced overcharges [11].
5. **Leniency and settlements.** The immunity applicant paid nothing and settling firms got 10% off [1]. Leniency reductions to many members may soften incentives to confess first [12].

**How the conclusion handles these points.** The case reports the court's 5% finding, the model estimate and the proxy estimate side by side and does not pick one as the true harm. It treats the Commission decisions as findings that the cartel existed, not as findings about how much any buyer lost, which national courts decide case by case.

## Values (labelled section: judgement, not fact)

*This section states value positions. It is not a finding.*

- **Public rules, private gain.** Emissions standards are a public decision to reduce pollution. Using them as a coordinating device for price increases abuses the trust behind regulation.
- **Small buyers.** If smaller hauliers paid proportionally more [7], the harm falls hardest on those with least bargaining power.
- **Accuracy in courts.** Courts should neither presume large harm nor demand impossible precision; transparent reasoning about uncertainty [8] serves fairness to both sides.
- **Accountability beyond fines.** When a cartel lasts 14 years, the question of who inside the firms knew is a legitimate public concern, even if EU law sanctions only undertakings.
