# Petrobras (2018): U.S. non-prosecution agreement and SEC order over a contractor bribery and bid-rigging scheme

Date: 2026-11-19

**Key to flags.** ⚑ marks a figure published by a government or intergovernmental body. Such figures are negotiated settlement amounts, not measures of harm. Agencies have reasons to present resolutions as large and successful, and amounts are often credited across several agencies, so headline totals can overstate new money. Every figure also covers only conduct that was detected and resolved, which is a detection bias: undetected bribery leaves no record.

---

## 1. Summary

In September 2018, Petróleo Brasileiro S.A. (Petrobras), Brazil's state-controlled oil company, signed a **non-prosecution agreement (NPA)** with the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) and consented to a **cease-and-desist order** from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Petrobras admitted that its books, records and internal controls broke the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA), because they hid a scheme that ran from about 2004 to 2012. In that scheme, contractors rigged bids and paid kickbacks of roughly 1–3% of contract value to Petrobras executives, Brazilian politicians and political parties. The agreed criminal penalty was **$853.2 million ⚑**. Under a split set out in the NPA, 80% went to Brazilian authorities, 10% to the DOJ and 10% to the SEC.

The case has an unusual structure. The U.S. resolution holds Petrobras responsible for false books and failed controls, not for paying bribes. The same record also states that Petrobras was a **victim** of an embezzlement scheme run by some of its own executives. This makes Petrobras both a defendant and a victim, which is the main point of disagreement over the case (section 7).

**Evidence strength: strong.** The company formally admitted the facts in the NPA's Statement of Facts, and the SEC issued its own findings in an order. Both documents were opened and read directly.

**Countries and contracts named in the record.** All the conduct was in Brazil. The Statement of Facts describes contractor cartels winning Petrobras infrastructure contracts through non-competitive means, with bribes "typically" totalling "approximately one to three percent of the value of the contracts" [R1, ¶15]. It also describes a Singapore-based shipyard company paying bribes to a political party and a Petrobras manager [R1, ¶36]. Executives are identified only by number (Executive 1 to Executive 4) and as Manager 1. This case does not name them.

**Related civil matter (not an adjudication of wrongdoing).** The NPA records that Petrobras agreed to pay **$2.95 billion** to settle *In re Petrobras Securities Litigation*, No. 14-cv-9662 (S.D.N.Y.) [R1]. Petrobras reported that the court gave final approval on 22 June 2018, and stated that the settlement "does not constitute an admission of guilt" [R8]. The amount is a private civil settlement, not a government sanction.

## 3. Timeline

Primary-record dates are unmarked. News items are labelled **SECONDARY**.

- **c. 2004–2012:** Period of the bid-rigging and bribery scheme described in the Statement of Facts [R1].
- **September 2010:** Global equity offering. The SEC later found the offering documents materially misleading [R4].
- **2014:** Brazilian federal investigation "Operation Car Wash" (Lava Jato) uncovers the scheme. Petrobras hires outside law firms and tells the DOJ it will cooperate [R1], [R7].
- **January–June 2018:** U.S. securities class action settled for $2.95bn; final approval 22 June 2018 [R1], [R8].
- **26–27 September 2018:** DOJ NPA and SEC order [R1]–[R5].
- **5 September 2019:** At Brazil's Supreme Federal Tribunal (STF), the parties sign an agreement that sends about **R$2.6 billion ⚑** of the money recovered from Petrobras to education (R$1.6bn) and environmental protection (R$1bn). This replaces an earlier MPF–Petrobras plan for using the funds, which had been challenged in the STF (ADPF 568; Rcl 33667) [R9].
- **26 September 2021:** the NPA's three-year term ends [R1]. Petrobras announces it has fulfilled its obligations on 4 October 2021 [R7].

## 4. How the scheme worked, as found in the record

- **Cartel plus kickback.** Contractors and suppliers agreed among themselves who would win Petrobras contracts. They then paid kickbacks to the Petrobras executives who steered the awards. Those executives shared the money with politicians and parties, some of whom had helped them get their posts [R1, ¶¶14–16]; [R5].
- **Political appointment.** Each of the four executives was "appointed to his position under the influence of a political party" [R1, ¶¶3–6].
- **Concealment.** The bribe money was "often funneled through fictitious costs, including consultancy agreements" incurred by the contractors [R1, ¶17].
- **Accounting effect.** Petrobras recorded the inflated payments as the cost of acquiring and improving assets, which overstated property, plant and equipment [R4], [R5].
- **Why this is an FCPA case at all.** Petrobras was a U.S. issuer with New York-listed shares. The U.S. offence is false books and inadequate controls (§ 78m), not the anti-bribery provision. Petrobras was the organisation whose officials took bribes, not the one that paid them.

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **No monitor.** Instead there was annual self-reporting to the DOJ for three years, which ended with a completion statement [R1], [R7].
- **Governance changes recorded by the DOJ:** both the Board of Directors and the Executive Board were replaced, the compliance function was elevated, and more decisions now require board approval [R1, ¶(d)].
- **Use of the Brazilian share.** The money's use became a dispute in Brazil itself. The STF approved a negotiated agreement sending it to public education and the environment [R9].
- **Peer-reviewed assessment of the response.** A case study of Petrobras's anti-corruption and compliance disclosures for 2010–2017 finds that the company voluntarily and substantially increased those disclosures to regain legitimacy. It also finds that the company prioritised executive control and independence from its controlling shareholder, the government. The authors conclude the company considered it had successfully regained legitimacy by 2017 (Whitehead & Belghitar 2022) [P1]. This is a single-company content analysis. It shows what the company disclosed, not whether its controls actually worked.

## 7. Fair counterarguments

1. **Victim or defendant?** The NPA counts as a mitigating factor that executives "engaged in an embezzlement scheme that victimized the Company and its shareholders" [R1, ¶(k)]. Petrobras stresses this victim status in its filings [R6], [R7], [R8]. Others note that a company whose own board-level officers ran the scheme still misled investors in a $69.9bn offering, and the SEC called the conduct "fraudulently" raising money [R5]. Both are true at once: the U.S. instruments punish the misleading accounts, while the bribery itself was resolved against contractors and individuals in Brazil.
2. **Who pays?** Petrobras is majority state-owned. A penalty paid by the company falls in part on the Brazilian state and minority shareholders, including people who bought in the misleading 2010 offering. The 80% credit to Brazil and the SEC Fair Fund were meant to reduce this circularity [R1], [R5]. Whether that worked has not been measured. **Open question:** no peer-reviewed study of who ultimately bore the cost was found.
3. **Double-counting of headline totals.** Press coverage often added the $853m penalty, the $933m disgorgement and the $2.95bn class settlement together. The SEC release itself says the payments are "subject to offsets" [R5]. Bu (2022) argues that multi-sovereign enforcement against the same conduct needs a coherent global settlement regime, partly so that crediting is predictable [P2].
4. **Did the domestic crackdown last?** Da Ros & Taylor (2022) trace Lava Jato from early prosecutorial success to its declining effectiveness and ultimate neutering. They treat it as a test of legal accountability in a consolidating democracy [P3]. Parts of the domestic Lava Jato record were later annulled or narrowed by Brazilian courts. This case does not rely on any annulled finding. **Open question:** a full map of which Brazilian convictions linked to Petrobras contracts still stand was not built here.
5. **Does bribery pay?** In a worldwide sample of bribery cases, the net present value of a bribe *conditional on being caught* is close to zero for the median firm. Larger bribes are associated with larger benefits, and the benefits are smaller in democracies with more information disclosure (Cheung, Rau & Stouraitis 2021) [P4]. On that evidence, contractors in a cartel such as this one could expect a positive return unless detection was likely.

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that Petrobras admitted false books and failed controls, because that rests on a signed non-prosecution agreement and an SEC order. It accepts the strongest objection, that Petrobras was also a victim of executives who took bribes, and so it does not describe Petrobras as a payer of bribes. Who finally bore the cost and which Brazilian convictions still stand are left open.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

*These are value judgements, each tied to the evidence it relies on.*

1. **Make state-owned victims pass recoveries to the people harmed.** Where a state-controlled company is both victim and defendant, recovered sums should go to identifiable harmed parties or to transparent public purposes, as in the 2019 STF agreement [R9] and the SEC Fair Fund [R5], and not back into company accounts. *Basis:* the circularity problem in §7.2. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative.*
2. **Publish a single crediting table with every multi-agency resolution.** The table should show gross amounts, credits and net new money per agency, so readers do not double-count. *Basis:* the offsets in [R1] and [R5], and Bu's case for a predictable global settlement regime [P2]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative.*
3. **Treat cartel detection as anti-bribery policy.** Here bid-rigging and bribery were one scheme [R1, ¶14]. Procurement bodies should run statistical bid-pattern screens alongside anti-bribery controls. *Basis:* the record itself. **Open question:** no peer-reviewed evaluation of bid-screening in this specific case was found. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). No evaluation of bid screening in this setting was found.*
4. **Insulate appointments to state-company boards from party influence.** *Basis:* the admitted fact that all four executives were appointed "under the influence of a political party" [R1], and the backlash dynamics described by Da Ros & Taylor [P3]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative.*
5. **Raise the expected cost of being caught.** Cheung et al. find that bribes pay when detection is unlikely [P4], and Jensen & Malesky find that stronger peer monitoring reduces firm bribery [P5]. Peer-monitoring and disclosure regimes are therefore preferable to one-off headline penalties. *Evidence rating: moderate. Jensen and Malesky (2018) find reduced bribery after stronger OECD peer monitoring; the specific proposal is untested.*

## 10. Recommendation (normative)

Use this case to teach the **victim-defendant problem**. Readers should be shown which wrongs were admitted (false books and failed controls), by whom, and which money was credited where, before any total is quoted.

## 11. Unverified or uncertain items

- The MPF agreement on the Brazilian share was not opened; the NPA credits up to $682.56m to Brazil, and the company's filing describes the special fund.
- Estimates: the 1–3% bribe rate is described in the NPA as typical, and the $2.5bn asset overstatement is an SEC estimate.
- Open questions: who ultimately bore the cost of the penalty, and which Brazilian convictions linked to Petrobras contracts still stand.
- Peer-reviewed sources were read as abstracts.
- Checked against the source on 19 November 2026: the penalty ($853,200,000, 25% below the guidelines range), the split ($85,320,000 to the US, up to $85,320,000 for the SEC and up to $682,560,000 for Brazil), the SEC figures ($933,473,797; $69.9bn offering; about $10bn in ADSs; $2.5bn overstatement) and the 1%–3% kickback range.

## 12. What this does not replace

This case is not a finding that Petrobras paid bribes; the US record treats it as a company whose books hid bribes taken by its own executives. It does not replace the NPA, the statement of facts or the SEC order. It is not investment or legal advice, and it does not reflect the status of any Brazilian criminal case.
