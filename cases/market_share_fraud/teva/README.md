# Teva (2016): U.S. deferred prosecution agreement, Russian subsidiary guilty plea and SEC judgment over bribes for drug tenders, registrations and prescriptions

Date: 2026-11-19

**Key to flags.** ⚑ marks a figure published by a government or court body. These are negotiated amounts and agreed profit estimates, not measures of harm to patients or budgets. Agencies have reasons to present resolutions as significant, and the figures cover only conduct that was detected (detection bias).

---

## 1. Summary

On 22 December 2016, **Teva Pharmaceutical Industries Ltd.**, an Israel-based maker of generic and branded drugs, entered a **deferred prosecution agreement (DPA)** with the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) on a two-count FCPA information. The counts were conspiracy to violate the anti-bribery provisions, and violation of the internal-controls provisions [R1]. Its Russian subsidiary, **Teva LLC (Russia)**, **pleaded guilty** to conspiracy to violate the FCPA anti-bribery provisions [R2]. In a parallel civil case, the SEC obtained **$236,101,824 ⚑** in disgorgement and prejudgment interest [R1], [R3].

The DOJ criminal penalty was **$283,177,348 ⚑**, so the total was about **$519 million ⚑** [R1], [R3].

The record covers three countries:
- **Russia:** bribes to win government tenders for the multiple-sclerosis drug Copaxone, 2006–2012.
- **Ukraine:** payments to a senior Ministry of Health official to gain drug registrations, 2001/2002–2011.
- **Mexico:** control failures that let a distributor pay doctors at state-owned hospitals to prescribe Copaxone, 2012.

Teva's profits from the conduct were put at **about $221 million ⚑** [R1]. An **independent compliance monitor** served for three years. The DPA charges were **dismissed in July 2020** after the term ended [R4], [R5].

**Evidence strength: strong.** The DPA and Statement of Facts, the plea agreement and the motion to dismiss were opened directly.

**Countries and contracts named in the record.**
- **Russia:** Copaxone supplied under the Ministry of Health's central purchasing programme for rare and costly illnesses, bought at "usually bi-annual auctions" from about 2008. The record says payments to a "Russian Official", who could influence those purchases, were made "through the high profit margins" of a repackager and distributor that the official owned and controlled [R1, ¶¶16, 21].
- **Ukraine:** A high-ranking Ministry of Health official was retained as a "registration consultant". From about June 2002 to March 2011 the official received cash and other items worth **about $200,000 ⚑**, including monthly fees and travel [R1, ¶¶17, 58, 71–72].
- **Mexico:** In January 2012, Teva Mexico agreed an extra 2% margin for a distributor. Its purpose "was to pay the government HCPs in exchange for their writing prescriptions of Copaxone". Between February and March 2012, cash was delivered to "at least seven" doctors employed by state-owned or state-controlled institutions [R1, ¶¶82–83]. HCPs means healthcare practitioners.

No official is named in this case.

## 4. How the scheme worked, as found in the record

- **Bribes hidden in distributor margins.** In both Russia and Mexico, the money for corrupt payments came from **extra distributor margin** rather than direct payments from Teva [R1, ¶¶16, 82]. The SEC described bribes "concealed as legitimate payments to distributors" [R3].
- **A consultant who was the regulator.** In Ukraine, the official deciding on drug registration was paid as a "registration consultant" [R1, ¶¶17, 58].
- **High-value product in a public budget.** Copaxone "cost up to tens of thousands of dollars" per prescription and was Teva's "most profitable product" [R1, ¶15]. That made public tenders and prescriptions worth corrupting.
- **Prior warnings.** The record says Teva was "aware of red flags and prior corruption-related misconduct" at its Mexican subsidiary [R1, ¶19].

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **A three-year monitor** was imposed because compliance reforms were untested [R1]. According to Teva, the monitor certified its programme and the monitorship ended in February 2020 [R5]. The monitor's reports are **not public**, so no independent judgment of their quality is possible here.
- **Dismissal with prejudice** followed the company's certification of compliance [R4], [R5].
- **Health-system harm.** Mackey & Liang (2012) argue that corruption in health systems causes financial waste and adverse health consequences. They also argue that responses limited to single countries or sectors are fragmented and criticized as ineffective, and they propose a global governance framework [P1]. The Teva record does not measure harm to patients or health budgets. **Open question:** no peer-reviewed study estimating that harm in this case was found.

## 7. Fair counterarguments

1. **Did the penalty exceed the gain?** The combined DOJ penalty and SEC disgorgement (about $519m) exceed the agreed profit (about $221m) [R1]. Cheung, Rau & Stouraitis (2021) find that larger bribes go with larger benefits, and that for the median firm a caught bribe has a net present value near zero [P2]. This resolution appears to remove more than the measured gain. Whether that deters depends on how likely detection is.
2. **Are monitors worth it?** The DPA imposed a monitor [R1]. Arlen (2016) argues that prosecutors impose such mandates without ex ante guidance or ex post review, which violates the rule of law unless constrained [P3]. Supporters point to the certified compliance programme [R5]. Critics note that the reports are secret.
3. **Distributor margins: hard to police?** Industry practice relies on distributors in many markets. The record shows margins used as bribe funds [R1]. Some argue for stricter distributor controls. Others warn that controls which are too strict could disrupt drug supply in high-risk markets. **Open question:** no peer-reviewed evaluation specific to distributor-margin controls was found.
4. **Who should be compensated?** The money went to the U.S. Treasury and the SEC. The health systems of Russia, Ukraine and Mexico received nothing under these instruments [R1], [R3]. Mackey & Liang argue for global governance of health-sector corruption [P1].

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that Teva admitted bribing officials in Russia, Ukraine and Mexico, because it rests on a DPA with a statement of facts, a subsidiary guilty plea and an SEC judgment. It accepts that the harm to patients and health budgets was not measured in any record opened, so it makes no claim about that harm, and it treats the completed monitorship as showing that terms were met, not that the risk has gone.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Disclose distributor margins in public drug tenders.** Public buyers should require bidders to disclose distributor margins and the distributors' beneficial owners, and treat unexplained margin increases as red flags. *Basis:* the admitted margin-funded schemes in Russia and Mexico [R1, ¶¶16, 82]; the Russian distributor was owned by the official [R1]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
2. **Make regulators' outside paid roles unlawful and visible.** Officials who decide product registrations should be barred from paid consultancies with applicants, and their declarations should be public. *Basis:* the Ukraine "registration consultant" arrangement [R1, ¶58]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
3. **Publish monitor summaries.** At the end of a monitorship, a summary of the monitor's findings should be made public. *Basis:* the secrecy that limits evaluation (§5, §7.2); Arlen [P3]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
4. **Restitution to health budgets.** Part of the recovered sums should go, under independent oversight, to health budgets in the affected countries. *Basis:* §7.4; Mackey & Liang [P1]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
5. **Raise detection through peer monitoring.** *Basis:* Jensen & Malesky [P4]; Cheung et al. [P2]. *Evidence rating: moderate. Jensen and Malesky (2018), a survey experiment, support peer review under the OECD Convention; the specific proposal is untested.*

## 10. Recommendation (normative)

Use Teva as the case study for **sector-specific bribery mechanics**: distributor margins, registration consultancies and payments for prescriptions. It shows how bribery can hide inside ordinary commercial terms.
