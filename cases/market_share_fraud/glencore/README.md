# Glencore (2022): coordinated US, UK and Brazilian bribery resolutions, and the 2024 Swiss order

Date: 2026-11-19

**How to read the figures.** Every money amount or count below that comes from a government agency or court is marked **[⚑ agency figure]**. That mark means: this is an agency-reported figure; agencies have an incentive to report headline totals; credits and offsets between jurisdictions can mean double-counting; and what was found depends on what investigators looked for (detection bias), so it is not a measure of all the bribery that happened.

## 1. Summary

- **What was found.**
  - **US:** on 24 May 2022, Glencore International A.G. pleaded guilty in the US District Court for the Southern District of New York to conspiracy to violate the FCPA. A separate group company, Glencore Ltd., pleaded guilty in Connecticut to commodity price manipulation (not bribery).
  - **UK:** Glencore Energy UK Ltd pleaded guilty at Southwark Crown Court to five counts of bribery and two counts of failure to prevent bribery (Bribery Act 2010). It was sentenced on 3 November 2022 to pay £280,965,092.95 **[⚑ agency figure]**.
  - **Brazil:** Glencore reached a resolution with Brazil's Federal Prosecution Service (MPF).
  - **Switzerland:** on 5 August 2024, the Office of the Attorney General (OAG) issued a final summary penalty order holding Glencore International AG liable for failing to prevent bribery by a business partner in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC).

  These are **adjudicated** findings: guilty pleas, a UK sentence and a final Swiss penalty order.
- **Scale.** The DOJ says Glencore paid more than $100 million to intermediaries over more than a decade, intending a significant portion to be used as bribes in seven countries **[⚑ agency figure]**. The UK court found agreed harm of £81,034,197 across seven African countries **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Why it matters.** The record shows company cash desks in Switzerland paying out large sums under false descriptions such as "office expenses", and cash carried by private jet. This is bribery to win crude-oil cargoes and favourable prices from state oil companies.

**Countries and contracts named in the record:**
- **US plea** (DOJ):
  - **West Africa:** about $79.6m paid to intermediaries (about 2007–2018) for business with state-owned entities in Nigeria, Cameroon, Ivory Coast and Equatorial Guinea. **Nigeria** alone accounts for more than $52m, linked to crude-oil and refined-product contracts with the state oil company.
  - **DRC:** about $27.5m to third parties, intended partly as bribes.
  - **Brazil:** about $147,202.
  - **Venezuela:** more than $1.2m through an intermediary for an official.

  All figures **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **UK sentence** (agreed harm by count; **[⚑ agency figure]**):
  - Nigeria £12,111,889
  - Ghana £3,905,199
  - Malawi £279,487
  - Cameroon £22,752,498
  - Ivory Coast £27,728,459
  - Equatorial Guinea £12,943,712
  - South Sudan £1,312,952

  The Cameroon count involved bribes to officials at the national oil and gas company and the national refinery (Sonara) to secure favourable crude-oil sales. Its total exceeds €10m. The Equatorial Guinea count concerned a $1m payment disguised as a loan. The South Sudan count concerned $800,000 in cash flown in by private jet in August 2011, after which a joint venture in which Glencore had an interest received a contract for two million barrels of crude.
- **Switzerland** (OAG): bribery by a business partner in 2011 connected with his acquisition, below value, of minority stakes in two DRC mining companies from the state-owned mining company. Other DRC facts from 2007 to 2017 were **abandoned** by the OAG, so they are not findings.

No individual is named in this case. The UK judge made "no findings of any kind against any specific individual", and the court placed reporting restrictions on some names. The DOJ notes a guilty plea in 2021 by a former employee; this case does not identify that person. Other individuals have been charged or investigated without conviction, so none is named (results not verified).

- About 2007–2018: the conduct period in the US record (DOJ).
- Aug 2011: cash is flown to South Sudan (UK sentencing remarks).
- Mar 2012 to Feb 2015: almost monthly cash withdrawals, usually above €200,000, from the Baar (Switzerland) cash desk for Cameroon (UK sentencing remarks).
- 5 Dec 2019: the SFO announces its case (SFO case page).
- Jun 2020: the Swiss OAG opens a criminal investigation (OAG).
- Jul 2021: a former employee pleads guilty in the US (DOJ).

- 21 Jun 2022: UK guilty pleas. 3 Nov 2022: UK sentence.
- 11 Nov 2022: the US government's sentencing memorandum asks the court to impose the agreed amounts, over $700m in total (memorandum). The sentencing date is **UNVERIFIED**.
- May 2023: the Swiss partial abandonment with compensation (OAG).
- 5 Aug 2024: the Swiss summary penalty order becomes final (OAG).

## 4. How the scheme worked (as found in the record)

- **Intermediaries** paid "service fees" under sham consulting agreements and inflated invoices, with the money passed on to officials (DOJ; UK remarks).
- **Cash desks.** Large cash withdrawals from the Swiss office were described as office expenses, office-opening costs or entertainment. One March 2012 withdrawal was labelled "light crude oil – Cameroon entertainment". The cash was carried to West Africa, sometimes by private jet (UK remarks).
- **Disguised loans.** A $1m payment was recorded as a loan and partly used for bribes (Equatorial Guinea) (UK remarks).
- **Trading advantage.** Bribes secured crude-oil cargoes, favourable prices and allocations from state oil companies (UK remarks; DOJ).
- **Weak cooperation.** The DOJ says Glencore was "delayed in producing relevant evidence", did not consistently show full cooperation, and did not discipline some employees promptly, so it received reduced credit.

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **Monitors.** Two independent compliance monitors, each for three years, under the FCPA plea and the manipulation plea (DOJ). The monitor appointment dates were not found in any record opened (**UNVERIFIED**). Reports are not public.
- **Credits and offsets.** The company's own table shows how amounts were credited between the DOJ, the CFTC, the SFO and Switzerland. For example, the CFTC's $1.186bn was mostly offset, leaving $333,548,040 payable to the CFTC (company statement). This makes simple adding-up of headlines misleading.
- **Switzerland and the Netherlands.** The OAG coordinated with the Netherlands Public Prosecution Service. The result of the Netherlands case was not verified here (not verified; see *Unverified or uncertain items*).
- **Harm hard to measure.** The UK judge noted that losses to particular state entities "are difficult to quantify", so the agreed harm figures are calculations made for sentencing, not measured losses.
- **Breaches.** None found in the records opened.

## 6. Evidence strength

- **Strong** for the UK conviction and sentence. Public sentencing remarks were opened, with count-by-count figures.
- **Strong** for the US FCPA plea and the CFTC order. The DOJ press release, the government's sentencing memorandum and the CFTC release were opened. The plea agreement itself was not separately opened.
- **Strong** for the Swiss order, from the OAG press release. The order text is available only on request and was not opened.
- **Weak (company-reported)** for the Brazilian amount (UNVERIFIED primary).
- **Measurement caution.** The SFO case page describes bribes "worth over $25 million" **[⚑ agency figure]**, while the court's agreed harm figure is £81,034,197 and the US figure is more than $100 million in payments to intermediaries. The figures measure different things, so they should not be compared directly.

## 7. Fair counterarguments

- **Settlement versus trial.** Unlike Airbus, the UK side here was a conviction, not a DPA. One view is that the SFO chose prosecution because cooperation was imperfect. Søreide and Vagle (2022) argue that settlement-heavy enforcement under-deters, which supports convictions in serious cases. Hock and Dávid-Barrett (2022) show how compliance signalling shapes leniency.
- **Commodity trading as a corruption risk.** Marcolongo and Zambiasi (2024) find that new shell-company incorporations rise by about 11% around oil exploration licence awards, which indicates heightened corruption risk. Tacconi and Williams (2020) review evidence that corruption in extractive sectors is often systemic, and argue that collective-action approaches fit better than principal-agent ones where "the principal is corrupt". An OECD development paper (Porter and Anderson 2021; intergovernmental, flagged) says losses from illicit flows in oil trade are hard to measure (the numbers are rubbery).
- **Who gets the money?** The penalties went to the US, UK, Brazil and Switzerland. The countries whose officials were bribed (Nigeria, Cameroon and others) are not shown as recipients in the records opened. Bu (2022) discusses the sovereignty tensions in multijurisdictional enforcement.
- **Double-counting.** The DOJ credited nearly $256m to other authorities **[⚑ agency figure]**, and the CFTC offset about $853m (company table). Headline totals of "$1.1 billion" or "$1.5 billion" combine bribery and manipulation and overlap.
- **No Glencore-specific peer-reviewed study** was found in the peer-reviewed literature searched.

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that Glencore companies bribed officials to win oil cargoes and prices, because it rests on guilty pleas in the US and the UK and a final Swiss order. It accepts two limits. The UK harm figures are sentencing calculations, not measured losses, as the judge said. And the headline totals mix bribery with market manipulation and overlap through credits, so the case does not add them together.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Ban cash-desk disbursements for trading activity.** The UK record shows monthly six-figure cash withdrawals under false labels over three years. Companies trading with state-owned entities should be barred from cash disbursement above small limits, and auditors should test cash-desk use as a matter of course. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Derived from the UK record; not evaluated.*
2. **Make intermediary payments in oil trading visible.** Given the evidence linking licence awards to offshore incorporations (Marcolongo and Zambiasi 2024), regulators should require disclosure of the beneficial owners of intermediaries paid in sales of oil by state companies (compare the OECD proposals, Porter and Anderson 2021, flagged). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Marcolongo and Zambiasi (2024) give moderate observational evidence of the risk; the disclosure remedy has not been evaluated.*
3. **Compensate the countries whose officials were bribed.** Settlements should include a public mechanism for the affected states and their citizens, with safeguards against the funds being captured by the same officials (Tacconi and Williams 2020 on systemic corruption). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
4. **Prosecute rather than settle when cooperation is partial.** The DOJ record of delayed evidence supports using convictions rather than DPAs in such cases (Søreide and Vagle 2022). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Rests on a theoretical model (Søreide and Vagle 2022).*
5. **Publish one net global figure.** Agencies should state the amount each actually keeps after credits, to end the double-counting of headlines (Bu 2022). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). A reporting reform; not evaluated.*

## 11. What this does not replace

This case is not a ruling on any individual trader or official; the UK judge made no findings against named individuals. It does not replace the sentencing remarks, the US plea or the Swiss order. It is not legal advice on the Bribery Act 2010, the FCPA or Swiss corporate criminal law, and its harm figures are not estimates of what Nigeria, Cameroon or the other countries lost.
