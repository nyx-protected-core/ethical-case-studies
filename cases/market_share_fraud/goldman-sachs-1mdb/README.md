# Goldman Sachs / 1MDB (2020): U.S. deferred prosecution agreement, Malaysian subsidiary guilty plea and multi-regulator sanctions for bribes to win bond-underwriting business

Date: 2026-11-19

**Key to flags.** ⚑ marks a figure published by a government, court or regulator. These are negotiated penalties and agreed figures, not measures of losses to the Malaysian public. Agencies have reasons to present resolutions as historic, and the figures cover only detected conduct (detection bias). The Malaysian government's statements about its own settlement are political as well as legal communications.

---

## 1. Summary

On 22 October 2020, **The Goldman Sachs Group, Inc.** entered a **deferred prosecution agreement (DPA)** with the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) in the Eastern District of New York, on a charge of conspiracy to violate the FCPA anti-bribery provisions. Its subsidiary **Goldman Sachs (Malaysia) Sdn. Bhd.** **pleaded guilty** to the same conspiracy [R1], [R2], [R3].

According to the agreed Statement of Facts, Goldman, acting through senior bankers and an intermediary, conspired to provide **about $1.6077 billion ⚑** to, or for the benefit of, officials in Malaysia and Abu Dhabi between 2009 and 2014. In exchange, Goldman won roles on **three bond offerings that raised about $6.5 billion ⚑** for 1Malaysia Development Berhad (1MDB), a Malaysian state investment fund, in 2012–2013. These earned Goldman **more than $600 million ⚑** in fees and revenue [R1].

The agreed criminal penalty was **$2,315,088,000 ⚑**, plus **$606 million ⚑** in disgorgement [R1]. The SEC imposed a **$400 million ⚑** civil penalty and $606.3 million disgorgement, the latter satisfied by payments to Malaysia [R4], [R5]. The U.S. information against the parent company was **dismissed with prejudice on 6 May 2024** after the DPA term [R6].

Two former Goldman employees were prosecuted individually in the United States: one pleaded guilty and one was convicted at trial [R7]. They are not identified here.

Separately, the **Government of Malaysia settled** with Goldman in July 2020 for $2.5 billion in cash plus a guarantee of at least $1.4 billion in asset recoveries ⚑, and **withdrew** the Malaysian criminal charges [R9], [R10]. The Malaysian part is therefore a **settlement, not an adjudication**.

**Evidence strength: strong** for the U.S. instruments and the SEC order. The Malaysian amounts come from official and company statements.

**Countries, entities and contracts.** The officials were in **Malaysia** (1MDB and the Ministry of Finance) and **Abu Dhabi** (IPIC and Aabar). The business won consisted of three 1MDB bond offerings (about $6.5bn, 2012–2013), advisory roles on energy acquisitions, and a hoped-for IPO of 1MDB's energy assets [R1, ¶¶23–24]. The DPA identifies the officials by number only. The intermediary is named in the DOJ record and has **not** been convicted; this case names no individuals.

## 4. How the scheme worked, as found in the record

- **An intermediary with political access.** Senior bankers "used [the intermediary's] connections within the Governments of Malaysia and Abu Dhabi" to obtain business, and "concealed [the intermediary's] involvement in the deals from certain employees and agents of Goldman" [R1, ¶23].
- **Bond proceeds as a bribe source.** The SEC found that bribes and kickbacks were paid "using, in part, misappropriated and embezzled proceeds from the Bond Deals" [R4]. The DPA refers to "funds misappropriated from the bond offerings" [R1]. The individual case involved laundering billions "embezzled" from 1MDB [R7].
- **Control failure.** The SEC's announcement stressed that companies need controls "tailored to the risks presented by persons employed at all levels" [R5]. Compliance staff were party to recorded calls discussing bribery allegations, according to the DPA [R1, ¶4(c)].
- **Fees versus bribes.** Bribes conspired (about $1.6bn) were more than twice the fees earned (over $600m) [R1, ¶¶23–24]. Part of the bribe money came from the client's own misappropriated bond proceeds, not from Goldman [R4].

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **No monitor.** The DPA required annual reporting instead [R1].
- **Malaysian settlement.** $2.5bn in cash plus a guarantee of at least $1.4bn in recoveries from assets traceable to the diverted bond proceeds. Goldman also agreed to appoint an asset-recovery specialist at its own cost. Malaysia's Finance Ministry said the settlement represented "assets that rightfully belonged to the Malaysian people" [R9]. Goldman said Malaysia agreed to withdraw the criminal charges and bring no further charges against the firm and its personnel, excluding the two former bankers [R10].
- **Political context, peer-reviewed.** Case (2017) analyses the 1MDB scandal as a stress test of Malaysian leadership. He shows how institutions that historically stabilised Malaysian politics, including a dominant party, a fused party-state and a controlled legislature, were first turned against and then brought back under the leadership's control [P1].

## 7. Fair counterarguments

1. **Settlement versus prosecution in the victim state.** Malaysia's Finance Ministry welcomed resolving the matter "outside the court system" to speed up recovery [R9]. Others argue that withdrawing charges against a global bank lets the bank avoid a court finding where the harm happened. Lord (2022) argues that non-trial resolutions can construct corporations as "exempt, not just deferred" [P2].
2. **Was the penalty proportionate?** The DOJ's headline (about $2.9bn ⚑, combining the criminal penalty and disgorgement across US, UK, Singapore, Hong Kong and Malaysian payments; only $1.263bn ⚑ was payable to the US after credits) far exceeds the fees (over $600m) [R1]. Cheung, Rau & Stouraitis (2021) find that firm benefits from bribery scale with bribe size [P3]. Since Goldman's benefit was fees, not the bribe pool, the penalty clearly exceeds the gain. Its deterrent effect on other banks has not been measured. **GAP.**
3. **Crediting across nine authorities.** The DOJ credited payments to eight other regulators [R1, ¶9]. Bu (2022) argues for a coherent global settlement regime [P4]. Supporters of crediting say it prevents piling on. Critics say it lets headline numbers overstate total deterrence.
4. **Individual accountability.** Here two former employees were prosecuted and one was convicted at trial [R7]. Garrett (2017) describes the spread of corporate prosecution worldwide [P5], and this case shows corporate and individual cases running in parallel. The intermediary has not been convicted. **Open question:** status not verified from a primary record.
5. **Professional enablers.** Cooley & Sharman (2017) argue that professionals in financial centres lower the costs of transnational corruption by senior foreign officials [P6]. In this case the enabler was a global investment bank's own staff [R1].

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that Goldman admitted conspiring to violate the FCPA, because it rests on a DPA with a statement of facts, a subsidiary guilty plea and an SEC order. It reports Malaysia's choice to settle outside court in Malaysia's own words, and it does not treat the headline totals as net amounts, because the DOJ credited payments to other authorities.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Independent review of underwriting for state funds.** Bond issues by sovereign or state investment funds above a set size should require independent disclosure of every intermediary and fee, and an issuer-side review of use of proceeds. *Basis:* the intermediary-driven scheme and bond-funded bribes [R1], [R4]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
2. **Recordings as a compliance tool, with consequences.** Recorded calls discussing bribery allegations should trigger mandatory escalation. Delay in producing them should reduce cooperation credit, as it did here. *Basis:* [R1, ¶4(c)]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
3. **Victim-state courts first.** Where a state fund is the victim, settlements should be reviewed by a court in that state, so that withdrawn charges come with a public record. *Basis:* §7.1; Lord [P2]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
4. **Publish one consolidated crediting table across all authorities.** *Basis:* [R1, ¶9]; Bu [P4]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*
5. **Enabler liability.** Strengthen due-diligence duties for banks and professionals who handle state-fund money. *Basis:* Cooley & Sharman [P6]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Not evaluated.*

## 10. Recommendation (normative)

Use this case to show **bribery inside capital markets**: the "contract" won was an underwriting mandate, and part of the bribe money came from the client's own misappropriated bond proceeds [R4]. Keep the Malaysian settlement clearly separate from the U.S. adjudications.
