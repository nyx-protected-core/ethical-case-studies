# Siemens AG (2008): coordinated US and German bribery resolutions

Date: 2026-11-19

**How to read the figures.** Every money amount or count below that comes from a government agency or court is marked **[⚑ agency figure]**. That mark means: this is an agency-reported figure; agencies have an incentive to report headline totals; credits and offsets between jurisdictions can mean double-counting; and what was found depends on what investigators looked for (detection bias), so it is not a measure of all the bribery that happened.

## 1. Summary

- **What was found.** On 15 December 2008, Siemens AG pleaded guilty in US federal court to criminal violations of the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) internal-controls and books-and-records provisions, and three subsidiaries (Argentina, Bangladesh, Venezuela) pleaded guilty to conspiracy counts. The same day it settled US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) charges and accepted a fine from the Munich public prosecutor for failing to supervise. The guilty pleas are **adjudicated and admitted** findings. The SEC judgment is a consented settlement in which Siemens neither admitted nor denied the allegations, and the Munich dispositions are corporate fines reported by the DOJ and SEC.
- **Scale in the record.** The SEC complaint (as summarised by the SEC) describes at least 4,283 payments totalling about $1.4 billion used to bribe government officials between March 2001 and September 2007 **[⚑ agency figure]**. Combined US and Munich payments announced that day were about $1.6 billion **[⚑ agency figure]**, plus an earlier €201 million German payment in 2007 **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Why it matters.** At the time, the US agencies described it as the largest amount ever paid to resolve corruption charges. The record shows bribery that was paid for and booked through the company's own systems (cash desks, slush funds, sham consultant contracts), not just individual misconduct.

**Countries and contracts named in the record (as quoted in the DOJ and SEC releases):**
- **Argentina:** about $31.26m in corrupt payments (March 2001 to about January 2007) tied to a $1 billion national identity card project **[⚑ agency figure]** (DOJ).

- **Bangladesh:** at least $5.32m (May 2001 to August 2006) for favourable treatment in bidding on a mobile telephone project **[⚑ agency figure]** (DOJ).
- **Iraq (UN Oil-for-Food Programme):** four subsidiaries won 42 contracts worth more than $80m. They paid at least $1,736,076 in kickbacks and earned more than $38m in profit on those contracts **[⚑ agency figure]** (DOJ).

## 3. Timeline (news-sourced items are labelled SECONDARY)

- 1999: Germany adopts the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention. The SEC says the Managing Board was ineffective from 1999 to 2003 in putting controls in place in response (SEC Litigation Release 20829).
- 12 Mar 2001: Siemens is listed on the New York Stock Exchange, which brings it under FCPA issuer provisions (DOJ, SEC).
- Nov 2006: new management begins reforms to internal controls (SEC Litigation Release 20829). All but $27.5m of the corrupt payments came before 15 November 2006 **[⚑ agency figure]** (SEC Litigation Release 20829). The DOJ says Siemens disclosed potential FCPA violations after the Munich prosecutor searched Siemens offices and employees' homes. The date of the searches is not stated in the sources opened.
- Oct 2007: Munich disposition for the Telecommunications group, €201m (DOJ).
- 12 to 15 Dec 2008: US informations and the SEC complaint are filed, the pleas are entered, and the Munich €395m disposition is announced (DOJ; SEC; company statement).
- 2 Jul 2009: World Bank Group settlement (World Bank).
- About 2012: the four-year monitorship would have ended; no DOJ or SEC record of its end was opened (**UNVERIFIED**).

## 4. How the scheme worked (as found in the record)

From the DOJ press release (facts admitted in the guilty pleas) and the SEC litigation release (SEC allegations; Siemens settled without admitting or denying them):
- **Cash desks and suitcases.** Employees drew large amounts of cash from company cash desks, sometimes carried in suitcases across borders to pay bribes.
- **Slush funds and off-book accounts** were held at unconsolidated entities.
- **Payments to purported business consultants** (DOJ). The SEC alleges sham contracts with false invoices for services "never intended to be rendered", with payments booked as management fees, consulting fees, supply contracts, "room preparation fees" and commissions.
- **Deliberate destruction of approval trails (SEC allegation).** Payment authorisations were written on removable sticky notes, which were later removed "to eradicate any permanent record".
- **Inflated UN contracts.** Prices were raised by about 10% and undisclosed side agreements were made with Iraqi ministries to fund kickbacks.
- **Tone at the top (SEC allegation).** The SEC alleges that, despite knowing about bribery in its two largest groups (Communications and Power Generation), the company's leadership created "a corporate culture in which bribery was tolerated and even rewarded at the highest levels".

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **Monitor.** The US plea and settlement required an independent compliance monitor for a four-year period (DOJ press release; SEC Litigation Release 20829). Siemens' own statement of 15 December 2008 confirms that a monitor was engaged (company statement). The end date of the monitorship is UNVERIFIED (see timeline).
- **Remediation credited by DOJ.** The DOJ cited an internal investigation "of unprecedented scope", an employee amnesty programme, disciplinary action against senior management and a complete restructuring of the company.
- **Debarment risk.** The World Bank settlement included a debarment of the Russian subsidiary of up to four years and a voluntary two-year abstention for the group **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Breaches.** No breach of the US plea or SEC judgment was found in the sources opened.

## 6. Evidence strength

- **Strong** for the existence, dates and amounts of the resolutions: guilty pleas and a consented judgment in US federal court, all documented on DOJ and SEC sites opened for this case.
- **Strong but admitted rather than tried** for the description of the scheme. The facts come from statements of offense and a settled SEC complaint that the company did not contest. No trial tested them.
- **Moderate** for the German side. The Munich amounts are reported by the DOJ, the SEC and the company, but the Munich orders themselves were not opened (UNVERIFIED).
- **Weak or unknown** for the true total of bribes. All payment counts are what investigators identified (detection bias), and $554.5m was paid "for unknown purposes" according to the DOJ **[⚑ agency figure]**.

## 7. Fair counterarguments

- **Was the penalty a deterrent or a cost of business?** The SEC alleges that Siemens earned over $1.1 billion in profits on the bribed transactions **[⚑ agency figure]**, against roughly $1.6 billion in combined 2008 payments **[⚑ agency figure]**. Søreide and Vagle (2022) argue that settlement-based enforcement in general leads to under-deterrence and less public information about the wrongdoing. Bu (2021) argues that negotiated resolutions can still reshape compliance.
- **Corporate fine versus individual accountability.** The company paid. Individual cases ran separately in Germany and the US, and their results were not verified here. Commentators disagree about whether corporate-level resolutions displace prosecutions of individuals; De Vita and Vozza (2024) argue that negotiated corporate resolutions offer "minimal retribution".
- **Rules versus culture after the scandal.** Eberl, Geiger and Aßländer (2015) find that tighter formal rules at Siemens signalled trustworthiness to outside stakeholders but caused dissatisfaction among employees, because the rules were hard to apply in practice. Berghoff (2017) frames the case as a question of why corruption took place at such scale, and places it within the wider compliance revolution of the 2000s.
- **The national enforcement gap.** Lord (2013) compares how the UK and Germany regulate transnational corporate bribery. The Siemens case is often cited as German enforcement acting alongside US enforcement rather than being replaced by it.
- **Double-counting.** The $1.6 billion headline combines US and German amounts **[⚑ agency figure]**. The SEC itself notes that its disgorgement figure "does not include profits factored into Munich's fine", which shows that the agencies coordinated to avoid charging twice for the same profits. Readers should still not add the 2007 €201m, the World Bank $100m and later national settlements to the headline without checking for overlap.

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion (bribery was organised through Siemens' own payment systems) because that rests on the company's guilty pleas, not on agency rhetoric. It accepts the deterrence objection as open: the SEC's profit figure and the combined payments are of similar size, so whether the penalty exceeded the gain cannot be settled from these records. The German side is treated as weaker evidence because the Munich orders were not opened.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

These are proposals, not findings. Each one is linked to its source.
1. **Price sanctions above the gain.** Where an agency's own record puts profits from bribery near the size of the penalty (SEC allegation: over $1.1bn in profits), penalties should clearly exceed the gain. Søreide and Vagle (2022) argue that settlement discounts that grow too large lead to under-deterrence. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Søreide and Vagle (2022) is a theoretical model; no gain-plus penalty rule has been evaluated.*
2. **Ban off-book payment channels by design.** Cash desks, sticky-note approvals and unconsolidated slush funds were central to this record (DOJ, SEC). Boards should require consolidated, auditable payment channels and test them independently. The record shows that unrecorded authorisation was the mechanism. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Derived from the case record; no evaluation of a ban on cash desks and off-book funds was found.*
3. **Pair formal rules with informal culture work.** Eberl et al. (2015) suggest managing the interplay between formal and informal rules, because tighter rules alone created employee dissatisfaction and implementation gaps. *Evidence rating: weak. Eberl, Geiger and Aßländer (2015) is a single-company qualitative study.*
4. **Publish monitor conclusions.** Monitor reports in this case are not public, and no official record of the monitor's conclusions was found. Publishing summary monitor findings would let the public check whether remediation worked. This is a normative view consistent with the concerns about lost information raised by Søreide and Vagle (2022). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative; no study of published monitor summaries was found.*
5. **Report penalties net of credits.** Agencies should publish one net, de-duplicated figure across jurisdictions so that headline totals are not added together twice. This responds to the double-counting risk discussed by Bu (2022, *International Annals of Criminology*, DOI 10.1017/cri.2022.18). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). A reporting reform; its effect has not been measured.*

## 11. What this does not replace

This case is not a compliance audit of Siemens today, and it does not say anything about current Siemens employees. It is not legal advice on the FCPA or German corporate-fine law. It does not replace the court files (plea agreements, statements of offense and the Munich orders) for anyone who needs the authoritative wording, and the dollar figures in it are not a measure of the harm done in Argentina, Venezuela, Bangladesh, Iraq or the other countries named.
