# Odebrecht S.A. and Braskem S.A. (2016): coordinated US, Brazilian and Swiss resolutions

Date: 2026-11-19

**How to read the figures.** Every money amount or count below that comes from a government agency or court is marked **[⚑ agency figure]**. That mark means: this is an agency-reported figure; agencies have an incentive to report headline totals; credits and offsets between jurisdictions can mean double-counting; and what was found depends on what investigators looked for (detection bias), so it is not a measure of all the bribery that happened.

## 1. Summary

- **What was found.** On 21 December 2016, the Brazilian construction conglomerate Odebrecht S.A. and its petrochemical affiliate Braskem S.A. each pleaded guilty in the US District Court for the Eastern District of New York to conspiracy to violate the anti-bribery provisions of the FCPA. Braskem also settled with the SEC. The same day, Switzerland's Office of the Attorney General (OAG) convicted Odebrecht S.A. and its subsidiary Construtora Norberto Odebrecht (CNO) by summary penalty order, and both companies settled with Brazil's Federal Prosecution Service (MPF). These are **adjudicated** findings (US guilty pleas and judgment; Swiss penalty order).
- **Scale.** Odebrecht admitted paying about $788 million in bribes in a number of countries **[⚑ agency figure]**. The DOJ called the resolutions the largest global foreign-bribery resolution to that date, at least $3.5 billion combined **[⚑ agency figure]**. Odebrecht was sentenced on 17 April 2017 to a $2.6 billion global criminal fine **[⚑ agency figure]**.
- **Important later development.** In September 2023, a justice of Brazil's Supreme Federal Court (STF) declared unusable, in any Brazilian proceeding, all evidence taken from Odebrecht's internal "Drousys" and "My Web Day B" systems that had been obtained through the leniency agreement. This decision affects Brazilian cases that rely on that evidence. It does not undo the US guilty pleas or judgment, or the Swiss penalty order, as recorded in the sources opened here.

**Countries and contracts named in the record** (Odebrecht plea, statement of facts; all **[⚑ agency figure]**):
- **Brazil:** about $349m in bribes (2003 to about 2016) to political parties, officials and their representatives. Benefit more than $1.9bn. Includes payments to obtain and keep business with Petrobras (about 2004–2012).
- **Angola:** more than $50m (2006–2013) for public works contracts. Benefit about $261.7m.
- **Argentina:** more than $35m (2007–2014), paid to intermediaries to be passed in part to officials.
- **Colombia:** more than $11m for public works contracts. Benefit more than $50m.
- **Dominican Republic:** more than $92m (2001–2014). Benefit more than $163m.
- **Ecuador:** more than $33.5m. Benefit more than $116m.
- **Guatemala:** about $18m. Benefit more than $34m.
- **Mexico:** about $10.5m. Benefit more than $39m.
- **Mozambique:** about $900,000.
- **Panama:** more than $59m (2010–2014). Benefit more than $175m.
- **Peru:** about $29m (2005–2014). Benefit more than $143m. One example is an infrastructure tender in about 2005, followed by about $20m in payments from 2005 to 2008.
- **Venezuela:** about $98m (2006–2015) for public works contracts.
- **Braskem** (DOJ): about $250m paid into Odebrecht's off-book system (2006–2014). Bribes went to Brazilian politicians and parties and to a Petrobras official. In return Braskem received preferential raw-material rates from Petrobras, contracts, and favourable legislation and tax programmes. Corrupt payments and/or profits totalled about $465m. The SEC states that Braskem made about $325m in profit through the bribes.

No individual is named in this case. A single justice of Brazil's highest court (the STF) has since declared key documentary evidence unusable in Brazilian proceedings (see sections 3 and 5). Findings against individuals in Brazil may therefore have been undone, so no person is named here. The DOJ case pages list related prosecutions of individuals, but their results were not checked (see *Unverified or uncertain items*).

## 3. Timeline (news-sourced items are labelled SECONDARY)

- 2001: the scheme begins at the latest (DOJ).
- 2006: the "Division of Structured Operations" is formalised as a stand-alone bribe department (DOJ).
- 2014: the Swiss OAG begins investigating the Petrobras affair; by December 2016 it was running about 60 criminal investigations (OAG press release). The date on which proceedings against the Odebrecht group opened is **UNVERIFIED**.
- 21 Dec 2016: the US pleas, the SEC settlement, the Swiss order and the Brazilian agreements, all coordinated (DOJ; SEC; OAG).
- 17 Apr 2017: Odebrecht is sentenced in E.D.N.Y. (judgment).
- 13 May 2020: Braskem discloses that the DOJ and SEC confirmed the end of its independent monitorship, after a final monitor report certifying implementation (Braskem SEC Form 6-K, a company statement).
- 6 Sep 2023: a single STF justice (the rapporteur) nullifies evidence taken from Odebrecht's Drousys and My Web Day B systems obtained through the leniency agreement, in any sphere or level of jurisdiction (STF news).

## 4. How the scheme worked (as found in the record)

From the DOJ press release and the plea's statement of facts:
- **A secret bribery department.** The "Division of Structured Operations" ran a "shadow" budget on a separate computer system. It used an off-book communications system with codenames and passwords. Until about 2009 it reported to the highest levels of the company for approvals.
- **Off-balance-sheet funding.** Funds were generated by the finance department and by subsidiaries, including Braskem, then sent to offshore entities not shown as related parties.
- **Delivery.** Money moved by wire through layers of offshore companies, and by cash delivered in packages or suitcases left at agreed locations, inside and outside Brazil.
- **Bid-rigging.** The DOJ describes a "bribery and bid-rigging scheme". Campos et al. (2021) find from judicial documents that bribes were used both to tailor auction terms and to win favourable contract renegotiations.

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **Monitors.** Both companies agreed to independent compliance monitors for three years (DOJ). Braskem reports that its US monitorship ended in May 2020 after the monitors certified its compliance programme, and that the MPF monitoring had ended in March 2020 (company statement). The end of Odebrecht's own monitorship was not checked (see *Unverified or uncertain items*).
- **Ability to pay.** Odebrecht's US fine was set below the agreed $4.5bn because it said it could pay only about $2.6bn over the life of the agreements **[⚑ agency figure]** (DOJ). This is an example of a sanction limited by the company's capacity to pay rather than its culpability.
- **Evidence annulment in Brazil.** The 2023 decision of a single STF justice ordered all material from the leniency agreement sent to the STF and an inquiry into how it had been obtained. It cited a Ministry of Justice report that no international legal-cooperation request had been found for receiving the system data (STF news). Brazilian cases that relied on that evidence may be archived by the natural judge of each case.
- **Breaches.** No breach of the US plea agreements was found in the sources opened.

## 6. Evidence strength

- **Strong** for the US resolutions: guilty pleas, a detailed statement of facts and a criminal judgment were all opened.
- **Strong** for the Swiss conviction, from the OAG press release. The penalty order itself was not opened.
- **Contested in Brazil.** A single justice of Brazil's highest court has ruled that key documentary evidence from Odebrecht's systems cannot be used in Brazilian proceedings. The US statement of facts was admitted by the company and is not undone by that ruling. Readers should still treat facts that depend only on that system data with care.
- **Uncertain** for the totals. Per-country bribe and benefit figures come from what the company admitted and investigators could trace (detection bias), and Campos et al. (2021) report that bribe sizes were smaller relative to project cost than many earlier studies suggested.

## 7. Fair counterarguments

- **Did Lava Jato strengthen or damage the rule of law?** Mészáros (2020) argues that Car Wash undermined due process and the presumption of innocence through media leaks and new legal tools, while appearing to strengthen the rule of law. The 2023 STF ruling on the Odebrecht systems evidence is part of this dispute. Other scholarship collected in Lagunes and Švejnar (2020) treats Lava Jato as exposing collusion in Brazil and 11 other countries, while noting the ambiguous role of the media.
- **Who should collect the money?** The US judgment sent about 96% of Odebrecht's fine to Brazil and Switzerland ($2.507bn of $2.6bn: $2.391bn and $116m) **[⚑ agency figure]**. Pimenta and Venturini (2021) show how investigative, sanction-based and settlement cooperation can reinforce or undermine one another. Their examples include later local agreements in the Dominican Republic and Peru, which raises the question of compensating the countries where the bribes were paid.
- **Why Brazil, and why not Odebrecht?** Bertran and Nasser (2022) argue that non-US companies and the oil and gas sector have been favoured targets of FCPA enforcement. They also argue that Odebrecht, unlike Petrobras, had weaker incentives to push for a cleaner environment after enforcement because of its fragile position.
- **Cost of corruption.** Campos et al. (2021) find average cost increases of 70.8% on projects where Odebrecht paid bribes, against 5.6% on projects without bribes. Bribes were in the range of 1–2% of project cost. This supports the view that the harm comes mainly through renegotiation, not only through the size of the bribes.
- **Ability-to-pay discounts.** Søreide and Vagle (2022) warn that negotiated sanctions can under-deter. Others treat ability-to-pay limits as necessary to avoid destroying employers and creditors (a point the DOJ release implicitly accepts).

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that Odebrecht and Braskem admitted large-scale bribery, because the US pleas, the E.D.N.Y. judgment and the Swiss penalty order do not depend on Brazilian courts. It takes the Lava Jato due-process critique seriously: the 2023 STF decision made key system evidence unusable in Brazil, so the case names no individual and warns readers about facts that rest only on that data. Whether US enforcement chose Brazilian targets selectively is left open.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Watch renegotiations, not only tenders.** Because Campos et al. (2021) find that bribed projects ran 70.8% over cost, mainly through renegotiation, public owners should require independent review and publication of every contract renegotiation above a threshold. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Campos et al. (2021) give moderate observational evidence of the harm (cost overruns); the proposed review has not been evaluated.*
2. **Compensate the harmed countries.** Pimenta and Venturini (2021) document how coordinated settlements allocated money between enforcing states. Settlements should include a transparent mechanism for affected countries such as Angola, Peru and Panama, which were not parties to the 2016 allocation. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative.*
3. **Protect evidence chains in cross-border cases.** The 2023 STF ruling turned on how system data was obtained and on the absence of a recorded legal-cooperation request (STF news). Agencies should use formal mutual legal assistance channels for documentary evidence so that it survives domestic review. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative.*
4. **Publish net and allocated amounts.** Report what each jurisdiction actually keeps after credits, as the E.D.N.Y. judgment does ($93m, $2.391bn, $116m), rather than gross headline totals (Bu 2022). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative.*
5. **Treat ability-to-pay reductions transparently.** Publish the method and conditions of any reduction, so that discounts do not become routine (Søreide and Vagle 2022). *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Normative.*

## 11. What this does not replace

This case is not a ruling on any Brazilian criminal case, and it must not be used to suggest guilt in a case where the STF has made evidence unusable. It does not replace the plea agreements, the statement of facts or the Swiss order. It is not legal advice on the FCPA, Swiss corporate criminal law or Brazilian leniency agreements, and its figures are not an estimate of losses to the public in the countries named.
