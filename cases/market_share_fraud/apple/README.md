# Apple as the infringer: an import ban over Masimo's patents, a paid VirnetX judgment, and contempt in the Epic case

Date: 2026-11-19

Three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Found**: what a court, jury, regulator or plea record decided or admitted. **Alleged**: what a prosecutor charged or a party claimed; an indictment or complaint is not a finding. **Reported**: facts known only from news coverage, labelled SECONDARY. ## The ethical question

Apple is often the party claiming that others copied it (see the Samsung case in this category). Here it is the defendant. Does a company with Apple's resources treat adverse findings as a cost to be managed, through redesign, delay and appeals, while keeping the market position the disputed features helped build? The records show three separate adjudicated findings against Apple. They also show that each was narrower than the headlines, and that one rested on patents the Patent Office later rejected.

The Epic matter is unfair competition, not intellectual property. It is included because it is an adjudicated finding about conduct aimed at protecting a market position, and because the contempt finding is a second, separate finding in the same dispute.

## Damages against market effect

- **VirnetX:** $454,033,859.87 paid, the largest sum in this case [5].
- **Masimo:** an import ban on the infringing models, not damages; Apple's redesigned products were later cleared [3].
- **Epic:** an injunction and contempt sanctions, no damages to Epic in the records opened [7][9].
- **Market effect.** No record opened measures sales or market share that Apple gained from the infringing features or the anti-steering rules. This case makes no estimate.

## Analysis

Three points stand out.

**Redesign limits what an exclusion order achieves.** The Masimo order was affirmed by the Federal Circuit (a certiorari petition is pending [20]), yet by the time it was affirmed the agency had already accepted that redesigned products fall outside it [2][3]. An exclusion order removes a specific infringing design, not the market position the product holds. That is how it is meant to work, but it means the order's deterrent force depends on how costly redesign is.

**Finality can outrun validity.** Most of the VirnetX claims the jury found infringed were later rejected by the Patent Office, and the Federal Circuit affirmed several of those rejections while Apple's appeal was pending; the court nonetheless left the judgment in place, and the trial court refused to reopen it because Apple waited too long [4]. The judgment is final and was paid. Whether it rewarded a valid invention is a separate question the record does not settle.

**Contempt is the clearest sign of strategy.** The Epic contempt finding is the only record in this case that speaks to intent: the district court and the Ninth Circuit found that Apple's compliance plan was designed to defeat the injunction and that Apple acted in bad faith [8][9]; the Supreme Court is reviewing the case [19]. That is a finding about one injunction, not about Apple's conduct in the patent matters.

Research on patent litigation is relevant in both directions. Suits by patent holders that do not make products reduce the targeted firms' innovation [10], and lowering the cost of patent litigation for defendants after a 2006 Supreme Court ruling was followed by more corporate research [11]. A large judgment is therefore not automatically a gain for innovation.

## Fair counterarguments

- **Legitimate protection versus litigation as a weapon.** Suing a rival over intellectual property is often legitimate protection of research. But litigation can also be used to raise a competitor's costs; research on suits by patent holders that do not make products found that targeted firms substantially reduce their innovative activity after settling or losing [10]. A finding in court shows that a line was crossed; it does not show that every claim the winning side made was right.
- **Learning is not taking.** The international standard protects undisclosed information only against acquisition "contrary to honest commercial practices" [12], which is narrower than all copying. Learning from public products and hiring experienced people are lawful and are how skills spread. This case concerns patents that a trade agency and a jury found infringed, and a separate finding of unfair competition and contempt; it involves no finding that Apple took confidential material, and patent infringement does not require copying.
- **The costs of litigation.** Long cases consume money and management attention on both sides, and delay can hurt the winner as much as the loser. Remedies that depend on ever-longer litigation are a weak answer to a problem of speed.
- **Apple won most of the Epic case.** Epic's federal antitrust claims failed at trial and on appeal, and Apple won its contract claim and, on appeal, its claim to attorney's fees [7].
- **The VirnetX patents fared badly at the Patent Office.** The jury's findings stand legally, but the later rejections of most of the asserted claims [4] support Apple's view that it paid for patents that should not have been granted.
- **Exclusion orders can hurt consumers.** An import ban on a widely used health feature affects buyers who did nothing wrong. The agency found that the public interest did not preclude the orders and exempted repairs and warranty replacements for earlier purchases [1].

## Proposed solutions

The proposals below are this case's own reasoning. Each carries an evidence rating for how well research supports the measure itself: **strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **UNTESTED** (no study cited here tests whether the measure works).

1. **Decide validity before large damages become final.** Where the patent office is reviewing the same claims, courts should, where practical, align timing so that a paid judgment does not rest on claims later found unpatentable [4]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. No study cited here measures the effect of such coordination.*
2. **Keep patent remedies proportionate.** Evidence that litigation pressure reduces research [10][11] argues against remedies that reward litigation itself. Remedies should track the value of the specific feature found to infringe. *Evidence rating: moderate. Two peer-reviewed empirical studies, both on US patent litigation.*
3. **Enforce injunctions quickly and in public.** The Epic record shows that an injunction can be undercut by how compliance is designed [8]. Early compliance reports to the court, with the other side able to respond, would surface this sooner. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. This is a design proposal; no study cited here tests it.*
4. **Certainty over severity.** General deterrence research finds that certainty of a sanction matters more than its size [13][14]. For a large firm, the reliable and timely enforcement of orders matters more than headline sums. *Evidence rating: moderate. Broad reviews; none tests patent or unfair-competition enforcement specifically.*

## What this does not replace

- **Legal advice or a legal opinion.** Nothing here says how any pending or future dispute should be decided.
- **The records themselves.** Summaries here are shortened. Where a figure or finding matters, read the cited decision or notice.
- **A view of Apple's present conduct.** Each finding concerns specific products, rules and dates. It is not evidence about Apple's present practices, and must not be used to judge individual employees.
- **Due diligence or an investment view.** This case is not a supplier-risk score or an investment recommendation.
