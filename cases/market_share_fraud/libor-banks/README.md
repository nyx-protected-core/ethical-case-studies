# The LIBOR Banks: Barclays, UBS, RBS and Deutsche Bank — Benchmark Manipulation, Findings, Harms and Reform

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Four banks are covered (Barclays, UBS, RBS and Deutsche Bank), each with findings against the institution itself: guilty pleas by subsidiaries, non-prosecution or deferred prosecution agreements with admitted statements of fact, CFTC orders, UK final notices, a Swiss disgorgement order and a European Commission cartel settlement. Overturned convictions of individual traders are reported as rulings about individuals, not about the banks. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies sum amounts across bodies.*

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers the manipulation of the London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR) and related interbank offered rates (EURIBOR, Euroyen TIBOR) by four panel banks: Barclays, UBS, The Royal Bank of Scotland (RBS) and Deutsche Bank. It covers the criminal, regulatory and competition-law outcomes against those four institutions between 2012 and 2015, the 2013 European Commission cartel decision on interest rate derivatives, later appellate rulings on individual prosecutions, and the reform that ended LIBOR.

- **Findings first.** The Findings section lists only adjudicated or admitted findings: guilty pleas, non-prosecution agreements (NPAs) and deferred prosecution agreements (DPAs) with admitted statements of fact, regulator orders and final notices, and a competition-authority settlement decision. Each is tied to its primary record. Allegations that were never adjudicated, or that were later overturned, are labelled as such.
- **Source tags.** **[DOJ]** US Department of Justice record; **[CFTC]** Commodity Futures Trading Commission order or release; **[UK-REG]** UK Financial Services Authority / Financial Conduct Authority final notice or statement; **[CH-REG]** Swiss regulator; **[EC]** European Commission decision or release; **[CT]** court judgment; **[LAW]** statute or regulation; **[STD]** official standard or reform body; **[PR]** peer-reviewed article. No news reports are used in this file.
- **Government figures are marked ⚑.** Penalty totals announced by agencies often add together amounts from several agencies, count credits or offsets, or describe a "global resolution" that no single body imposed. Leniency means the first bank to report a cartel can pay nothing, so a low or zero fine is not evidence of less wrongdoing. These figures measure enforcement outcomes, not the size of the harm.
- **People.** No individual is named. Traders, submitters and managers are described by role. Court decisions about individuals are cited by court and case number, not by the names in their captions.
- **Evidence strength.** Findings are rated **Strong**, **Moderate**, **Limited** or **Very limited** for how firmly the record supports them. Proposed solutions carry a separate evidence rating: strong, moderate, weak or UNTESTED.

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts, each with its primary record)

### 1.1 Barclays (June 2012)

- **US criminal:** Barclays Bank PLC entered a non-prosecution agreement with the US Department of Justice and agreed to pay a $160 million penalty ⚑ for providing false LIBOR and EURIBOR contributions; the agreement was conditioned on two years of ongoing obligations and did not cover individuals [1]. *Status: admitted (NPA with statement of facts).*
- **CFTC:** an order bringing and settling charges of attempted manipulation and false reporting, with a $200 million civil penalty ⚑ and required controls over benchmark submissions [2].
- **UK:** the Financial Services Authority imposed a penalty of £59.5 million ⚑ (reduced by settlement discount from £85 million) [3]. The final notice records two kinds of misconduct: between January 2005 and July 2008, US dollar LIBOR and EURIBOR submissions took account of requests from the bank's interest rate derivatives traders, sometimes on behalf of traders at other banks; and, during the financial crisis, submissions were lowered out of concern over "negative media" perception of the bank's funding [3].
- **Breach later found:** in May 2015 Barclays agreed that its foreign-exchange conduct violated the 2012 NPA and agreed to pay a further $60 million criminal penalty ⚑ [16].

*Strength: Strong (three regulators; admitted facts).*

### 1.2 UBS (December 2012)

- **US criminal:** UBS Securities Japan Co. Ltd. pleaded guilty to one count of wire fraud for manipulating Yen LIBOR; the District of Connecticut entered judgment and imposed a $100 million fine in September 2013 [4; 5]. UBS AG entered an NPA with a $400 million penalty ⚑ [4].
- **CFTC:** an order for manipulation, attempted manipulation and false reporting of LIBOR, EURIBOR, Euroyen TIBOR and other benchmarks "from at least 2005 to at least 2010", with a $700 million penalty ⚑ [6].
- **UK:** a £160 million penalty ⚑ [7]. The final notice records more than 800 documented internal requests from traders concerning JPY LIBOR during 1 January 2005 – 31 December 2010, more than 40 individuals directly involved, and collusion with brokers and other panel banks [7].
- **Switzerland:** FINMA found that UBS "severely violated" organisational and proper business conduct requirements and ordered disgorgement of CHF 59 million ⚑ [8].
- **NPA breach:** in May 2015 the Department of Justice found that UBS had breached the 2012 NPA through deceptive foreign-exchange practices; UBS agreed to plead guilty to the original LIBOR-related wire fraud charge and pay a $203 million criminal penalty ⚑ [16].
- The DOJ statement of facts records that a senior trader or a colleague tried to move Yen LIBOR on at least 335 of 738 trading days between November 2006 and August 2009 [4].

*Strength: Strong.*

### 1.3 RBS (February 2013)

- **US criminal:** RBS Securities Japan Limited agreed to plead guilty to wire fraud for manipulating Yen LIBOR and pay a $50 million fine ⚑; the parent, RBS plc, entered a DPA on charges of wire fraud and a Sherman Act price-fixing conspiracy (rigging Yen LIBOR with other banks), with a further $100 million penalty ⚑ [9].
- **CFTC:** a $325 million penalty ⚑ for manipulation, attempted manipulation and false reporting of Yen and Swiss franc LIBOR [10].
- **UK:** an £87.5 million penalty ⚑ [11]. The final notice covers January 2006 – March 2012, records at least 21 individuals involved, and cites at least 96 written requests from derivatives traders to primary submitters on JPY and CHF LIBOR between December 2008 and November 2010 [11].

*Strength: Strong.*

### 1.4 Deutsche Bank (April 2015)

- **US criminal:** DB Group Services (UK) Limited pleaded guilty to wire fraud and agreed to a $150 million fine ⚑; Deutsche Bank AG entered a DPA resolving wire fraud and Sherman Act charges (rigging Yen LIBOR with other banks), with a further $625 million penalty ⚑ and a three-year corporate monitor [12]. The admitted conduct ran "from at least 2003 through early 2011" [12].
- **CFTC:** an $800 million penalty ⚑ for manipulation, attempted manipulation and false reporting of LIBOR (US dollar, yen, sterling and Swiss franc) and EURIBOR [13].
- **UK:** a £226.8 million penalty ⚑ (before discount, £324 million) [14]. Beyond the manipulation itself, the final notice finds that the bank gave the regulator "false, inaccurate or misleading" information, including a formal attestation about its systems and controls that "was known to be false" by the person who drafted it [14].
- The DOJ release also records a $600 million New York Department of Financial Services penalty ⚑ and a headline total of about $2.519 billion ⚑ [12].

*Strength: Strong.*

### 1.5 European Commission cartel decision (December 2013)

In settlement decisions, the Commission fined eight institutions a total of €1,494,302,000 ⚑ (amended figure) for cartels in euro interest rate derivatives (EIRD, September 2005 – May 2008) and yen interest rate derivatives (YIRD) [15]. Relevant to the four banks:

- **EIRD:** Barclays received full immunity and avoided a fine of about €690 million ⚑; Deutsche Bank was fined €465,861,000; RBS €131,004,000 [15].
- **YIRD:** UBS received full immunity for five infringements and avoided about €2.5 billion ⚑; RBS was fined €260,056,000 and Deutsche Bank €259,499,000 [15].
- The Commission found collusion that included trader discussions of certain JPY LIBOR submissions and exchanges of information on trading positions and future submissions [15].

*Strength: Strong (settlement with admitted liability). Reporting incentive:* the two banks that reported first paid nothing to the Commission, which is how leniency is designed to work, but it means the fine column does not rank culpability.

### 1.6 Individual prosecutions: convictions later overturned (not findings against the banks)

- **United Kingdom.** In July 2025 the UK Supreme Court ([2025] UKSC 29) quashed the conspiracy-to-defraud convictions of a former UBS/Citigroup yen derivatives trader (LIBOR) and a former Barclays euro derivatives trader (EURIBOR) [17]. It held that the trial judges had wrongly directed juries that a submission which took any account of trading advantage could not, as a matter of law, be a genuine or honest assessment; whether a submission was genuine was a question of fact for the jury [17].
- **United States.** In January 2022 the Second Circuit (No. 19-3806) reversed the wire fraud and conspiracy convictions of two former Deutsche Bank employees, finding the evidence "insufficient as a matter of law to permit a finding of falsity", and ordered acquittals [18].
- **What this does and does not change.** These rulings concern individuals and the law of fraud as charged. They do not vacate the corporate pleas, NPAs, DPAs, regulator orders or the Commission decision above, which rest on each institution's own admissions. They do weaken any claim that every trader-influenced submission was, by definition, false.

*Strength: Strong that these convictions were quashed or reversed.*

## How the scheme worked

1. **The benchmark.** LIBOR was the average of rates submitted daily by panel banks for what they believed they would pay to borrow unsecured from other banks, published for 10 currencies and 15 maturities; the DOJ cited a Bank for International Settlements estimate of about $450 trillion ⚑ in outstanding interest rate contracts in the second half of 2009 [9]. Submissions were judgements, not transaction prices, which is what made them movable [29].
2. **Trader requests ("portfolio" manipulation).** Derivatives traders whose positions were priced off LIBOR asked their own bank's submitters to move submissions up or down on particular days [3; 7; 11; 14]. The DOJ record gives one illustration: a senior trader estimated that a 0.01 percentage-point move in one Yen LIBOR fixing could be worth about $2 million to the bank [4].
3. **Collusion across banks and brokers.** Traders asked counterparts at other panel banks, and inter-dealer brokers, to influence those banks' submissions, which is why the conduct was also charged as Sherman Act price-fixing and treated by the Commission as a cartel [7; 9; 12; 15].
4. **"Lowballing" during the crisis.** Separately, Barclays lowered submissions to avoid appearing to pay more for funding than peers [3]. This motive was about reputation and perceived creditworthiness rather than trading profit.
5. **Why controls failed.** A criminological crime-script analysis built from enforcement records and interviews found that the same organisational and regulatory systems meant to guard the benchmark also facilitated the manipulation (the "guardian paradox") [32].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

- **Integrity of a widely used reference rate.** The size of contracts referencing LIBOR (about $450 trillion ⚑ in interest rate contracts, BIS estimate cited by DOJ) is a measure of exposure, not of loss [9].
- **Distortion was real but per-day effects were small.** Peer-reviewed analysis of submissions from 2001 to 2012 found evidence consistent with banks manipulating LIBOR to profit from positions and to signal creditworthiness in distressed periods; the evidence was stronger for banks later sanctioned and disappeared after the investigations began in 2010 [27]. A study of panel submissions found that the rate-setting design gave individual banks meaningful influence, which alternative fixing methods would reduce [28].
- **Counterparties.** The DOJ and CFTC records describe counterparties to interest rate derivatives as the defrauded parties [4; 9; 12]. No published, adjudicated figure for total net loss to borrowers or investors was located.
- **Trust and cost of transition.** Ending LIBOR required repapering or legislating fallbacks for very large numbers of contracts [24; 30]. No sourced figure for the transition's total cost was located.

*Strength: Moderate that manipulation moved rates; Very limited for any global loss figure (none adjudicated).*

## Fair counterarguments

1. **How much did manipulation move LIBOR?** An early peer-reviewed screen of one-month US dollar LIBOR found anomalous patterns in individual bank quotes but did not establish material manipulation of the rate itself [26]. Later work with full submission data found evidence consistent with manipulation [27]. The two are compatible: trader requests often aimed at small, day-specific moves that are hard to detect in averages.
2. **Was a trader-influenced submission false?** The UK Supreme Court and the Second Circuit both rejected the idea that any account taken of trading positions automatically makes a submission false or dishonest [17; 18]. Regulators' findings were framed differently, around failures of systems, controls and integrity [3; 7; 11; 14], and the banks admitted facts in their agreements. Readers should not merge the two standards.
3. **Who carried the blame?** A socio-legal study argues that criminal prosecution fell on mid-level traders while law "undervalued the role of top managers and organizations", which mostly resolved by settlement [31]. A counter-view is that the corporate resolutions did impose admissions, monitors and very large penalties ⚑ [12; 16].
4. **Do headline totals overstate punishment?** Agency announcements sum amounts across agencies ("more than $1.5 billion", "approximately $2.519 billion") ⚑ [4; 12]; some payments are credited against others, and settlement discounts and leniency reductions apply [3; 14; 15]. Conversely, the repeated NPA breaches by Barclays and UBS [16] suggest the first-round sanctions did not prevent further misconduct.
5. **Lowballing and official encouragement.** Whether the authorities tolerated or encouraged low crisis-era submissions is disputed in public commentary. No court or regulator finding on that question was located, so it is **not** treated as a finding here.

**How the conclusion handles these points.** The case rests each bank's entry on that bank's own admissions and regulator orders, not on the individual prosecutions, two of which were overturned. It reports headline totals as enforcement outcomes rather than measures of harm, and treats claims about official encouragement of low submissions as unadjudicated.

## Values (labelled section: judgement, not fact)

*This section states value positions and the tensions between them. It is not a finding.*

- **Honesty in shared infrastructure.** A benchmark is a public good built from private statements. Bending it for a trading book is a breach of trust towards everyone who relies on it, whatever the measured loss.
- **Organisational versus individual responsibility.** Fairness argues against making a few traders the sole face of a practice that the records show was widespread and poorly controlled [7; 14; 31]. Deterrence argues for both: corporate penalties to change incentives, and individual accountability to change behaviour.
- **Due process.** The quashed and reversed convictions [17; 18] are a reminder that public anger does not lower the standard of proof, and that the law must define the wrong clearly before people are punished for it.
- **Leniency's moral cost.** Immunity for the first to confess [15] is effective policy but can feel unjust. The trade-off is acceptable only if it demonstrably breaks more cartels than it excuses.

## Proposed solutions (each sourced and rated for evidence)

The last column rates evidence that each measure works: **strong** means consistent causal evidence from more than one careful study; **moderate** means one careful study or consistent observational evidence; **weak** means model-only, indirect or single-case support; **UNTESTED** means no evaluation of effect was located.

| Measure | Source | Evidence of effect | Evidence rating |
|---|---|---|---|
| Replace judgement-based rates with transaction-based, near risk-free rates (SOFR in the US, reformed SONIA in the UK) | ARRC selection of a broad Treasuries repo rate, 2017 [22]; Bank of England SONIA [23]; review of the post-LIBOR regime [30] | LIBOR panels ceased: most settings after 31 December 2021 and the remaining US dollar settings after 30 June 2023 [24]. Rates built on large volumes of transactions remove the submission step that was abused [23; 29] | moderate (the judgement-based panels ended; no causal study of misconduct after the switch) |
| Regulate benchmark administration and submission | Wheatley Review 2012 [19]; IOSCO Principles for Financial Benchmarks 2013 [21]; EU Benchmarks Regulation (EU) 2016/1011 [25] | Created governance, oversight and evidence requirements for submissions. No peer-reviewed causal estimate of these rules' effect was located | UNTESTED |
| Make benchmark manipulation a specific crime | UK Financial Services Act 2012, s. 91 [20] | Creates a specific benchmark offence (in force from 2013) instead of relying on conspiracy to defraud; the 2025 convictions were quashed for misdirection on whether submissions were genuine [17], and whether the specific offence would have changed those outcomes is untested. Effect on conduct not measured | UNTESTED |
| Design fixings to resist manipulation | Peer-reviewed mechanism design and submission analysis [28; 29] | Alternative fixing rules (trimming, transaction anchoring, fines for deviation from comparison rates) shown in models and back-tests to cut the effect of manipulation [28] | weak (models and back-tests only) |
| Visible, large public enforcement | Gandhi et al. [27] | Evidence of manipulation disappeared after investigations began, consistent with deterrence by penalties and reputational loss [27] | moderate (observational) |
