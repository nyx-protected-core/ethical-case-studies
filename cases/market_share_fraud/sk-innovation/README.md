# SK Innovation and LG's electric-vehicle battery secrets: a default finding after evidence destruction, a ten-year order, and a settlement

Date: 2026-11-19

## The ethical question

In a dispute over trade secrets for electric-vehicle batteries, the decision against the respondent came not from a full trial but from a finding that evidence had been destroyed. A ten-year import ban was imposed, then carved out for carmakers, then lifted after the companies settled. Does a sanction like this protect fair competition, and what does it say that the order did not last?

## Analysis

The case shows two things. First, **destroying evidence can itself decide the case**: the violation rested on default after spoliation, not on a full trial on the merits [1]. That is a legitimate and necessary sanction, but it means the record does not contain a merits finding about what was taken and how it was used. This case therefore describes the finding precisely: a default-based violation, not a proven theft.

Second, the **carve-outs** show how a long exclusion can be designed so that third parties are not cut off overnight [1]. The quick rescission after settlement [3] shows the other side: a ten-year order may in practice work as leverage for a private deal. Whether that serves the public interest in deterrence, or only the two companies, is an open question.

## Fair counterarguments

- **Legitimate protection versus litigation as a weapon.** Suing a rival over intellectual property is often legitimate protection of research. But litigation can also be used to raise a competitor's costs; research on suits by patent holders that do not make products found that targeted firms substantially reduce their innovative activity after settling or losing [4]. A finding in court shows that a line was crossed; it does not show that every claim the winning side made was right.
- **Learning is not taking.** The international standard protects undisclosed information only against acquisition "contrary to honest commercial practices" [5], which is narrower than all copying. Learning from public products and hiring experienced people are lawful and are how skills spread. This case concerns confidential material that a court, regulator or plea record found was taken or misused, not learning from public products or hiring experienced people.
- **The costs of litigation.** Long cases consume money and management attention on both sides, and delay can hurt the winner as much as the loser. Remedies that depend on ever-longer litigation are a weak answer to a problem of speed.
- **Industrial policy and jobs.** The orders affected battery supply for US electric-vehicle programmes and the Trade Representative publicly welcomed the settlement [2]. Governments can have reasons to prefer a settlement over enforcement; those reasons are legitimate but different from deterrence.

## Proposed solutions

The proposals below are this case's own reasoning, built on the cited evidence. Each carries an evidence rating for how well research supports the measure itself: **strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **UNTESTED** (no study cited here tests whether the measure works).

1. **Make the expected penalty larger than the gain, mainly by raising the chance of being caught.** In the standard economic model, a firm deterred only by money compares the gain with the probability of a sanction times its size [6][7]. Reviews of the evidence find that certainty of apprehension deters more consistently than severity [8], and that single punitive sanctions against companies do not deter consistently while combinations of measures (regulation, inspection, sanctions together) do [9]. Detection funding, forensic review of departing-employee transfers in litigation, and protected reporting channels therefore matter more than headline fines. *Evidence rating: moderate. The reviews cited are broad; none tests trade-secret or infringement enforcement specifically.*
2. **Measure the remedy by the gain, not only the victim's provable loss.** US civil law already allows recovery of unjust enrichment and up to double exemplary damages for wilful and malicious trade-secret theft [10], and criminal fines for organisations can reach three times the value of the stolen secret, including avoided research and design costs [11]. Courts and prosecutors should use the gain-based measures the law already allows, and report the gain they found, so that the public can compare penalty with benefit. *Evidence rating: weak. Supported by enforcement theory [6][7]; UNTESTED as a measured change in behaviour.*
3. **Take away the product advantage, not the whole firm.** Where products are made with misappropriated technology, a product-specific exclusion, verified by an independent technical expert and lasting at least as long as the head start gained, removes the market benefit while sparing customers and workers who did nothing wrong. A ten-year US exclusion order for products made with misappropriated trade secrets is an existing precedent [12][13]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. A legal precedent exists; no study cited here measures its deterrent effect.*
4. **Protect the people who report it.** US law gives immunity to a person who discloses a trade secret in confidence to officials or to a lawyer in order to report a suspected violation [14]. Similar protection, and a clear duty to report in engineering codes, should be standard. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. The legal protection exists; no study cited here measures its effect on detection.*
5. **Do not over-correct into employer-friendly secrecy.** Stronger trade-secret law was associated with more R&D in some industries [15], but stronger employer-friendly secrecy rules were associated with less innovation by individual inventors [16], and intellectual-property rights that are too broad can reduce follow-on research [17]. Remedies should target taking and using confidential material, not ordinary job mobility or learning. *Evidence rating: moderate. Three peer-reviewed empirical studies, with mixed directions by industry and by inventor.*
6. **Public terms for rescinding public orders.** Where a public exclusion order is lifted because of a private settlement, the main terms that bear on the public interest (for example, whether the products remain free of the disputed secrets) should be stated in the rescission record. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. This is a design proposal; no study cited here tests it.*

## What this does not replace

- **Legal advice or a legal opinion.** Nothing here says how any pending or future dispute should be decided. Anyone involved in a trade-secret, patent, copyright or related matter needs a qualified lawyer in the relevant country.
- **The court record itself.** Summaries here are shortened. Where a figure or finding matters, read the cited judgment, plea or notice.
- **A company's current conduct or compliance standing.** A finding describes past conduct at a past date. It is not evidence about the company's present practices, products or staff, and must not be used to judge individual employees.
- **Due diligence or a sanctions screen.** This case is not a supplier-risk score, a trade-control list or an investment recommendation.
