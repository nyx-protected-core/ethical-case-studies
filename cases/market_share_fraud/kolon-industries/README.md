# Kolon Industries and DuPont's Kevlar secrets: a vacated civil verdict, then a guilty plea and $360 million

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study. Research and writing only; this is not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Found**: what a court, jury, regulator or plea record decided or admitted. **Alleged**: what a prosecutor charged or a party claimed; an indictment or complaint is not a finding. **Reported**: facts known only from news coverage, labelled SECONDARY. Figures are quoted as the source gives them. Government figures (prosecution estimates, agency loss figures) are flagged, because agencies and prosecutors have reasons to present large numbers when announcing results. Individuals are not named except where a public office or a court record makes the name part of the public finding; private individuals are not named.*

## The ethical question

A firm making a competing high-strength fibre recruited former employees of the market leader to improve its own product. A civil jury found wilful theft, but the judgment was thrown out on appeal for an evidence error. A criminal plea followed. What does this sequence say about how reliable each route to accountability is?

## Findings at a glance

| Step | Status | What the record says | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| June 2006 – February 2009 | **Admitted** | Kolon conspired with former DuPont employees and others to steal DuPont's trade secrets for making Kevlar para-aramid fibre, in an attempt to improve its own fibre, Heracron | [1] |
| 2011 (civil trial) | Found, later vacated | A jury found that Kolon wilfully and maliciously misappropriated the trade secrets and awarded DuPont $919.9 million | [2] |
| 3 April 2014 | Vacated | The Fourth Circuit vacated the judgment and ordered a new trial because relevant evidence had been excluded | [2] |
| 30 April 2015 | **Found (plea)** | Kolon Industries, appearing through two successor entities, pleaded guilty to one count of conspiracy to convert trade secrets and was sentenced to an $85 million criminal fine and $275 million in restitution | [1] |

Only the 2015 plea is treated as the adjudicated finding here; the 2011 verdict no longer stands.

## Damages against market effect

- **Civil verdict, vacated.** The 2011 jury put the benefit to Kolon at $919.9 million [2]. Because the judgment was vacated, this figure is reported for history only.
- **Criminal payment.** $85 million fine plus $275 million restitution, $360 million in total [1].
- **Market effect.** No record opened measures Kolon's market gain in para-aramid fibre. This case makes no estimate.

## Analysis

Kolon shows that **one route can fail and another succeed**. A civil verdict that would have taken nearly a billion dollars was undone on a procedural point [2]; the criminal case then produced an admission and a large payment [1]. Multiple, overlapping enforcement tools are what the deterrence evidence recommends over single sanctions [3].

It also shows how theft travels through people. The admitted conduct ran through repeated meetings with former employees of the victim [1]. That is the same recruitment pattern seen in Hytera and UMC, and it is why the proposals below include protections for people who report, as well as limits that keep ordinary job changes lawful.

## Fair counterarguments

- **Legitimate protection versus litigation as a weapon.** Suing a rival over intellectual property is often legitimate protection of research. But litigation can also be used to raise a competitor's costs; research on suits by patent holders that do not make products found that targeted firms substantially reduce their innovative activity after settling or losing [4]. A finding in court shows that a line was crossed; it does not show that every claim the winning side made was right.
- **Learning is not taking.** The international standard protects undisclosed information only against acquisition "contrary to honest commercial practices" [5], which is narrower than all copying. Learning from public products and hiring experienced people are lawful and are how skills spread. This case concerns confidential material that a court, regulator or plea record found was taken or misused, not learning from public products or hiring experienced people.
- **The costs of litigation.** Long cases consume money and management attention on both sides, and delay can hurt the winner as much as the loser. Remedies that depend on ever-longer litigation are a weak answer to a problem of speed.
- **The vacated verdict cuts both ways.** The appeal court found that the trial excluded evidence Kolon was entitled to present [2]. That is a reminder that large verdicts can rest on flawed trials, and that a fair system must allow them to be undone.

## Proposed solutions

The proposals below are this case's own reasoning, built on the cited evidence. Each carries an evidence rating for how well research supports the measure itself: **strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **UNTESTED** (no study cited here tests whether the measure works).

1. **Make the expected penalty larger than the gain, mainly by raising the chance of being caught.** In the standard economic model, a firm deterred only by money compares the gain with the probability of a sanction times its size [6][7]. Reviews of the evidence find that certainty of apprehension deters more consistently than severity [8], and that single punitive sanctions against companies do not deter consistently while combinations of measures (regulation, inspection, sanctions together) do [3]. Detection funding, forensic review of departing-employee transfers in litigation, and protected reporting channels therefore matter more than headline fines. *Evidence rating: moderate. The reviews cited are broad; none tests trade-secret or infringement enforcement specifically.*
2. **Measure the remedy by the gain, not only the victim's provable loss.** US civil law already allows recovery of unjust enrichment and up to double exemplary damages for wilful and malicious trade-secret theft [9], and criminal fines for organisations can reach three times the value of the stolen secret, including avoided research and design costs [10]. Courts and prosecutors should use the gain-based measures the law already allows, and report the gain they found, so that the public can compare penalty with benefit. *Evidence rating: weak. Supported by enforcement theory [6][7]; UNTESTED as a measured change in behaviour.*
3. **Take away the product advantage, not the whole firm.** Where products are made with misappropriated technology, a product-specific exclusion, verified by an independent technical expert and lasting at least as long as the head start gained, removes the market benefit while sparing customers and workers who did nothing wrong. A ten-year US exclusion order for products made with misappropriated trade secrets is an existing precedent [11][12]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. A legal precedent exists; no study cited here measures its deterrent effect.*
4. **Protect the people who report it.** US law gives immunity to a person who discloses a trade secret in confidence to officials or to a lawyer in order to report a suspected violation [13]. Similar protection, and a clear duty to report in engineering codes, should be standard. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. The legal protection exists; no study cited here measures its effect on detection.*
5. **Do not over-correct into employer-friendly secrecy.** Stronger trade-secret law was associated with more R&D in some industries [14], but stronger employer-friendly secrecy rules were associated with less innovation by individual inventors [15], and intellectual-property rights that are too broad can reduce follow-on research [16]. Remedies should target taking and using confidential material, not ordinary job mobility or learning. *Evidence rating: moderate. Three peer-reviewed empirical studies, with mixed directions by industry and by inventor.*

## What this does not replace

- **Legal advice or a legal opinion.** Nothing here says how any pending or future dispute should be decided. Anyone involved in a trade-secret, patent, copyright or related matter needs a qualified lawyer in the relevant country.
- **The court record itself.** Summaries here are shortened. Where a figure or finding matters, read the cited judgment, plea or notice.
- **A company's current conduct or compliance standing.** A finding describes past conduct at a past date. It is not evidence about the company's present practices, products or staff, and must not be used to judge individual employees.
- **Due diligence or a sanctions screen.** This case is not a supplier-risk score, a trade-control list or an investment recommendation.
