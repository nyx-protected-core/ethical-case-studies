# The Auto-Parts Cartels: Yazaki, Denso, Bridgestone and Others — Bid-Rigging Across the Car Supply Chain

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study in the market share fraud series. Research and writing only; not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. The companies named here (including Yazaki, Denso, Furukawa Electric, Bridgestone and the other parts makers listed) each pleaded guilty in the United States or were fined or ordered by the European Commission or the Japan Fair Trade Commission; executives appear by role only. Government figures carry a ⚑ flag because agencies tend to announce cumulative totals.*

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers the group of price-fixing and bid-rigging conspiracies among automotive parts suppliers that came to light from about 2010, often called the auto-parts "super cartel". It focuses on adjudicated outcomes in the United States (guilty pleas in the US Department of Justice's largest criminal antitrust investigation), the European Union (the 2013 wire harness settlement) and Japan (the 2012 wire harness orders). Named companies are those with a plea, order or decision. Other parts cartels decided elsewhere are mentioned only where a primary record was opened.

- **Findings first.** The Findings section lists only adjudicated or admitted findings, each with its primary record.
- **Source tags** are defined at the top of Sources. No news reports are used.
- **Government figures are marked ⚑.** DOJ cumulative totals ("over $2.9 billion", "more than 100 companies and executives") combine many separate cases and years, and are announced by the agency that achieved them. Affected-commerce figures are DOJ's own estimates used in sentencing and press releases. Leniency means the first reporter can pay nothing (for example Sumitomo Electric in the EU), so fines do not rank culpability.
- **People.** No individual is named. Executives are described by role.
- **Peer-reviewed evidence** on overcharges is mostly from cross-cartel meta-analyses, not from these cartels specifically. It is labelled as general evidence wherever used.
- **Evidence strength.** Findings are rated **Strong**, **Moderate**, **Limited** or **Very limited** for how firmly the record supports them. Proposed solutions carry a separate evidence rating: strong, moderate, weak or UNTESTED.

## Findings (adjudicated or admitted facts, each with its primary record)

### 1.1 United States: Yazaki and Denso (2012)

- **Yazaki Corporation** agreed to plead guilty to three Sherman Act conspiracies: to rig bids for and fix prices of automotive wire harnesses and related products (from at least January 2000 until at least February 2010), instrument panel clusters (from at least December 2002) and fuel senders (from at least March 2004), and to pay a $470 million criminal fine ⚑ [1; 2]. The plea agreement was filed in the Eastern District of Michigan [2]. The Justice Department release [1] could not be opened in this check; its description of the fine as the second largest Sherman Act fine at that time ⚑ is UNVERIFIED, while the $470 million amount is confirmed by the plea agreement [2].
- **DENSO Corporation** agreed to plead guilty to conspiracies to rig bids for and fix prices of electronic control units and heater control panels and to pay a $78 million fine ⚑ [1] (UNVERIFIED: the release could not be opened in this check).
- Four Yazaki executives agreed to plead guilty and serve prison terms of 15 months to two years in the United States [1].
- The DOJ described the method: agreeing in meetings and conversations to allocate supply on a model-by-model basis and to coordinate responses to price-reduction requests from car manufacturers [1].

### 1.2 United States: the wider investigation

- **Earlier plea.** Furukawa Electric Co. Ltd. was charged in September 2011 over wire harnesses and fined $200,000,000 ⚑; three of its executives were sentenced to prison terms of about one to one and a half years [3].
- **September 2013.** Nine Japan-based companies and two executives agreed to plead guilty to conspiracies covering more than 30 products, with more than $740 million in fines ⚑, including Hitachi Automotive Systems ($195 million), Mitsubishi Electric ($190 million), Mitsuba ($135 million), Jtekt ($103.27 million) and NSK ($68.2 million) [4]. The DOJ stated that, with that announcement, 20 companies and 21 executives had been charged and the 20 companies had agreed to pay more than $1.6 billion ⚑ [4].
- **Bridgestone (February 2014).** Bridgestone Corporation agreed to plead guilty to a conspiracy to allocate sales, rig bids and fix prices of anti-vibration rubber parts (from at least January 2001 until at least December 2008) and pay a $425 million fine ⚑ [5]. Bridgestone had pleaded guilty in October 2011 in the marine hose case without disclosing this conspiracy; the DOJ said that failure to disclose "was a factor in determining the $425 million fine" [5].
- **Cumulative.** The DOJ Antitrust Division states that the investigation led to the prosecution of more than 100 companies and executives and over $2.9 billion in criminal fines ⚑, with 32 individuals sentenced to fines and prison [6].

*Strength: Strong (pleas entered or agreed in court records).*

### 1.3 European Union: wire harnesses (July 2013)

The European Commission fined Yazaki, Furukawa, S-Y Systems Technologies and Leoni a total of €141,791,000 ⚑ for five cartels in the supply of wire harnesses to Toyota, Honda, Nissan and Renault, including tenders for European manufacturing plants [7]. Yazaki was fined €125,341,000; Furukawa €4,015,000; S-Y Systems €11,057,000; Leoni €1,378,000. Sumitomo Electric received immunity [7]. The infringements ran for periods between March 2000 and December 2009, depending on customer [7]. The decision was adopted by settlement [7].

*Strength: Strong.*

### 1.4 Japan: wire harnesses (January 2012)

The Japan Fair Trade Commission issued cease-and-desist and surcharge payment orders to Yazaki, Sumitomo Electric Industries and Fujikura for bid rigging on wire harnesses and related products ordered by Toyota and other car makers, with total surcharges of about ¥12.9 billion ⚑ [8]. The JFTC notes that the English text is a tentative translation [8]. The JFTC page could not be opened in this check, so these orders and the total are UNVERIFIED here; Yazaki's and Furukawa's conduct is separately found in the US and EU records [2; 3; 7].

*Strength: Strong (administrative orders).*

## How the scheme worked

1. **Bid rigging at the model level.** Car makers buy many parts through requests for quotation for each new vehicle model. Suppliers agreed in advance which of them would win particular models, then submitted coordinated bids [1; 7].
2. **Coordinating responses to cost-down demands.** Car makers ask suppliers for regular price reductions. The conspirators coordinated how far to concede [1].
3. **Monitoring.** Meetings and conversations were used to check adherence [1]. This matches the general features of cartel organisation documented across European Commission decisions: allocation, monitoring of sales, and regular meetings [17].
4. **Many linked cartels.** The conspiracies were product-specific but overlapped through shared members and shared customers. One scholar counts 70 to 80 interconnected schemes discovered between 2008 and 2017, with almost half of convicted firms Japan-based parents or subsidiaries [18, WP].
5. **Cascading leniency.** The DOJ describes "cascading cooperators" [6]: a firm caught in one product cartel could gain credit by reporting another. Bridgestone's case shows the penalty for not doing so [5].

## Harms (quantified only where sourced)

- **Affected commerce ⚑.** For the nine companies in September 2013 alone, the DOJ said the conspiracies "affected more than $5 billion in automobile parts sold to U.S. car manufacturers" and more than 25 million cars [4]. These are prosecutor estimates, not court-tested loss figures.
- **Overcharges: general evidence, not case-specific.** No peer-reviewed overcharge estimate for these specific cartels was located. Meta-analyses of other cartels give a sense of scale: a European sample with mean 20.7% and median 18.4% of selling price [11]; and earlier surveys finding averages of 18–37% for US cartels and higher for EU ones [10]. These figures should not be applied mechanically to auto parts.
- **Who bears the cost.** The direct buyers were car makers; how much was passed on to car buyers is an empirical question in damages cases. The DOJ's figure of more than 25 million affected cars is an agency estimate ⚑ [4], not a finding of pass-on.

*Strength: Strong that the conduct occurred; Very limited for the size of harm.*

## Fair counterarguments

1. **Why did these cartels form?** One account suggests the cartels may have been a response to sustained cost-down pressure from car makers after traditional supplier loyalty in Japan began to break down around 1999, though the author poses this as a question rather than a finding [18, WP]. Others see the cartels simply as profitable collusion that leniency eventually broke. Neither view excuses the conduct.
2. **How large are overcharges?** Earlier surveys report averages of 18–37% or more [10]; a European sample finds a mean of 20.7% [11], and estimates vary with method and sample. Courts in damages cases usually rely on case-specific expert evidence.
3. **Are fines high enough to deter?** Several peer-reviewed studies argue that, compared with likely overcharges, cartel fines are moderate or too low [10; 11; 21]. Others find that the European Commission's 2006 fining guidelines and the 2014 Damages Directive appear to have reduced overcharges [16].
4. **Does prison deter international cartels?** The US sought prison terms for foreign executives [1]. A peer-reviewed review found the policy raised the number and length of sentences but that no-jail plea deals persisted, almost half of executives who went to trial were acquitted, and extradition was rare [14].
5. **Does leniency work?** Statistical tests on 20 years of US indictments are consistent with leniency improving detection and deterrence [12]. A study of the 1996 EU programme found it sped up investigations and raised fines but found no clear sign of greater deterrence [13]. Granting reductions to many cartel members ("leniency inflation") may weaken the race to confess [15].
6. **Headline totals ⚑.** "Over $2.9 billion" [6] sums many separate pleas over several years and products; per-company fines reflect plea bargaining and cooperation credit.

**How the conclusion handles these points.** Company entries rest on pleas, plea agreements and Commission decisions. Overcharge figures come from studies of other cartels and are given only as a range, not as a measure of harm in these cases. The case does not claim that prison terms or fines deterred later conduct.

## Values (labelled section: judgement, not fact)

*This section states value positions. It is not a finding.*

- **Competitive bidding is a promise.** A buyer who runs a tender assumes the bidders are rivals. Rigging the tender turns a market into a private tax.
- **Supplier pressure is not a defence.** Even if buyers pushed hard on price, the remedy is negotiation, exit or a complaint to an authority, not secret allocation.
- **Fairness in leniency.** Immunity rewards the first firm to confess, even though it may have been as culpable as the rest. That trade-off is acceptable only if it reliably exposes cartels that would otherwise last for years.
- **Individuals and firms.** Prison for managers places responsibility on people who made decisions, but corporate culture and incentives also need to change.

## Proposed solutions (each sourced and rated for evidence)

The last column rates evidence that each measure works: **strong** means consistent causal evidence from more than one careful study; **moderate** means one careful study or consistent observational evidence; **weak** means indirect or single-case support; **UNTESTED** means no evaluation of effect was located.

| Measure | Source | Evidence of effect | Evidence rating |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corporate leniency, including credit for reporting other cartels | DOJ Leniency Policy [9]; DOJ account of "cascading cooperators" [6] | US evidence consistent with better detection and deterrence [12]; EU evidence mixed [13]; risk of "leniency inflation" [15] | moderate (observational; cascading investigations documented by the enforcer) |
| Higher penalties for repeat or non-disclosing offenders | Bridgestone plea [5] | Signals a cost to withholding; deterrent effect not measured | UNTESTED (single plea; deterrent effect not measured) |
| Criminal liability and prison for individuals | DOJ practice [1; 6] | More and longer sentences, but limits on reach in international cases [14] | weak (sentences rose, but a peer-reviewed review found limits on reach) |
| Fines set with reference to likely overcharge | Peer-reviewed overcharge studies [10; 11] | Studies argue current fines are often below the gain from collusion [10; 11] | UNTESTED (the gap between fines and gains is moderately supported; the effect of raising fines has not been evaluated) |
| Private damages actions | EU Damages Directive 2014/104/EU [20]; peer-reviewed evaluation [16] | Directive associated with higher private damage payments and lower overcharges in the EU [16]; may reduce leniency take-up if applicants are not protected [15] | moderate (one peer-reviewed evaluation) |
| Procurement design by buyers | OECD analysis of cartels in intermediate goods [19] | Buyer-side tender design and monitoring discussed as detection tools; not evaluated here | UNTESTED (guidance only; not evaluated) |
