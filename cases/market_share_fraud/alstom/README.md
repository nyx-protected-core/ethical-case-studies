# Alstom (2014): U.S. guilty plea, subsidiary pleas and DPAs, and a later UK conviction, for bribes to win power, grid and transport contracts

Date: 2026-11-19

**Key to flags.** ⚑ marks a figure published by a government or intergovernmental body. These are negotiated or court-imposed penalty amounts, not measures of harm. Agencies have reasons to present penalties as record-setting, and every figure covers only the conduct that was detected and charged (detection bias).

---

## 1. Summary

On 22 December 2014, **Alstom S.A.**, a French power and transport group, **pleaded guilty** in the U.S. District Court for the District of Connecticut to two FCPA counts: falsifying books and records, and failing to keep adequate internal controls. It agreed to a criminal fine of **$772,290,000 ⚑** [R1], [R3]. On the same day, its Swiss subsidiary pleaded guilty to conspiracy to violate the FCPA's anti-bribery provisions, and two U.S. subsidiaries entered deferred prosecution agreements (DPAs) [R3], [R4].

According to the companies' admissions, Alstom entities paid **more than $75 million ⚑** in bribes, mainly through sham "consultants", to win **about $4 billion ⚑** in state-owned power, grid and transport projects. The countries named were Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, the Bahamas and Taiwan. Alstom's gain was about **$296 million ⚑** [R2], [R4].

The court imposed the agreed fine on 13 November 2015 [R4]. In the United Kingdom, the Serious Fraud Office (SFO) later secured a conviction of **Alstom Network UK Ltd** (April 2018) for conspiracy to corrupt over a Tunis metro tram contract, and guilty pleas by **Alstom Power Ltd** over a Lithuanian power-plant contract [R6].

**Evidence strength: strong.** The plea agreement, the criminal information and the sentencing release are court or DOJ records, and all three were opened. The UK facts come from the SFO's own press release, read in a verbatim republication because the SFO's page could not be opened, and the UK court records were not opened (see sources).

**Individuals (U.S.).** The plea record says Alstom's late cooperation came "after the Department had publicly charged multiple Alstom executives and employees" [R1]. One individual was convicted by a jury. In 2022 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit **affirmed his acquittal on the FCPA counts**, because there was no agency or employee relationship with the U.S. subsidiary. It also affirmed the rulings that left his money-laundering convictions in place [R5]. This case does not name individuals, including those acquitted in the UK.

## 4. How the scheme worked, as found in the record

- **Sham consultants.** The criminal information describes consultants who "purportedly provided legitimate services" but were in fact "retained for the purpose of paying bribes" [R2]. In Saudi Arabia, the consultants included the brother and a close relative of members of the board of the state-owned electricity company. They used code names such as "Geneva", "Paris", "London" and "Old Friend" [R2], [R1 Statement of Facts].
- **Size of payments.** One Saudi consultant was paid "at least $30 million" and another "at least $10 million" under several consultancy agreements [R1 Statement of Facts].
- **Books.** The payments were recorded as consultancy fees, so the books were false. That is the U.S. offence for the parent company [R1], [R2].
- **UK pattern.** The same intermediary method was used in Tunisia. Alstom "helped to produce paperwork as 'evidence' of services rendered", although the intermediary "was simply a conduit for bribes" [R6].

## 5. Monitorships and aftermath

- **Reliance on the World Bank monitorship.** The DOJ relied on the World Bank's monitorship rather than appointing its own, with a fallback monitor if Alstom fell short [R1]. This is an early example of one authority deferring to another's oversight.
- **Remediation the DOJ credited:** more compliance staff, better alert procedures, more training and testing, and an end to the use of "external success fee-based consultants" [R1, ¶(4)].
- **Peer-reviewed view.** Hatchard (2016) uses Alstom as a case study of the "art of persuasion", meaning the mix of prosecution, debarment and monitoring used to push powerful companies towards integrity. The article draws "lessons and challenges" from the case [P1]. Only the abstract was read.

## 7. Fair counterarguments

1. **Repeat offender, record fine: is that deterrence?** The DOJ gave non-disclosure, several years of refusal to fully cooperate and prior misconduct as reasons for the record fine [R1]. Cheung, Rau & Stouraitis (2021) find that for the median caught firm the net present value of a bribe is about zero, while larger bribes go with larger benefits [P2]. A firm that is rarely caught can therefore still come out ahead, and a record fine may not change behaviour unless the chance of detection rises.
2. **Limits on reaching individuals.** The 2022 appellate ruling narrowed the FCPA's reach over foreign nationals acting outside the U.S. who are not agents of a U.S. company [R5]. Some argue this protects proper limits on extraterritorial law. Others argue it leaves a gap, because companies pay while some managers fall outside the statute. Garrett (2017) describes the rapid globalisation of corporate prosecution over 15 years [P3]. This case shows that globalisation reaching companies more easily than people.
3. **Several countries, one course of conduct.** The U.S., Switzerland, the World Bank and the UK each resolved parts of the Alstom conduct [R1], [R6]. Bu (2022) argues that multiple sovereigns pursuing the same entity complicate compliance and calls for a global settlement regime [P4]. Others argue that each country prosecuting the bribes paid in its own sphere is legitimate and not double punishment. The Alstom record does not settle this.
4. **Debarment versus monitoring.** The World Bank route (debarment plus monitoring) and the criminal route (fine plus plea) were combined here [R1]. Whether debarment or fines do more to deter is contested. **Open question:** no peer-reviewed comparison specific to Alstom was found.

**How this case handles them.** The case keeps its conclusion that Alstom companies admitted paying bribes through consultants, because it rests on a guilty plea, a subsidiary plea and two DPAs in a US court, plus a UK jury conviction. It accepts that the UK trial also produced acquittals on the India and Poland counts, so each count is reported as proven or not proven on its own, and that the 2022 appeal ruling limits how far the FCPA reaches individuals abroad.

## 9. Values and proposals (normative)

1. **Ban success-fee intermediaries in public tenders, or require them to be disclosed.** The admitted method was the success-fee "consultant", and ending that practice was the main remediation the DOJ credited [R1, ¶(4)]. Procurement rules should require each intermediary's beneficial owner and fee basis to be disclosed to the buyer. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). No evaluation of the measure is cited.*
2. **Make cross-authority monitoring explicit and published.** The DOJ relied on the World Bank's monitorship [R1]. If authorities rely on one another's monitors, the monitor's reports or summaries should be public so the reliance can be checked. *Basis:* Arlen's case that mandates imposed through agreements need oversight [P5]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). No evaluation of the measure is cited.*
3. **Set clear rules for the reach of individual liability.** The 2022 ruling [R5] shows liability turning on agency doctrine. Lawmakers, not case-by-case litigation, should decide how far foreign-bribery statutes reach individuals. *Basis:* [R5]; Garrett [P3]. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). No evaluation of the measure is cited.*
4. **Credit and coordinate across countries, without granting immunity.** Adopt the global settlement approach Bu proposes [P4], but leave each country free to prosecute bribes affecting its own public. The UK Tunisia and Lithuania convictions [R6] show why. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). No evaluation of the measure is cited.*
5. **Raise detection, not just fines.** *Basis:* Cheung et al. [P2]. Fund investigators and whistleblower channels in the procuring states. *Evidence rating: untested (UNTESTED). Cheung, Rau and Stouraitis (2021) is correlational; funding detection has not been tested as an intervention.*

## 10. Recommendation (normative)

Present Alstom as the benchmark for late cooperation. The record ties a large share of the penalty to failure to self-report and to delayed cooperation [R1]. Pair it with the UK result, which included both convictions and acquittals [R6], so readers see that each count was proven or not proven separately.

## 12. What this does not replace

This case is not a judgment on any individual, including those acquitted in the UK, and it does not replace the plea agreement, the information or the UK court records. It is not a current compliance assessment of Alstom or of companies that later bought Alstom businesses, and it is not legal advice on the FCPA or the UK law of conspiracy to corrupt.
