# Coal and energy security: Canada

Date: 2026-11-19

Slug: coal-and-energy-security-canada

## Summary

Canada is a small coal burner and a large energy exporter. Federal regulations under the Canadian Environmental Protection Act, 1999 (CEPA) require conventional coal-fired power to meet a 420 t CO2/GWh standard by 31 December 2029, which in practice ends unabated coal power by 2030 [11][12]. Ontario stopped burning coal for power in 2014 and later put the ban in statute [26][13]. Alberta, once the largest coal-power province, finished its phase-out in June 2024, mostly by switching units to natural gas [3]. Saskatchewan said in June 2025 that it will run its coal units past 2030 [25]. Coal mining continues: in 2024, 67% of Canadian output was metallurgical coal for steelmaking, and Canada exported 36 Mt of the 42.6 Mt produced that year (nearly two thirds of output, on average, over the past 10 years) [17] [GOV-flag: Natural Resources Canada survey data; risk low, see Background]. Selenium from coal waste rock in the Elk Valley has led to a $60 million Fisheries Act penalty [15] [GOV-flag: court-imposed amount reported by the prosecutor; risk low] and is now measured more than 575 river kilometres downstream, across the border with the United States [6] [GOV-flag: US federal scientists; see Health section]. Canada exports oil, gas, uranium and electricity, almost entirely to the United States [18] [GOV-flag: Canada Energy Regulator; risk low, see Energy-security section], so its energy security is mostly about whether it can sell, not whether it can buy. The Clean Electricity Regulations (2024) set limits from 2035 [20], but the federal government has agreed to hold them in abeyance in Alberta while a constitutional reference is heard [21][22].

## Background

Canada was the third-largest generator of hydroelectricity in the world in 2023 [18] [GOV-flag: Canada Energy Regulator ranking, citing international data; mild incentive to stress Canada's hydro weight; risk low], and coal is a small part of its power mix. Coal produced 6% of Canadian electricity in 2023, burning 13.1 Mt of coal, down 68% from 41.1 Mt in 2011 [17] [GOV-flag: Natural Resources Canada; a department that champions the phase-out has some incentive to stress the decline, but the tonnage comes from trade and production surveys that are hard to bend; risk low].

Four provinces still burned coal for power in 2023: Alberta (41% of coal burned for power), Saskatchewan (36%), Nova Scotia (17%) and New Brunswick (6%) [17] [GOV-flag: same source and incentive; risk low]. Alberta's share has since dropped to zero [3].

The case is about two different coals. Thermal coal is burned for power. Metallurgical (coking) coal is used to make steel, and it is now most of what Canada mines and exports [17]. The phase-out rules cover only the first.

## Coal fleet and phase-out record

**Federal rules.**

- The Reduction of Carbon Dioxide Emissions from Coal-fired Generation of Electricity Regulations (SOR/2012-167), made under CEPA, set a 420 t CO2/GWh standard for new units and for old units at the end of their useful life [11].
- The 2018 amendments (SOR/2018-263) make every coal unit meet that standard by 2030 at the latest, which the government described as phasing out conventional coal by 2030 [12].
- The amendments' own impact statement forecast about 94 Mt CO2e of cumulative cuts from 2019 to 2055 and a net benefit of $2.7 billion, with about three quarters of compliance costs in Nova Scotia and New Brunswick [12] [GOV-flag: Environment and Climate Change Canada's cost-benefit statement for its own rule; incentive to over-report benefits and under-report costs; the department notes that both were revised down between draft and final].
- Units can comply by fitting carbon capture, but most were expected to close or convert to gas [12].

**Ontario (completed 2014).**

- In 2003, coal represented approximately 25% of Ontario's electricity generation (about 7,560 MW of capacity); in 2014 it represented 0% [26] [GOV-flag: Ontario government page presenting the phase-out as a success; incentive to stress a complete exit and its benefits; the generation shares are attributed there to Independent Electricity System Operator data].
- The Ending Coal for Cleaner Air Act, 2015 (S.O. 2015, c. 25) then banned coal-fired power at the Atikokan, Lambton, Nanticoke and Thunder Bay stations and, with narrow industrial exceptions, everywhere in the province after 31 December 2014 [13].
- A peer-reviewed modelling study estimates the health benefits of the Ontario, Alberta and national phase-outs [2]; its figures are not reported here because the paper could not be opened (UNVERIFIED).

**Alberta (completed June 2024).**

- Coal made close to half of Alberta's power in 2016, fell below 8% by the end of 2023 and was fully phased out by June 2024, as an industrial carbon price pushed generators to natural gas and wind [3] [GOV-flag: figures as reported in the peer-reviewed paper, from Alberta Electric System Operator market data; the operator has no obvious incentive to misstate annual fuel shares, but the June 2024 end date is also a politically salient milestone].
- Alberta agreed to pay the owners of six coal units that would otherwise have run past 2030. Its 2017 budget booked a $1.1 billion liability in 2016-17, the present value of $97 million a year for 14 years, funded from carbon-pricing revenue [14] [GOV-flag: Alberta Treasury Board; the government had an incentive to present the payments as cheap, and the budget itself calls them less than $10 per tonne; that per-tonne claim is not independently audited here].
- Peer-reviewed work warns that one-to-one negotiations like these can overpay owners because the owners know their true closure costs and the government does not; auctions are one proposed fix [4].

**Saskatchewan and the Atlantic provinces.** In June 2025 Saskatchewan said it would extend the lives of its coal plants despite the federal deadline (SECONDARY, date only) [25]. Nova Scotia and New Brunswick still burned coal in 2023 [17].

## Energy-security dependencies (gas, oil, uranium, critical minerals, grid)

Canada's exposure is to its buyer, not its suppliers. The figures below are federal data.

- **Oil.** In 2023 Canada exported an average of 4.0 million barrels a day, nearly 80% of its production, almost all to the United States; 61% went to the US Midwest [18] [GOV-flag: Canada Energy Regulator; it has little reason to distort volumes, which are metered at pipelines; risk low].
- **Gas.** In 2023 Canada exported 8.1 Bcf/d of natural gas and imported 2.5 Bcf/d, almost all by pipeline to and from the United States [18] [GOV-flag: same source; risk low]. Liquefied natural gas exports to Asia were only starting [18].
- **Uranium.** Canada was the world's second-largest uranium producer in 2022, at about 15% of world output. It produced 11,373 t in 2023 and exports about 85% [18] [GOV-flag: Canada Energy Regulator citing producer data; mild incentive to stress Canada's strategic weight; risk low].
- **Critical minerals.** The 2022 Critical Minerals Strategy lists 31 critical minerals and puts six first: lithium, graphite, nickel, cobalt, copper and rare earth elements. It also says Canada wants to avoid the dependence on "non-like-minded countries" that Europe suffered with gas [19] [GOV-flag: a promotional strategy document; incentive to overstate opportunity and investment figures, so its dollar figures are not used here].
- **Grid.** All of Canada's electricity trade is with the United States, over 1,522 km of international lines. Net exports fell 46%, from 51.3 TWh in 2022 to 27.6 TWh in 2023, because of dry weather [18] [GOV-flag: Canada Energy Regulator; risk low; the drop shows that hydro exports depend on rainfall]. Coal units that convert to gas move Alberta and Saskatchewan's power security from coal onto gas [3].

What this means for coal: phasing out coal power does not touch Canada's main energy-security risk, which is that it sells most of its oil, gas and power to a single buyer. It does, however, raise how much the coal provinces depend on gas.

## Health and environmental harm (air pollution mortality, water, mining)

**Air.**

- A peer-reviewed global model links long-term exposure to fine particles (PM2.5) to 8.9 million deaths worldwide in 2015 [10] [GOV-flag: lead authors include Health Canada and US EPA scientists; they have no clear incentive in either direction on a global total, but note that it is a government-led model].
- Health Canada estimates that air pollution above background levels, from all sources, caused 17,400 premature deaths in Canada in 2018 [24] [GOV-flag: Health Canada; a health regulator may lean towards higher burden estimates; the department itself says part of the rise from earlier reports came from changes to its PM2.5 model].
- That estimate is for all sources. No peer-reviewed figure for coal power's share of it was opened for this page (see Gaps).

**Water: the Elk Valley (British Columbia).**

- Teck Coal pleaded guilty in the Provincial Court of British Columbia to two Fisheries Act counts and was sentenced on 26 March 2021 to fines and monetary orders totalling $60 million [15] [GOV-flag: court-imposed amount reported by the prosecutor; risk low].
- In an agreed statement of facts, it admitted that in 2012 it released waste-rock leachate with high selenium into the Upper Fording River and Clode Pond, and calcite that cements riverbeds [15] [GOV-flag: Public Prosecution Service of Canada record of a court sentence; the penalty is fixed by the court, so reporting risk is low].
- The same release says Teck spent about $986 million on water quality from 2011 to 2020 [15] [GOV-flag: a company-reported figure repeated by the prosecutor; incentive to over-report remediation spending; not audited here].
- A peer-reviewed model attributes about 80% of the valley's selenium load to waste-rock volume and about 13% to natural background; that study was part-funded by Teck [5].
- United States Geological Survey scientists found rising selenium trends (35 to 89%, 2005 to 2021) at all five mine-affected sites and evidence of transport over 575 river kilometres into the cross-border Lake Koocanusa and Columbia River system [6] [GOV-flag: a US federal agency studying pollution entering its own country; possible incentive to stress harm; the paper is peer-reviewed and its data are public].

**Water: Alberta's mountain coal.**

- Downstream of Tent Mountain, a mine that closed in 1983 and was partly reclaimed, selenium reached 185 µg/L in a lake below the spoil pile [7].
- Fish in Crowsnest Lake carry 5 to 26 µg/g dry weight of selenium, compared with 0.2 to 2.5 µg/g in 840 fish from 29 reference lakes [9].
- At three reclaimed mines in the McLeod River basin, selenium is still above aquatic-life guidelines decades after closure, and the authors conclude that current reclamation rules are not meeting their goals [8] [GOV-flag for [7][8][9]: authors are Alberta government scientists, and [8][9] were funded by the Alberta government; as the regulator, the province would usually have an incentive to under-report harm, yet these papers report harm, which argues against bias].
- In 2021 a joint federal-provincial review panel, acting as the Alberta Energy Regulator, found the Grassy Mountain project not in the public interest. It found likely significant harm to surface water and to threatened westslope cutthroat trout, and it judged the company's plan to capture 95 to 98% of selenium-rich water unlikely to work [16] [GOV-flag: joint review panel / regulator finding; the panel had an incentive to document residual risk, and the capture rates are the proponent's claim as assessed by the panel, not an independent performance result].

## Policy and law

- **CEPA coal regulations** (SOR/2012-167, amended by SOR/2018-263): 420 t CO2/GWh standard; every unit must meet it by the end of 2029 [11][12].
- **Ontario:** Ending Coal for Cleaner Air Act, 2015, S.O. 2015, c. 25, royal assent 3 December 2015 [13].
- **Alberta:** Climate Leadership Plan, 2015, with coal transition payments from 2017 to 2030 [14].
- **Clean Electricity Regulations** (SOR/2024-263, registered 13 December 2024): annual emission limits based on 65 t/GWh from 2035 to 2049 and 0 t/GWh from 2050 [20]. The impact statement forecasts total benefits of $54.9 billion, costs of $40.3 billion, a net benefit of $14.6 billion, and cuts of 181 to 193 Mt [20] [GOV-flag: Environment and Climate Change Canada's analysis of its own rule; incentive to over-report net benefits; its sensitivity table shows net benefits can turn negative under some assumptions].
- **Canada–Alberta MOU** (27 November 2025): suspend the Clean Electricity Regulations in Alberta immediately, pending a carbon-pricing deal [21]. The **implementation agreement** (15 May 2026) keeps them in abeyance while a constitutional reference is before the Alberta Court of Appeal and any appeal to the Supreme Court. If they are upheld, the two governments will negotiate an equivalency agreement; if they are struck down, they will be repealed [22].
- **Fisheries Act s. 36(3):** bans depositing harmful substances in water frequented by fish. It is the basis of the Teck penalty [15].
- **Audit:** the Commissioner of the Environment and Sustainable Development (2022, Report 1) found that the federal government was not prepared to support a just transition, and that its programmes for coal workers relied on existing tools and fell short [23] [GOV-flag: an independent officer of Parliament auditing government; incentive runs towards finding faults, not hiding them].

## Ethical analysis

Values section. The facts above are not in dispute; how to weigh them is.

1. **Who pays for an early exit?** Alberta paid coal owners about $97 million a year through 2030 [14] [GOV-flag: Alberta budget figure; see Coal fleet section]. Coal workers got programmes that auditors judged short of a just transition [23]. Paying capital more reliably than labour is a choice about fairness, not a technical result. The peer-reviewed critique of negotiated payments [4] adds an efficiency concern to the fairness one.
2. **Consistency at home and abroad.** Canada is phasing out coal power while exporting steelmaking coal [17]. Two defensible readings exist. One (an argument, not sourced here) is that metallurgical coal has few substitutes in steelmaking yet, so exports meet demand that would be filled anyway. The other is that every exported tonne is still burned, and the pollution in Canadian water stays behind [6][15]. Which matters more depends on whether one counts only what happens at home or the whole chain.
3. **Pollution across borders.** Selenium from British Columbia reaches the US part of Lake Koocanusa and the Columbia River [6]. The people downstream did not consent and have little say in British Columbia's mine permits.
4. **Reclamation promises over decades.** Evidence that selenium persists decades after "reclaimed" closure [7][8][9] means today's permits put risks on people who are not yet born. The precautionary principle and plain cost-benefit analysis give different answers on new mines, and the Grassy Mountain panel leaned precautionary [16].
5. **Energy security versus climate rules.** Holding the Clean Electricity Regulations in abeyance in Alberta [21][22] and Saskatchewan's extension [25] put provincial control and grid reliability ahead of a national climate rule. Federalism lets these values conflict openly. The open ethical question is who carries the risk if the bet on gas or extended coal fails.
6. **Trusting the numbers.** Many key figures here come from bodies with a stake in them: departments grading their own rules [12][20], a company reporting its own spending [15], and a funder of the research [5]. That is why each figure carries a flag.

## Solutions

Ratings: Strong (several peer-reviewed or adjudicated lines agree), Moderate (one solid line), Limited (indirect or modelled only), Very limited (proposal or expert opinion). UNTESTED means it has not been tried and evaluated in Canada.

1. **Keep the 2029 federal coal deadline and enforce it through CEPA** rather than case-by-case deals. Rating: **Strong**. Ontario and Alberta show that complete phase-outs are achievable on a fixed date [26][3][13]; the legal tool already exists [11][12].
2. **Use reverse auctions, not bilateral talks, for any future early-closure payments.** Rating: **Limited**, UNTESTED in Canada. The case rests on Germany's experience and economic theory [4], and auctions do not suit markets with only a few plant owners [4].
3. **Make worker transition funding a legal condition of closure, with published results.** Rating: **Moderate**. The audit record shows that the current approach fell short [23]. The specific design is UNTESTED.
4. **Tie coal-mine permits to proven selenium control before approval, not adaptive management afterwards.** Rating: **Strong**. Supported by the Grassy Mountain panel's findings [16], the legacy-mine studies [7][8][9] and the Fisheries Act conviction [15].
5. **Run joint Canada–US monitoring with public data for the Elk–Kootenay system.** Rating: **Moderate**. Peer-reviewed USGS trend work shows such data can detect cross-border transport [6]. A formal binational limit is UNTESTED.
6. **Plan for gas dependence after coal, through storage, interties and non-emitting supply, before units convert.** Rating: **Limited**, UNTESTED. Supported by evidence that gas replaced coal at the margin in Alberta [3] and by the regulator's data on how weather swings hydro trade [18].
7. **Require independent audits of government cost-benefit statements on electricity rules.** Rating: **Very limited**, UNTESTED. The idea follows from the incentive flags above [12][20], and no evaluation of it was found.

## Gaps

- **Ontario health benefits:** the peer-reviewed estimate [2] was not opened, so its figures are UNVERIFIED and left out. A Fraser Institute report on the same question is not peer-reviewed and is excluded.
- **Coal power's share of air-pollution deaths in Canada:** no peer-reviewed national figure was opened. The Health Canada total [24] covers all sources.
- **Alberta compensation totals** beyond the budget record [14] (for example, the commonly reported total of about $1.36 billion over 14 years) come from news and company releases, so they are not used.
- **Teck's coal business has changed owners since 2021.** Current ownership and compliance under the federal Direction were not checked against a record.
- **Uranium mining and critical-minerals harms** (tailings, Indigenous consent) are not covered in depth here, and no peer-reviewed source on them was opened.
- **The Clean Electricity Regulations reference case** is pending. Its outcome will change this page.
