# Coal and energy security: Russia as a coal and gas exporter, and energy as leverage

Date: 2026-11-19

## Summary

Russia is one of the world's largest exporters of coal, gas and oil. Its budget, several coal regions and its foreign policy have all depended on those exports. Peer-reviewed work documents that Russia has used energy supply and pricing to reward and to punish other states, and that it curbed gas supply to Europe in 2021 and 2022. Since 2022, EU sanctions and embargoes have pushed Russian coal and oil towards Asia. A peer-reviewed study finds that the oil embargo forced large discounts on Russian crude, while the price cap had a negligible effect on prices. At home, Russia has no coal phase-out, satellite studies rank it among the largest sources of oil and gas methane leaks and flaring, and air pollution in the main coal region carries measured health risks. Russian official data are hard to check independently, so many figures below carry a [GOV-flag].

## Background

- Russia's economy has long depended on oil and gas rents. Gaddy and Ickes show that swings in those rents have played a key role in Russian economic performance, and that how rents are distributed affects whether production can be sustained [P1].
- In 2023, Russia was the world's second-largest producer of crude oil and of dry gas, the third-largest exporter of coal and of gas, and the fifth-largest coal producer [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA estimates partly rest on Russian reporting and third-party trackers; the agency's home government sanctions Russia, which could bias framing but not obviously the volumes].
- Natural gas supplied 53% of Russia's own primary energy in 2023 and coal 16%. Gas generated 46% of its electricity and coal 15% [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA estimates partly rest on Russian reporting and third-party trackers; the agency's home government sanctions Russia, which could bias framing but not obviously the volumes].
- Climate policy abroad is a direct economic risk. A model study projects that importers' efforts to meet the Paris goals will lower Russia's GDP growth rate by about half a percentage point, and that Russia cannot sustain fossil-export-led growth [P5].
- Peer-reviewed analysis describes Russian energy policy as "business as usual": decarbonisation is "not yet on the horizon" and scepticism about climate change prevails among stakeholders [P4].

## Coal fleet and phase-out record

- Russia produced 529 million short tons of coal in 2023, 4% more than in 2022, and consumed 267 million short tons, 12% less than in 2022 [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA; production data originate largely from Russian sources, which have an incentive to show resilience under sanctions].
- The Kuzbass (Kuznetsk Basin) in Western Siberia produces more than half of Russian coal [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA, citing a non-governmental mine tracker].
- Coal is the "backbone of several regional economies and local communities". After EU sanctions, exporters tried to speed up their shift east and coal infrastructure is being expanded for Asia-Pacific markets [P3].
- Overland and Loginova conclude that the industry "is not preparing for more long-term changes in international coal markets", which raises risks for coal communities inside Russia and for global decarbonisation [P3].
- In 2021, 52% of Russian coal exports already went to Asia and Oceania, so the post-2022 shift built on an existing trend [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA estimates partly rest on Russian reporting and third-party trackers; the agency's home government sanctions Russia, which could bias framing but not obviously the volumes].
- Phase-out record: no national coal phase-out date was found in any source opened for this case. The 2020 Energy Strategy to 2035, its 2025 successor to 2050, and the 2021 climate law could not be fully opened (UNVERIFIED, see R5-R7). GAP: no peer-reviewed study of Russian coal-plant retirement plans was found.
- Mine safety is a second coal harm. Peer-reviewed data on Russian mine fatalities were not found. GAP.

## Energy-security dependencies

Russia is mainly a supplier, so its energy-security story runs in two directions: other states' dependence on Russia, and Russia's dependence on export revenue and on Western technology.

**Gas.**
- In 2021 the EU imported about 155 bcm of gas from Russia, around 45% of its gas imports and almost 40% of its gas consumption [R4, GOV-flag: IEA, an agency of consumer countries with an interest in stressing supply risk; figures not audited here].
- Russia curbed gas supply to Europe in 2021 and 2022. German consumers cut use by 23% below a temperature-adjusted baseline in the second half of 2022; industry began cutting as early as September 2021 [P6].
- Transit through Ukraine was a recurring risk. Le Coq and Paltseva compare gas transit risks for Russia and the EU [P8; findings beyond the title UNVERIFIED, full text not opened].
- Europe remained Russia's primary gas market as of 2024, because of pipeline links, limited EU bans on Russian LNG and demand from non-EU states [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA estimates partly rest on Russian reporting and third-party trackers; the agency's home government sanctions Russia, which could bias framing but not obviously the volumes].

**Oil.**
- The 2022 EU embargo forced Russia to accept a $32/bbl Urals discount in March 2023 compared with January 2022. Nearly half came from longer shipping routes to India; the rest ($17/bbl) is explained by India's greater bargaining power. The G7 price cap's effect on Russian export prices in March 2023 was "negligible" [P7].
- India became the largest buyer of Russian crude between 2023 and 2024 (34% of exports in 2024), ahead of China (26%) [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA estimates partly rest on Russian reporting and third-party trackers; the agency's home government sanctions Russia, which could bias framing but not obviously the volumes].

**Uranium, critical minerals and grid.** GAP: no peer-reviewed source was opened on Russian nuclear fuel or critical-mineral exports as leverage. This case makes no claim on them.

**Russia's own dependence.** Analysts describe growing technological isolation and financial constraints as the main near-term risks to the Russian energy sector [P4]; climate policy abroad threatens the export model itself [P5].

**Energy as leverage.**
- Newnham traces Russian energy leverage to the integrated networks of the former USSR and Warsaw Pact. Some states (Armenia, Belarus, and Ukraine before 2005) received heavily subsidised energy; others (Georgia, Moldova, the Baltic states, and Ukraine after 2005) faced supply disruptions and punitive price increases [P2].
- Smith Stegen sets out a framework for testing whether Russia's "energy weapon" works against Europe [P9; findings beyond the title UNVERIFIED, full text not opened].
- The 2021-22 supply curbs [P6] are the largest documented episode. This case does not assign motive beyond what the cited studies state.

## Health and environmental harm

- **Air pollution in coal regions.** A 2025 risk assessment of Kemerovo and Novokuznetsk, using 2022-24 data from 12 state monitoring posts, found unacceptable non-cancer and cancer risks. Benzo(a)pyrene, formaldehyde and nickel compounds were priority pollutants, contributing 25-89% of non-cancer risk; respiratory and blood diseases and congenital defects were priority disease groups [P12]. Limits: this is a modelled risk assessment, not a count of deaths, and it rests on state monitoring data [GOV-flag: Russian monitoring agencies, which run the federal clean-air programme being evaluated and so have an incentive to under-report].
- **Mortality.** GAP: no peer-reviewed estimate of deaths from Russian coal or gas pollution was opened that met this case's source rules.
- **Methane.** In a 2019-20 satellite survey, oil and gas "ultra-emitters" were concentrated in the largest basins worldwide and made up 8-12% of global oil and gas methane emissions reported in national inventories, a contribution not included in most current inventories [P10]. The published paper ranks Turkmenistan highest, at 1.3 million tonnes of methane a year, followed by Russia, the United States (excluding the Permian basin), Iran, Kazakhstan and Algeria. A published country total for Russia was not given in the open text (UNVERIFIED). Limits: satellite coverage over Russia was incomplete. An open preprint of the same study had ranked Russia first, at about 1.5 million tonnes a year; that preprint figure is not used here.
- **Flaring.** Russia flared more gas than any other country in 2023, over 1 trillion cubic feet, 19% of the world total [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA, citing satellite estimates; the agency's home government sanctions Russia, which could bias framing]. Satellite flaring estimates are rated at about +/-9.5% accuracy [P11], which makes them an independent check on official figures.
- **Water and spills.** After a diesel fuel spill in Norilsk in May 2020, hydrocarbon pollution in river sediments spread 31 km downstream but did not enter Pyasino Lake [P13]. That the fuel came from a power-plant tank, and the size of any court damages award, are reported in news only and were not opened (UNVERIFIED).
- **Mining.** GAP: no peer-reviewed study of coal-mine water pollution or land damage in the Kuzbass was opened.

## Policy and law

- **EU sanctions (records).** The EU banned imports of Russian coal in its fifth sanctions package of April 2022 [R1] and seaborne Russian crude and oil products in its sixth package of June 2022 [R2]. Both texts could not be opened because the EU law portal blocked access (UNVERIFIED). The US EIA reports that the EU's 18th package (July 2025) lowered the oil price cap [R3, GOV-flag: US EIA; the agency's home government sanctions Russia, which could bias framing of sanctions, though this is a legal-record report].
- **Effect of sanctions.** The best peer-reviewed evidence found is on oil: the embargo cut Russian export prices; the price cap did not, at least to March 2023 [P7]. GAP: no peer-reviewed study of the coal ban's effect on Russian coal revenue was opened.
- **Russian policy (records).** The Energy Strategy to 2035 [R5] could not be opened (UNVERIFIED). Official publication records show it was succeeded by Order No. 908-r of 12 April 2025, identified in legal-catalog records as the Energy Strategy to 2050 [R7, UNVERIFIED: order number and date confirmed from the official catalog; the full text was not opened, so no figures are taken from it]. The 2021 federal law on limiting greenhouse gas emissions [R6] could not be opened (UNVERIFIED). Peer-reviewed analysis finds Russia relies on fossil fuels and has kept solar and wind marginal [P4].
- **Courts and regulators.** No court, regulator or audit finding against a named Russian energy company was opened for this case, so none is named. GAP: arbitration awards on gas supply contracts are reported in news but were not opened.

## Ethical analysis

**Facts first (from the sections above).**
- Energy exports fund the Russian state and several coal regions [P1, P3].
- Supply and price have been used to reward and punish other states [P2], and supply to Europe was curbed in 2021-22 [P6].
- Importers responded fast: German gas use fell 23% below baseline [P6], and the EU cut Russian coal and oil [R1-R2, UNVERIFIED texts].
- Sanctions redirected rather than stopped exports: coal and oil moved east, with large oil discounts [P3, P7, R3].
- Methane leaks, flaring and coal-region air pollution impose costs inside Russia and on the global climate [P10-P12].

**Where people disagree.**
- *Is energy leverage a weapon or ordinary commerce?* One reading treats subsidies and cut-offs as coercion [P2]. Another treats price rises for some neighbours as a move from subsidised to market pricing, with disruption as a side effect of transit disputes [P8, UNVERIFIED content]. The cited evidence supports the existence of differential treatment; it does not settle intent in each case.
- *Do sanctions work?* The oil embargo lowered Russian prices [P7]; the price cap's measured price effect was negligible [P7]. Supporters argue sanctions still raise costs; critics argue they shift trade to buyers such as India and China and strengthen those buyers' bargaining power [P7, R3].
- *Climate versus security.* Replacing Russian gas pushed some European demand back towards coal [P3]. Some see this as a short-term cost of security; others see it as locking in emissions.
- *Who bears the cost of decline?* Coal communities in Russia face risks that the industry is not preparing for [P3].

**Values (separate from the facts above).**
- *Self-determination:* using supply to pressure another state's political choices conflicts with its right to choose freely. This is a value judgement, not a finding.
- *Responsibility to third parties:* households in importing states, and coal communities in Russia, bear costs of decisions they did not make.
- *Intergenerational duty:* expanding coal export capacity for decades conflicts with climate goals most states have accepted.
- *Fairness of sanctions:* sanctions aim at a state but also fall on ordinary workers and on importers' poorer households; how to weigh that is contested.
