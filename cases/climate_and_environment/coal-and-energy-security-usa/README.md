# Coal and energy security in the United States

Date: 2026-11-19

## Summary

Coal's share of United States utility-scale electricity generation fell from 33.2% in 2015 to 15.1% in 2024, then rose to 16.6% in 2025. [GOV-flag: EIA generation statistics from mandatory plant reports; physical output can be cross-checked against continuous emissions monitors, which limits the incentive to misstate kilowatthours, but series can be revised.] Peer-reviewed decompositions of the earlier decline attribute most of the lost coal production and most eastern-interconnection plant retirements to lower natural-gas prices and electricity-demand conditions, not to environmental rules. Those rules, together with retirements, nonetheless cut coal-related fine-particle exposure sharply. In the Medicare population aged 65 and older, a 2023 national study estimated 460,000 deaths attributable to coal-plant sulfur-related fine particles in 1999–2020, falling to about 1,600 in 2020. The country has been a net total-energy exporter since 2019 and a net coal exporter for decades, but it remains a net crude-oil importer, buys most reactor uranium and enrichment services abroad (including a still-large Russian share of enrichment), and is highly import-reliant for several minerals used in non-coal electricity technologies. [GOV-flag: EIA and USGS trade, uranium and mineral figures are official survey or apparent-consumption products; cargo origin, scrap and inventory definitions can be framed to emphasize or downplay import risk.] Remaining oversight problems include residual exposure inequities, leaking coal-ash storage, and inconclusive evidence on community health effects of mountaintop mining. A 2025 two-year exemption from the 2024 tightened mercury and air-toxics particle limits at specified plants was later overtaken by a 24 February 2026 final rule, effective 27 April 2026, that repealed those tightened filterable-particle and related 2024 amendments.

## Background

Coal remains a sizable, declining, and recently uneven source of United States electricity. In 2025 it supplied 734,928 thousand megawatthours of utility-scale generation out of 4,425,029 thousand, compared with 652,156 of 4,308,634 in 2024 and 1,352,398 of 4,078,714 in 2015 (EIA, Tables 1.1 and 3.1.A). Natural gas was the largest source in 2025 (1,811,004 thousand megawatthours, 40.9%), up from 32.7% in 2015 (1,334,668 of 4,078,714 thousand megawatthours). Utility-scale solar generation rose from 24,893 thousand megawatthours in 2015 to 300,303 in 2025 (EIA, Table 3.1.A). [GOV-flag: Forms EIA-923 and EIA-860 are mandatory industry reports. Generation and capacity can be cross-checked against continuous emissions monitors and market settlements, which limits the incentive to misstate physical output, but operators and agencies may time or revise retirement and forecast series for commercial or political reasons.]

The earlier collapse of coal output coincided with cheaper gas from shale production, slower electricity demand growth, and tighter air rules. Coglianese, Gerarden and Stock (2020) estimate that the decline in the price of natural gas relative to coal explains 92% (standard error 2.5 percentage points) of the 2008–2016 drop in coal production, and that environmental regulations, mainly the Cross-State Air Pollution Rule and the Mercury and Air Toxics Standards (MATS), explain an additional 6% (standard error 2.2 points). Linn and McCormack (2019), using a unit-level model of the eastern interconnection, find that electricity consumption and natural-gas prices account for nearly all of the coal-plant profitability losses and resulting retirements in that system, and that environmental regulations had little effect on those retirement outcomes. Public debate has often attributed the same decline mainly to regulation. The opened peer-reviewed decompositions do not support that as the dominant cause for 2005–2016. They also do not imply that the rules failed to cut pollution: sulfur and nitrogen-oxide emissions from the electricity sector fell sharply over the same years (Linn and McCormack 2019 report a two-thirds decline in those pollutants between 2005 and 2015), and later health studies attribute large exposure reductions to scrubber installation and then to retirements.

Other utility-scale sources in 2025 included nuclear (785,304 thousand megawatthours, 17.7%), wind (461,577, 10.4%), and solar (300,303, 6.8%), plus an estimated 93,041 thousand megawatthours of small-scale solar (EIA, Tables 1.1 and 3.1.A). [GOV-flag: same Forms EIA-923 and EIA-861M; small-scale solar is partly estimated, which can lead to later revision of that series.]

The cause of the 12.7% rise in coal generation in 2025 is GAP in the peer-reviewed sources opened for this case. [GOV-flag: 12.7% is the official EIA Table 1.1 percentage change, from the same mandatory generation reports; limited incentive to misstate physical output.] Coglianese, Gerarden and Stock (2020) also tested renewable portfolio standards and other factors for 2008–2016 and report that those contributions were small and offsetting beside the gas-price and regulation terms.

## Coal fleet and phase-out record

At year-end 2025 the utility-scale coal fleet comprised 448 generators with 182,490.1 MW of nameplate capacity and 168,598.5 MW of net summer capacity, out of 1,283,803.8 MW of total utility-scale summer capacity (EIA, Table 4.3). [GOV-flag: Form EIA-860 is self-reported by plant owners; capacity is usually verifiable, but primary-fuel classification and planned-retirement dates can be revised.]

Coglianese, Gerarden and Stock (2020), citing EIA production statistics, report that United States coal production fell 37%, from 1,172 million short tons in 2008 to 739 million in 2016, and that coal-mining employment, as measured by the Bureau of Labor Statistics and by EIA, fell from 87,000 to 52,000 over those years. [GOV-flag: employment and mine-output series depend on survey definitions and establishment coverage; they are widely reused in political argument, which creates an incentive to emphasize or downplay particular years, but the direction of the 2008–2016 decline is consistent across those official series.]

Coal's 2025 share of net summer capacity was 13.1% (168,598.5 of 1,283,803.8 MW), below its 16.6% share of utility-scale generation, which is consistent with remaining units still producing energy rather than standing idle as reserve only. [GOV-flag: as for Table 4.3.] No opened peer-reviewed source establishes a complete, dated national phase-out path aligned with a stated climate target. A large residual fleet remains in service, and 2025 generation moved up, not down. Opened sources do not include a regulator reliability assessment of planned coal retirements; that official record is GAP.

## Energy-security dependencies (gas, oil, uranium, critical minerals, grid)

The United States has been an annual net total-energy exporter since 2019, after imports peaked in 2007 (EIA, *Imports and exports*). It has been a net coal exporter since at least 1949 and a net natural-gas exporter since 2017, with gas exports at a record in 2024. It remained a net crude-oil importer in 2024; crude oil was 67% of total energy imports that year. [GOV-flag: trade balances are compiled from customs and survey filings. Firms have some incentive to classify cargoes in ways that affect tariffs or sanctions, but the long-run exporter/importer status of these aggregates is not in serious dispute in the opened record.]

As coal retired, the electricity system became more dependent on natural gas for bulk and peak supply (40.9% of 2025 utility-scale generation). That substitution improved average air quality, documented below, but it ties power reliability to pipeline scheduling and winter gas deliverability. Opened peer-reviewed work does not provide a recent national quantification of gas-shortage outages; that is a GAP.

Civilian reactor operators remain heavily dependent on foreign uranium and enrichment. In 2025 they purchased 46,871 thousand pounds U3O8 equivalent, of which 43,495 thousand (92.8%) was foreign-origin and 3,376 thousand (7.2%) was domestic-origin (EIA, Uranium Marketing Annual Report, Table 3). Purchases of enrichment services were 12,710 thousand separative work units (SWU), of which 9,780 thousand (77%) were foreign, including 3,284 thousand (26%) of Russian origin, and 2,930 thousand (23%) were of United States origin (Table 16). [GOV-flag: Form EIA-858 is a survey of reactor operators. After sanctions and the 2024 import prohibition, origin classification, withholding, and waiver-dependent Russian volumes may be sensitive; several country cells are withheld to avoid disclosing individual firms.]

Public Law 118-62 (13 May 2024) prohibits, beginning 90 days after enactment, imports of unirradiated low-enriched uranium produced in the Russian Federation or by a Russian entity, with waivers by the Secretary of Energy, in consultation with other departments, if no viable alternative is available to keep a reactor or a United States nuclear company operating, or if importation is in the national interest. The prohibition is set to terminate on 31 December 2040. Russian-origin enrichment was still 26% of 2025 purchases in the EIA survey, so the security effect of the statute is not yet established in opened outcome evidence.

Minerals used in batteries, magnets, and other non-coal equipment are a separate dependency. USGS estimated 2025 net import reliance as 100% for natural graphite, more than 50% for lithium, 79% for cobalt, 67% for rare-earth compounds and metals (down from more than 95% in 2021–2022), and 41% for nickel, or nearly 100% if scrap is excluded. For 2021–2024, USGS reports that 71% of rare-earth compound and metal import sources were China, and that China produced an estimated 82% of world natural graphite in 2025. Lithium import sources in 2021–2024 were reported as Chile 54% and Argentina 43%. [GOV-flag: net-import-reliance ratios depend on how scrap, inventories, and apparent consumption are defined, and the figures are commonly used to support domestic-mining policy, which creates an incentive to emphasize import risk. USGS marks 2025 figures as estimated.]

## Health and environmental harm (air pollution mortality, water, mining)

Henneman et al. (2023a) linked fine-particle pollution associated with sulfur dioxide from 480 coal electricity-generating units to individual Medicare death records covering more than 650 million person-years. A 1 μg/m³ increase in annual coal PM2.5 was associated with a relative risk of 1.0125 (95% CI 1.0113–1.0137) for all-cause mortality, about 2.1 times the risk they compare with total PM2.5 in the same cohort. They estimate 460,000 excess Medicare deaths (95% CI 420,000–500,000) in 1999–2020 relative to zero coal PM2.5, equal to 1.2% of Medicare deaths in the period, and to 25% of all PM2.5-related Medicare deaths before 2009 and 7% after 2012. Annual excess deaths averaged more than 43,000 in 1999–2007 (390,000 in total; 95% CI 360,000–430,000) and fell to 1,600 (95% CI 1,400–1,700) in 2020. Alternative risk functions used in earlier assessments produced 200,000 or 240,000 deaths. Average ZIP-code coal PM2.5 fell from 2.34 μg/m³ in 1999 to 0.07 μg/m³ in 2020. Most deaths were associated with a minority of high-emitting plants near population centers; large drops followed scrubber installation and retirement (for one Pennsylvania facility, average annual attributable deaths fell from 640 before 2008 to 80 after 2009–2010 scrubbers).

Limits of that mortality evidence are material and should be read with the point estimates. The cohort is ages 65 and older, so total deaths are understated. Coal PM2.5 is defined from sulfur dioxide, so other coal particles are not fully captured. Exposure is estimated with a reduced-complexity transport model. Baseline death rates for 2017–2020 are projected from 2014–2016. The authors cannot rule out unmeasured confounding; their E-value is 1.125, which is modest.

Henneman et al. (2023b) find that nationwide population-weighted coal PM2.5 fell from 1.96 μg/m³ in 1999 to 0.06 μg/m³ in 2020, that most of the 2007–2010 reduction came from sulfur-dioxide scrubbers, and that most of the later reduction came from retirements. Black populations in the South and North Central regions and Native American populations in the West were inequitably exposed early in the period. Overall equity improved as emissions fell, but the study reports that some inequity remains for Black populations exposed to North Central plants and for Native populations exposed to western plants.

Bekbulat, Cromar and Marshall (2025) examine a presidential proclamation signed 8 April 2025 and published 21 April 2025 (90 Fed. Reg. 16777) that granted a two-year exemption, running 8 July 2027 to 8 July 2029, from the 2024 MATS filterable-PM limit of 0.010 lb/MMBtu (89 Fed. Reg. 38508) to plants of 47 companies. They estimate that 83% of the exempted facilities already operated below the new limit, and that additional emissions from the rest would cause 32 deaths (95% CI 22–43) over two years, with sensitivity results ranging from 47% lower to 169% higher. Hazardous-air-pollutant and non-mortality effects were not quantified. A later EPA final rule, published 24 February 2026 and effective 27 April 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 9088), repealed the 2024 revised filterable-PM standard and related 2024 MATS amendments, so the modeled exemption is a historical counterfactual relative to a standard that was not in force after that effective date.

Harkness, Sulkin and Vengosh (2016) report geochemical evidence that coal-ash ponds leaked to adjacent surface water and shallow groundwater at sites in five states. Boron and strontium exceeded their background thresholds at all sites; 65 of 165 monitoring wells had high boron, and 49 had high coal-ash contaminant levels. They also report Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) counts, from the coal-combustion residuals (CCR) rule record, of 40 confirmed and 113 potential damage cases, 60% of them water contamination from surface impoundments. [GOV-flag: confirmed damage cases depend on reporting and legal criteria; regulated owners have an incentive not to volunteer damage, so confirmed counts may be low.] National, post-2016 evidence on how later CCR amendments changed leakage is GAP.

A National Toxicology Program systematic review (Boyles et al. 2017) found that mountaintop-removal mining releases particles, metals and other substances to air and water, and it reported prior work showing substantially more public-drinking-water violations per system in mountaintop-mining counties, often for failure to sample. It could not reach conclusions on community health effects because of risk of bias in the human studies, especially exposure characterization, confounding, and overlapping study populations. Experimental work suggested biological plausibility, but the reviewers judged it too limited for definitive inference.

## Policy and law

The Clean Air Act authorizes, among other things, national ambient air-quality standards, the Title IV sulfur-dioxide allowance program, interstate transport rules, and hazardous-air-pollutant standards under section 112, the authority cited for MATS. Section 111 directs EPA to base performance standards on the “best system of emission reduction … that has been adequately demonstrated.”

In *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022), the Supreme Court held that Congress did not grant EPA authority under section 111(d) to set emissions caps for existing plants based on the grid-level “generation shifting” approach used in the 2015 Clean Power Plan. Source-level measures such as heat-rate improvements were distinguished from a system that would have required shifting generation from coal to gas or renewables. The decision limits one statutory path for a federal coal-generation cap. It does not repeal MATS, interstate transport rules, or the CCR disposal rule.

EPA tightened the MATS filterable-PM limit for coal units to 0.010 lb/MMBtu in a 7 May 2024 final rule (89 Fed. Reg. 38508); a presidential proclamation signed 8 April 2025 and published 21 April 2025 (90 Fed. Reg. 16777) then granted the two-year exemptions, beginning 8 July 2027, modeled by Bekbulat, Cromar and Marshall (2025). On 24 February 2026 EPA published a final rule, effective 27 April 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 9088), repealing that revised filterable-PM standard, the associated compliance-demonstration amendments, and the 2024 revised mercury standard for lignite-fired units. Public Law 117-169 (16 August 2022), 136 Stat. 1818, enacted a clean-electricity production credit (26 U.S.C. § 45Y), a clean-electricity investment credit (26 U.S.C. § 48E), and a zero-emission nuclear production credit (26 U.S.C. § 45U). Public Law 118-62, described above, restricts Russian low-enriched uranium. Opened sources do not establish the later administrative status of every 2024 power-sector greenhouse-gas rule; that status remains GAP.

The Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act and the 2015 coal-combustion residuals rule are part of the mining and waste legal setting. Their full texts were not opened for this case, except as discussed in Harkness, Sulkin and Vengosh (2016) and Boyles et al. (2017), and are therefore GAP as primary records.

## Ethical analysis

Facts. The opened health evidence shows a large historical mortality burden from coal-plant sulfur-related particles in older adults, a steep decline after controls and retirements, and a much smaller but non-zero residual burden in 2020. Alternative risk functions produce smaller totals, but not a null finding. Exposure reductions improved average equity and still left documented residual inequities. Mining and ash disposal have documented water-quality and monitoring failures; causal community-health conclusions for mountaintop mining are not established. The decline of coal has also been accompanied by large officially recorded losses of mining employment. Official trade and survey records show that the country is a net coal exporter, remains a net crude-oil importer, and purchases most reactor uranium, enrichment services, and several listed minerals from abroad. Opened peer-reviewed sources do not establish a net shift in energy-security risk, or a ranking of residual coal against import-disruption risk.

Strength of evidence. The mortality and exposure studies are national in scope, peer-reviewed, and internally consistent on the direction of change, with stated limitations (age scope, sulfur-based exposure, reduced-form transport, modest E-value). The economic decompositions are period-specific (roughly 2005–2016) and should not be extrapolated uncritically to 2025. Government energy, uranium and mineral figures are official statistical or survey products and carry the reporting incentives flagged above. The 2025 MATS-exemption mortality estimate is a timely model, not an observed death count, and is sensitive to the concentration-response choice.

Disagreements. One view holds that environmental regulation was the main reason coal declined and that loosening rules would restore the fleet and improve security. The opened peer-reviewed decompositions find that relative fuel prices and demand, not those rules, explain most 2005–2016 production and retirement outcomes, which implies that rolling back rules would do little to restore coal while risking a reversal of documented exposure gains. Another view treats remaining coal as a reliability reserve against gas-pipeline and renewable-resource shortfalls. Opened sources confirm the system’s growing gas share and a 2025 uptick in coal generation, but they do not quantify a reliability gap that only coal can fill. A third view treats import dependence on uranium and battery and magnet minerals as a reason to retain coal. That is a values judgment, not a result demonstrated in the opened studies: those studies do not compare the health and climate burden of residual coal with the disruption risk of mineral or fuel imports.

### Values

The following judgments are values, not findings. Whether residual coal-plant deaths in older adults, and residual inequities of exposure, are acceptable in exchange for dispatchable capacity is a weighting of health against reliability and cost. Whether a society should accept concentrated import dependence for minerals used to replace coal is a weighting of combustion harm against supply-chain risk. How much public compensation is due to mining communities that lost employment because gas undercut coal is a distributive choice; the employment decline is a recorded fact, but no opened source establishes a unique just payment. Intergenerational climate harm from remaining coal carbon dioxide is ethically relevant and is not quantified in the sources opened for this case (GAP).

## Solutions

Each item is tied to a failure identified above.

Ratings use the required scale: Strong, Moderate, Limited, or Very limited. UNTESTED means the proposal has not been evaluated as a complete policy in opened outcome evidence.

1. Maintain and enforce sulfur, nitrogen-oxide and hazardous-air-pollutant controls, including scrubbers and the MATS particle limits that remain in force, at operating coal units. This addresses the historical mortality burden. The 2025 exemption from the later-repealed 2024 particle limit is not the operative legal setting after 27 April 2026. Evidence for past controls is **Strong**: Henneman et al. (2023a, 2023b) attribute most of the 2007–2010 exposure drop to scrubber installation and document plant-level mortality declines after controls.

2. Retire remaining high-harm units on announced schedules, with replacement capacity in place. This addresses residual deaths concentrated at a minority of plants. Health evidence for past retirements is **Strong** (Henneman et al. 2023a, 2023b). Evidence that any particular future retirement path preserves reliability is **Limited** (not quantified in opened sources). A formal ranking-based retirement mandate is **UNTESTED**.

3. Do not rely on loosening air rules as a coal-revival or energy-security policy. This addresses the mismatch between public claims and the opened decompositions. Evidence is **Moderate** for the 2005–2016 period (Coglianese, Gerarden and Stock 2020; Linn and McCormack 2019). Application to 2025–2026 markets is **UNTESTED**.

4. Do not treat time-limited exemptions, or later repeal, of tightened MATS particle limits as costless for health. This addresses the 2025 proclamation and the 2026 repeal of the 2024 limit. Bekbulat, Cromar and Marshall (2025) provide a **Moderate** modeled estimate of added deaths from a two-year delay of the 2024 limit; the estimate is not an observed outcome, omits hazardous-air-pollutant effects, and is a counterfactual relative to a standard later repealed (91 Fed. Reg. 9088). Outcome evidence on the health effect of the 2026 repeal itself is **Limited** and **UNTESTED** in opened sources.

5. Close, line, or excavate leaking coal-ash impoundments and complete groundwater corrective action. This addresses documented pond leakage. Site-level geochemical evidence of leakage is **Moderate** (Harkness, Sulkin and Vengosh 2016). National effectiveness of later CCR implementation is **Limited** and partly **UNTESTED** in opened post-2016 research.

6. Diversify uranium conversion and enrichment so that a single foreign supplier is not a large share of SWU. This addresses the 2025 Russian enrichment share and the incomplete effect, so far, of Public Law 118-62. The statute exists; outcome evidence that it has reduced security risk is **Very limited** and **UNTESTED**.

7. Reduce concentration in mineral processing for graphite, rare earths, lithium, cobalt and nickel, with transparent accounting of scrap and inventories. This addresses USGS-reported import reliance. Policy instruments in Public Law 117-169 exist. Outcome evidence that they have lowered disruption risk is **Limited** and, as a complete strategy, **UNTESTED**.

8. Improve gas-electric operational coordination so that gas can replace coal without correlating generator outages in winter. This addresses the security side of the coal-to-gas shift. Opened peer-reviewed quantification of current shortage causes is **GAP**; recommended contract or coordination reforms are **UNTESTED** in this case file.

9. Fund independent economic adjustment in mining communities, separate from claims that restoring coal combustion is the only remedy for job loss. This addresses the recorded 2008–2016 employment decline. The decline is documented; evidence on which adjustment programs work is **Very limited** here and **UNTESTED** in the opened set.

10. Carbon capture on remaining coal units is sometimes proposed as a way to keep plants for security while cutting carbon dioxide. Opened sources for this case do not evaluate that trade-off. Rating: **Very limited**, **UNTESTED**.

## Gaps

- Cause of the 2025 increase in coal generation, including any role of higher demand, fuel prices, or delayed retirements.
- Mortality in people under 65, and total deaths from the residual post-2020 coal fleet.
- A causal estimate of community health effects of mountaintop-removal mining (the 2017 systematic review could not reach one).
- National, post-2016 measurement of coal-ash groundwater contamination after later CCR amendments.
- Health or reliability outcomes of the 24 February 2026 MATS repeal (effective 27 April 2026) were not opened.
- Opened, peer-reviewed quantification of electricity-reliability effects of remaining coal retirements, and of gas-fuel shortages at power plants.
- Outcome evaluation of Public Law 118-62 and of Public Law 117-169 credits as energy-security measures.
- United States-specific, peer-reviewed quantification of climate damages from the remaining coal fleet (not opened here).
- Independent audit of planned-retirement reporting on Form EIA-860 against realized retirements.
- Occupational lung disease and injury in current underground and surface coal mining (not opened here).
- A regulator (for example, a reliability-organization) assessment of resource adequacy after further coal retirements, which was not opened for this case.
