# China: coal and energy security

Date: 2026-11-19

Author: anonymous

## Summary

China burns more coal than any other country, and coal still supplied 53.2% of its total energy use in 2024 [GOV-flag: national statistics; incentive to under-report, because coal-share and energy-intensity targets bind officials] (R1).
Its official coal and emissions numbers have moved a lot between editions. Peer-reviewed work on the 2015 revisions of official energy statistics finds they imply 925 MtCO2 (11.2%) higher emissions for 2013 and 7.6 GtCO2 (9.2%) higher total emissions for 2000-2013 (P3) [GOV-flag: same incentive]. A news report put the 2012 coal-use revision at roughly 600 million tonnes; that figure is SECONDARY and was not checked against the official yearbook (S1).
At the same time, measured Chinese coal emission factors run about 40% below the default values, so the "true" emissions number depends on which correction you apply (P1).
Coal-plant approvals surged after approval power moved to provinces in late 2014 (P16). An NGO tracker that is not peer-reviewed also reports a later rise in permits after the 2021-22 power shortages; that count is SECONDARY and is not used as analysis (S2).
Coal-related air pollution remains a leading cause of early death (P8, P9, P10). Mining adds methane, water stress and workplace deaths (P12, P13, P14, P15).
Evidence strength: the data-quality findings and the health findings are Strong (several independent peer-reviewed methods agree). The causes of the 2021-22 shortages and the outlook for a coal phase-out are Moderate to Limited.

## Background

- China's energy statistics come from a top-down system run by the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS), which publishes both national and provincial energy figures (P2).
- For 2010, CO2 calculated from the national data and from the sum of the provincial data differed by 1.4 Gt. Most of the gap came from coal used in coal washing and manufacturing (P2). [GOV-flag: provinces had an incentive to over-report output and growth; the centre had an incentive to show lower energy intensity]
- The 2021 NDC commits to "dual control" of total energy use and energy intensity (R2). Local officials' careers depend on growth and on targets such as safety targets (P15, P16), which gives an incentive to under-report energy use.
- In September 2020 the head of state announced at the UN General Assembly that China would peak CO2 before 2030 and reach carbon neutrality before 2060 (R2).
- In September 2025 China announced its 2035 NDC: cut economy-wide net greenhouse-gas emissions by 7-10% from the peak. This is its first absolute reduction target (R7). [GOV-flag: the baseline is a future peak that China's own statistics will measure, so there is an incentive to report a high peak]

## Coal fleet and phase-out record

- **Capacity.** At the end of 2024, thermal capacity (mostly coal) was 1,444.45 GW out of 3,348.62 GW in total. Wind was 520.68 GW and grid-connected solar 886.66 GW (R1). [GOV-flag: installed capacity is a physical stock and is harder to misstate; wind and solar may be over-reported against targets, and the "thermal" category mixes coal with gas and biomass]
- **The 2015 approval surge.** Ren et al. used approval records from 2013 to 2016. They found coal-power approval rates were about three times higher once approval authority moved from the centre to provinces in late 2014, and higher still in coal-producing provinces. They estimate local coal production accounted for about 54 GW of the 2015 approvals, roughly a quarter of the year's total (P16).
- **The mechanism.** Guaranteed operating hours and administered tariffs made coal investment close to risk-free. The central approval gate was the main brake (P16). This is an incentive finding, not a finding that any named party broke the law.
- **The 2022-23 approval surge.** An NGO tracker that is not peer-reviewed reports about 218 GW of coal power permitted between the start of 2022 and the end of 2023, and at least 106 GW in 2023 alone (S2). SECONDARY, timeline only. A news report with the 2022 figure could not be opened (paywall, HTTP 401) and is not relied on.
- **Phase-out pledges.** The 2030 Action Plan says coal consumption growth is to be "strictly controlled" in the 14th Five-Year Plan (2021-25) and coal is to move toward a role of "ensuring basic needs" and "system regulation" (R3). The 2024 Energy Law, Art. 28, gives coal a "basic guarantee and system-regulating role" (R6). Neither text sets a date for a coal phase-out.
- **Retirement research.** A plant-by-plant study of 1,037 operating plants found 18% scored poorly on technical, economic and environmental grounds. Retiring those first would allow a complete phase-out by 2045 (1.5 °C path) or 2055 (well below 2 °C) (P21). This is a model result. UNTESTED.
- **Earlier targets.** The 2013 Air Pollution Action Plan set a goal of coal below 65% of total energy use by 2017 (R4). [GOV-flag: progress toward the target was measured by the same statistics system the target judged]

## Energy-security dependencies (gas, oil, uranium, critical minerals, grid)

- **Oil.** China imports and consumes more crude oil than any other country. Geopolitical conflict, sanctions and transport disruption reach it through both supply and price (P19). A composite index for 2000-2018 found four types of geopolitical risk lowered China's oil security, mainly through supply disruption (P18). Index values are model outputs, not measured import shares.
- **Gas.** Gas use rose 7.3% in 2024 (R1) [GOV-flag: low incentive to misstate gas use, which customs and pipeline flows constrain]. This case cites no peer-reviewed import-dependence share for gas or oil (see Gaps).
- **Uranium.** No peer-reviewed source was opened for this case. GAP. Nuclear capacity was 60.83 GW at the end of 2024 (R1) [GOV-flag: physical capacity, low incentive to misstate].
- **Critical minerals.** A study of 42 non-fuel minerals found China more than 50% import-reliant for 19 of them, including lithium, copper and manganese. Eleven overlap with the minerals the US also imports, which makes them likely points of competition (P17). [GOV-flag: the study's authors are government geological-survey scientists using trade data, and both trade partners have strategic reasons to shade these figures] So China's dominance in processing sits alongside real import exposure for some inputs.
- **Grid.** A model of China's coal-heavy grid estimated 2.6 PWh a year of wind could be integrated by 2030, about 26% of projected demand. Making the coal fleet more flexible was the main lever (P20). This is a model result. UNTESTED.
- **The 2021-22 shortages.** Since 2020 China has had three outages that hit several provinces for weeks. The government responded by letting coal-power prices move more and by opening wholesale markets to industrial and commercial users. The authors judge that the market structure still falls short of what reliable supply needs (P11). How much local dual-control enforcement contributed to the 2021 rationing is contested and unverified here (see Gaps).
- **Statutory security framing.** The Energy Law (in force 1 January 2025) puts energy security in Art. 1, sets up government and company energy reserves (Arts. 47-49), and allows countermeasures against foreign discriminatory measures (Art. 78) (R6).

## Health and environmental harm (air-pollution mortality, water, mining)

- **Mortality.** Under the Huai River heating policy, cities north of the river received free or subsidised coal for winter heating. Using that sharp policy line, a regression-discontinuity study found suspended particulates about 55% higher in the north and life expectancy about 5.5 years lower, mainly from cardiorespiratory causes (P8).
- A follow-up study with better data estimates that each extra 10 µg/m3 of PM10 reduces life expectancy by 0.64 years (95% CI 0.21-1.07) (P9).
- PM2.5-related deaths are estimated at 1.73 million in 2002, 2.26 million in 2012 and 2.12 million in 2017 (95% CI 1.79-2.52 million). Emission-control technology avoided an estimated 0.87 million deaths over the period (P10).
- From 2013 to 2017, population-weighted PM2.5 fell from 61.8 to 42.0 µg/m3. Tougher power-plant and industrial standards, boiler upgrades and cleaner household fuel did most of the work (P7).
- **Water.** Large coal-power hubs have raised water stress in arid catchments in the north-west, even though national freshwater withdrawal has decoupled from generation growth (P13). Hot spots include the Hai River and East Yellow River basins and arid parts of Xinjiang (P12).
- **Mining: methane.** Satellite (GOSAT) observations show China's methane emissions rising by about 1.1 Tg a year from 2010 to 2015, to 61.5 Tg in 2015, mostly from coal mining. The authors found no detectable effect from the coal-mine methane rules that took full effect in 2010 (P14).
- **Mining: safety.** Firms with politically connected executives had worker death rates two to three times higher. The gap was smaller where regulators' promotion depended on safety targets (P15).
- The official coal-mine death rate was 0.059 per million tonnes in 2024, down 37.2% (R1). [GOV-flag: strong incentive to under-report, because safety records feed officials' evaluations (P15)]

## Ethical analysis

### Facts (evidence strength stated)

- **Strong:** official data on air pollution and emissions have been unreliable at times. Three independent methods agree:
  - a discontinuity test found about 50% of cities reported dubious PM10 levels at the cut-off (P6);
  - once monitoring was automated, reported PM10 rose immediately and lastingly by about 35% (P22);
  - plant-reported SO2 diverged from satellite data in the strictest regions (P4).
- **Strong:** coal combustion is a large and well-identified cause of early death (P8, P9, P10).
- **Moderate:** the cut to emission estimates from lower emission factors (P1) and the rise from revised coal statistics (P3) act on different terms. Net emissions remain uncertain by several hundred Mt a year.

### Where people disagree

- **Emission totals.** One peer-reviewed estimate gives 2.9 GtC less carbon for 2000-2013 than earlier inventories (P1). Another, built on the revised statistics, gives 7.6 GtCO2 more over the same period (P3). They use different energy data and different emission factors, so they are not a direct contradiction.
- **SO2 audit.** A published comment argues the SO2 audit misread which plants faced which standards (P5). The original study stands as published.
- **Energy security vs climate.** The 2024 Energy Law treats coal as a base supply (R6). Modelling studies say early retirement is affordable (P21, P23). The disagreement is about risk tolerance, not about the data.

### Values and proposals (normative)

- Accurate data comes first. Pledges such as the 7-10% cut from the peak cannot be checked if the peak is measured by a system with a stake in the result.
- The Huai River line shows how an administrative rule can push large, unequal health harm onto one region's residents. Fairness asks that the harm be measured and that remedies be prioritised by exposure.
- Security of supply is a legitimate public goal. Overbuilding coal, though, shifts the cost to future grids and the global climate. Approval incentives (P16) should be judged by who carries that cost.
- The burden of change should match capacity. That cuts both ways: China's development needs count, and so do the global harms of its emissions.

## Solutions

Each solution is rated by the strength of evidence that it works.

1. **Independent top-down verification of coal use and emissions** (satellite NO2, SO2 and CH4, plus inverse modelling), published next to official statistics. Rating: **Strong** for detecting gaps (P4, P14, P22); **Limited** as a remedy, because no study has tested publication-linked accountability. UNTESTED as a policy.
2. **Automatic, tamper-resistant monitoring with direct central data access.** Rating: **Moderate**. One quasi-experimental study found reported pollution rose about 35% once automation arrived (P22).
3. **End-of-pipe and boiler standards at coal plants and in industry**, kept up and extended. Rating: **Strong** (P7, P10).
4. **Keep a central gate on coal approvals and reform guaranteed hours and tariffs.** Rating: **Moderate** for the diagnosis (P16). The reform's effect is UNTESTED.
5. **Plant-by-plant retirement order, worst plants first.** Rating: **Limited**, model-based (P21). UNTESTED.
6. **Market pricing and flexible coal output to prevent shortages and absorb wind and solar.** Rating: **Limited** (P11, P20). UNTESTED at national scale.
7. **Water caps and water-risk screening for new coal hubs in arid basins.** Rating: **Limited** (P12, P13). UNTESTED.
8. **Coal-mine methane rules enforced through satellite checks rather than self-reporting.** Rating: **Very limited**. The rules alone showed no detectable effect (P14). The verification-linked version is UNTESTED.
9. **Tie mine-safety regulators' promotion to independently verified fatality data.** Rating: **Limited**. The connected-firm death gap shrank where promotion depended on safety targets (P15). UNTESTED as a reform.

- No adjudicated data-falsification case tied to coal statistics was found.
- Mortality figures depend on the exposure-response model chosen (P10). Other models give different totals.
