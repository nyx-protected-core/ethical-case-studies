# Coal and energy security: the big players compared

Date: 2026-11-19

## Summary

The five large coal and energy-security cases do not share one path. China still took 53.2% of its total energy from coal in 2024 [GOV-flag: national statistics; incentive to under-report, because coal-share and energy-intensity targets bind officials] [R10]. The United States cut coal's share of utility-scale electricity from 33.2% in 2015 to 15.1% in 2024, then saw it rise to 16.6% in 2025 [GOV-flag: mandatory plant reports; physical output can be cross-checked against continuous emissions monitors, which limits the incentive to misstate kilowatthours, but series can be revised] [R1]. In the European Union, hard coal and brown coal together supplied 9% of electricity in 2025, down from 35% in 1990, after a brief 2021–22 rebound [GOV-flag: climate and phase-out pledges create an incentive to present the 2022 rebound as small and brief; series are revised] [R16]. Russia produced more coal than it consumed in 2023 and has no opened national phase-out date [GOV-flag: production data originate largely from Russian sources, which have an incentive to show resilience under sanctions] [R20]. Energy-security problems differ: concentrated gas imports, crude and nuclear-fuel imports, or dependence on export revenue. Peer-reviewed health evidence is Strong for coal-related deaths in the United States and China, and for a 2015 European baseline; it is Limited where only risk assessments were opened. Official energy figures are unevenly reliable.

## Ethical analysis

The facts in the table are not all of one kind: some are official records, some are peer-reviewed estimates, and some are labelled Limited or UNVERIFIED. The tensions below are about how to weigh them. Analysis uses peer-reviewed sources only.

1. **Security measures can revive a fuel whose health harm is already measured.** In the Union's first crisis year, energy savings cut greenhouse-gas emissions by 8.6%, while greater use of coal and oil raised other burdens, including particulate-matter formation and toxicity [P4]. An official abstract on six Union countries finds that on days when coal was cheaper than gas, coal generation rose 23% and several pollutants rose with it; that paper's health numbers are illustrative, not observed outcomes [P6]. A 2015-based European study already associated coal-plant particles with at least 16,800 excess deaths a year [P5]. In the United States, most of the measured drop in coal-related deaths followed scrubbers and then retirements, not a formal security exception [P1][P2]. Using coal as a reserve is a choice about whose lungs carry a supply shock.

2. **A concentrated supplier can be used as leverage, but the evidence is uneven.** Peer-reviewed case work documents subsidised energy for some neighbours and cut-offs or punitive prices for others [P19]. In 2021–22, gas supply to Europe was curbed and German consumption fell 23% below a temperature-adjusted baseline [P20]. Official import shares show how large that concentration had become [R12][R21]. Those records do not, by themselves, settle motive. They do show that a single-supplier share of the size recorded in 2021 is a political fact, not only a market fact.

3. **Official energy figures are not all equally trustworthy.** Three independent methods have found problems in Chinese pollution and energy reporting: a 1.4 Gt inventory gap [P10], a 9.2% upward revision in cumulative CO2 after official coal statistics were restated [P11], and immediate, lasting increases in reported PM10 once monitoring was automated [P30]. A European health study concludes that official plant inventories likely understate emissions [P5]. Russian official energy totals could not be audited in the opened set. Where a target is judged on figures produced by the same state that holds the target, the reporting incentive should be read with the number.

4. **The remaining coal burden is uneven inside countries, not only between them.** In the United States, residual coal-particle exposure has been inequitable for Black populations and for Native American populations in the West, even as overall exposure fell [P2]. In China, an administrative heating line produced a sharp north–south gap in particles and in life expectancy [P8]. In the Union, the remaining coal electricity is concentrated in a few member states [R16]. Phase-out and “security reserve” decisions therefore distribute harm, not only megawatts.

5. **Diversifying away from one fuel can create other dependencies.** As United States coal retired, power became more dependent on gas, and the system already relies on foreign uranium, enrichment and several battery and magnet minerals [R4][R5][R6][R7]. The Union reduced its 2021 pipeline-gas concentration: in 2024 Norway supplied 45.6% of gaseous natural-gas imports, and the United States supplied 45.3% of liquefied-gas imports, with a residual Russian liquefied-gas share of 17.5% [GOV-flag: trade values and volumes are revised; liquefied-gas origin can be harder to trace than pipeline gas] [R13][P8a]. China is import-reliant for 19 of 42 non-fuel minerals studied, even as it dominates some processing [P16]. A security policy that only tracks barrels or cubic metres misses those shifts.

## Solutions

Each item is aimed at a failure that appears in more than one of the rows above. Ratings: Strong, Moderate, Limited or Very limited. UNTESTED means the proposal has not been evaluated as a complete policy in the opened outcome evidence. No item below is based on an NGO report.

1. **Keep and enforce sulfur, nitrogen-oxide, particle and other end-of-pipe controls at operating coal plants.** This addresses the measured mortality burden. Rating: **Strong** for past controls. In the United States, most of the 2007–10 drop in coal PM2.5 came from scrubbers, and plant-level deaths fell after controls [P1][P2]. In China, tougher plant and industrial standards were a main driver of the 2013–17 PM2.5 decline, and controls avoided an estimated 0.87 million PM2.5-related deaths over 2002–17 [P31][P9].

2. **Publish independent top-down checks (satellite and automated monitors) next to official coal, air and methane figures.** This addresses inventory gaps and unaudited official data. Rating: **Strong** for detecting gaps; **Limited**, and **UNTESTED** as a policy, as a remedy that changes official reporting. Evidence: reported PM10 rose about 35% once monitoring was automated [P30]; plant-reported SO2 diverged from satellite data in the strictest regions [P32]; coal-mine methane rules that took full effect in 2010 had no detectable effect in satellite observations [P33]; European plant inventories likely understate source strength [P5]; satellite flaring methods are rated at about ±9.5% [P23].

3. **Treat demand reduction as a first security instrument in a supply shock, not as an afterthought.** This addresses the finding that savings, not a planned coal increment, drove the Union's first-year greenhouse-gas cut [P4]. Rating: **Strong** for that 2022 episode; **Moderate** for durability, because the same study warns that a mild winter and curtailed industry may not last. A German observational study finds a 23% temperature-adjusted gas-use cut in late 2022 [P20].

4. **If coal is restarted for security, keep the reactivation strictly time-limited, and do not rewrite a long-term exit around a temporary rebound.** Health and life-cycle evidence shows why a familiar fuel is a costly reserve [P5][P4][P6]. Rating: **Strong** as a constraint on policy. Whether every 2022 extension stayed inside that form is **UNTESTED** in the opened Union set.

5. **Retire remaining high-harm plants first, with replacement capacity in place.** Past retirements cut exposure [P1][P2]. A plant-by-plant model of 1,037 Chinese plants finds that retiring the worst-scoring 18% first would allow a complete phase-out by 2045 or 2055, depending on the temperature path; that is a model result [P34]. Rating: **Strong** for the health benefit of past retirements; **Limited**, and **UNTESTED**, as a formal ranking mandate. Evidence that any particular future path preserves reliability is **Limited** in the opened set.

6. **Diversify gas and nuclear-fuel suppliers, and avoid new long-lived contracts or import capacity that lock in a dominant supplier.** This addresses the 2021 Union concentration [R12][R21] and remaining nuclear-fuel dependence [P7]. Rating: **Moderate** for the diagnosis. Security outcomes of post-2022 alternative-fuel contracts, and the “right” residual liquefied-gas capacity, are **UNTESTED** in the opened set.

7. **Find and fix oil and gas methane ultra-emitters, and use satellite surveys to check official totals.** Ultra-emitters made up 8–12% of global oil and gas methane in a 2019–20 survey, a contribution not included in most inventories [P22]. Rating: **Moderate** for the monitoring tool and for published cost-effectiveness; **UNTESTED** as an enforced programme in the producer case opened here.

8. **Do not treat loosening air rules as a coal-revival or energy-security policy.** For 2005–16, opened United States decompositions attribute almost all of the coal-production and eastern-interconnection retirement drop to gas prices and demand, not to environmental rules [P3][P35]. Rating: **Moderate** for that period. Application to 2025–26 markets is **UNTESTED**.

## Gaps

- There is no single peer-reviewed coal-share series on a common basis (electricity versus total energy versus production) for all five players.
- No opened peer-reviewed death count from Russian coal or gas pollution, and no independent audit of official Russian energy totals.
- No opened peer-reviewed oil or gas import-dependence share for China.
- United States mortality evidence covers people aged 65 and older; the cause of the 2025 rise in coal generation was not established in opened sources.
- Causal evidence that Union storage rules, rather than prices or weather, averted shortage was not opened. Health figures in the 2021/22 coal-switch paper are illustrative only [P6].
- Several Russian legal texts, and two energy-weapon papers, are UNVERIFIED and are not used for findings [P36][P37][R22][R23][R24].
- Climate-damage estimates for the remaining fleets were not opened on a common basis.
