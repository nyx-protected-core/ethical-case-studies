# Coal and energy security in Europe (European Union and United Kingdom)

Date: 2026-11-19

## Summary

- Before 2022, the European Union relied on a single external supplier for a large share of its fossil-fuel imports. Official trade figures put that supplier’s share of Union gas imports at 45% in 2021 (150 billion cubic metres). The same official stocktake reports 52 billion cubic metres and a 19% share in 2024 [S11][GOV-flag: a progress stocktake has an incentive to show a steep drop and a successful plan; Eurostat trade figures can still be revised and may under-count molecules routed through third countries]. A peer-reviewed life-cycle study, using official statistics, puts the same supplier at about 38% of Union gas consumption in 2021 [S1][GOV-flag: the underlying official consumption and trade series can be revised and may under-count routed cargoes].
- After the 2022 invasion of Ukraine and the subsequent cut in pipeline deliveries, wholesale gas prices surged and several Union states and the United Kingdom brought coal plants back or held them as winter reserve. A peer-reviewed ex-post assessment finds that the Union’s actual first-year crisis management cut greenhouse-gas emissions by 8.6%, mainly through energy savings, while coal and oil use worsened other environmental burdens [S1]. Official electricity statistics show only a brief coal rebound: coal’s share of Union generation was 16% in 2022 and 12% in 2023, then fell further [S9][S8][GOV-flag: climate and phase-out pledges create an incentive to present the rebound as small and brief; series are revised].
- The ethical tension is not between “security” and “climate” as slogans. It is between a short, price-driven return to coal that raises air-pollution and mining harms already documented in Europe; a diversification into liquefied gas that reduces one dependency while creating others; and demand reduction, which the opened life-cycle study finds drove the first-year emissions cut, alongside renewable deployment, whose crisis-era acceleration the opened policy study finds uneven and moderate.

## Background

Union climate law had already set a 2050 climate-neutrality aim and a 2030 emissions-reduction target of 55%, with renewable-energy policy running on a separate track from energy-security policy [S2]. Security policy favoured resilience — storage, alternative pipelines and emergency solidarity — rather than reducing reliance on a dominant supplier [S2]. Between 2010 and 2019, that supplier’s gas exports to Europe rose by 43% and their share from 26% to 47%, according to the political-economy sources cited in the same study [S2]. The United Kingdom, already far along a coal exit and less exposed to pipeline gas from that supplier, still faced the same wholesale-price shock and treated residual coal units as a winter contingency [S13][S14].

The REPowerEU plan, issued in 2022, recast renewables, savings and import diversification as instruments for ending that fossil-fuel dependence [S1][S2][S11]. In the short term it also contemplated delaying coal and nuclear plant closures and raising coal-fired generation [S1].

## Coal fleet and phase-out record

Official Union statistics show a long decline interrupted, briefly, in 2021–2022. Hard-coal consumption had already fallen from a plateau of about 250 million tonnes in the 2010s; estimated 2025 consumption is 107 million tonnes, 52% below the level eight years earlier [S8][GOV-flag: climate and phase-out pledges create an incentive to present the 2022 rebound as small and brief; series are revised]. Brown-coal consumption, after a sharp drop in 2018–2020, rose again in 2021 and 2022, then fell; estimated 2025 consumption is 184 million tonnes, 50% below 2018 [S8][GOV-flag: as above]. Deliveries of both hard coal and brown coal to power plants rose in 2021 and 2022, then fell in 2023 and 2024 to the lowest levels on record [S8][GOV-flag: as above]. In 2024, Union coal production and consumption reached recorded lows of 242 million tonnes and 306 million tonnes; consumption had already fallen 23% between 2022 and 2023 [S9][GOV-flag: as above]. Solar overtook coal in Union electricity production in 2024 [S8][GOV-flag: as above]. In 2025, hard coal and brown coal accounted for 4% and 5% of Union electricity, down from a combined 35% in 1990 [S8][GOV-flag: as above].

The remaining fleet is uneven. In 2024, Poland was the only Union country in which coal was the largest source of electricity (55%). Coal was the second-largest source in Czechia (36%) and Bulgaria (21%), and in Germany (21%, behind wind) [S8][GOV-flag: national generation mixes are reported by member states and can be revised]. Eight Union countries still produced brown coal in 2025, with Germany accounting for 46% of Union brown-coal consumption, followed by Poland, Czechia, Bulgaria, Romania and Greece [S8][GOV-flag: as above].

In 2021, Russia still supplied 52.4% of the Union’s hard-coal imports (a high of 61 million tonnes had been reached in 2018). A Union import ban came into force in August 2022. Official figures show those imports falling to 27 million tonnes in 2022, 1 million tonnes in 2023 and less than 250,000 tonnes in 2024, a 99.5% drop from 2021 [S8][S9][GOV-flag: “ultimate origin” reporting may lag or under-count cargoes that change hands in transit]. In 2023, 90% of hard-coal net imports came from Australia and the United States (25% each), Colombia (18%), South Africa (14%) and Kazakhstan (9%) [S9].

REPowerEU envisaged a temporary increase in coal-fired generation. The short-term plan allowed up to 24 billion cubic metres of gas to be replaced by coal, equal to about 117 terawatt-hours a year, or a 28% increase in coal electricity relative to 2021 [S1]. The International Energy Agency material cited in that study reports that 10 gigawatts of coal plant re-entered the German market in 2022 [S1]. The same study finds that actual first-year use of coal and nuclear was smaller than the plan, with energy savings making up the difference [S1]. Official data then show the decline resuming in 2023–2024 [S8][S9][S2].

In the United Kingdom, official department letters from May–September 2022 record a request to the electricity system operator to examine options for winter 2022/23, and subsequent agreements to extend operation temporarily at West Burton A, at Drax’s coal-fired units, and at unit 4 of Ratcliffe-on-Soar [S13][GOV-flag: official letters have an incentive to present the extensions as limited, temporary and last-resort]. The statutory security-of-supply report for 2022 records a new Demand Flexibility Service for that winter, introduced after the invasion of Ukraine [S14]. The contingency did not reverse the exit: the country’s last coal-fired power station, Ratcliffe-on-Soar, closed at the end of September 2024, and the government reports that coal’s share of United Kingdom electricity went from around 40% in 2012 to 0% [S18][GOV-flag: a government announcement has an incentive to present the exit as complete and early].

## Energy-security dependencies (gas, oil, uranium, critical minerals, grid)

**Gas.** Official Commission figures: imports from the dominant 2021 supplier fell from 150 billion cubic metres (45% of Union gas imports) in 2021 to 52 billion cubic metres (19%) in 2024 [S11][GOV-flag: as above]. Eurostat trade data for 2024 show Norway supplying 45.6% of gaseous natural-gas imports, Algeria 19.3% and Russia 16.6%; for liquefied gas, the United States supplied 45.3%, Russia 17.5% and Algeria 10.7% [S7][GOV-flag: trade values and volumes are revised; liquefied-gas origin can be harder to trace than pipeline gas]. Pipeline diversification and new or expanded liquefied-gas terminals reduced the previous pipeline concentration, but they also raised the United States’ share and left a residual Russian share in liquefied gas [S2][S7]. The Commission reports that Union gas demand fell 17% between August 2022 and January 2025, about 70 billion cubic metres a year, against a 15% reduction plan [S11][S7][GOV-flag: demand-reduction figures can reflect mild winters and industrial shutdowns as well as policy, and a stocktake has an incentive to credit the plan]. A peer-reviewed study reports a 55 billion cubic metre demand drop in the first year, the steepest on record, driven by high prices, a mild winter and policy [S1]. Wholesale prices reached a monthly average of 240 euros per megawatt-hour in August 2022, against 38.5 euros before the invasion and 32.3 euros at the end of 2023 [S1].

**Oil.** The Commission stocktake reports crude-oil import shares from Russia down from 27% to 3% [S11][GOV-flag: as for gas]. Eurostat’s 2024 partners for petroleum oils were the United States (16.1%), Norway (13.5%) and Kazakhstan (11.5%) [S7][GOV-flag: trade shares are revised and origin can be obscured by blending and transit].

**Uranium and nuclear fuel.** Unlike coal, oil and pipeline gas, nuclear supply was not the main target of the 2022 sanctions discussed in the opened research [S4]. A peer-reviewed mapping of the state nuclear vendor’s portfolio finds long-lived fuel, technology and spent-fuel dependencies in Union states with water-water energetic reactor fleets. At the time of that study, fuel from the vendor’s fuel subsidiary still supplied reactors in Bulgaria, Czechia and Finland; Hungary and Slovakia were planning to base decarbonisation primarily or entirely on nuclear power, so fuel-import dependence can exceed what the reactors’ share of electricity alone implies [S4]. The study attributes 42% of electricity in Hungary and 37% in Bulgaria to reactors built by that vendor [S4]. It also notes that changing fuel vendor is possible — Ukraine did so — but that the process took more than a decade and that Western fabrication capacity was limited in the short term [S4]. A global conversion and enrichment share sometimes quoted for the same vendor is not used here: it rests on an industry handbook cited in that paper, which was not independently opened. The Commission later stated that Union countries operating those reactor designs are increasingly turning to non-Russian alternatives [S11][GOV-flag: a stocktake has an incentive to report diversification as already under way].

**Critical minerals and grid.** The opened studies do not include a full official minerals balance. They do show that replacing gas with wind, solar, heat pumps or hydrogen can shift burdens onto metal and mineral resource use, land, water and toxicity, depending on the technology and the power mix [S1]. Grid connection, transmission and storage are identified as binding constraints on wind deployment, especially offshore, and as reasons why target-setting alone has not matched implementation [S2]. The United Kingdom’s 2022 security report likewise treats storage and demand flexibility as system-integrity tools, not as optional extras [S14].

## Health and environmental harm (air pollution, water, mining)

A 2021 peer-reviewed chemistry-transport and health-impact study, using 2015 emissions, estimates that fine-particle pollution from European coal-fired power plants was associated with at least 16,800 excess deaths per year over the European domain (95% confidence interval 14,800–18,700). The authors conclude that official plant-level inventories likely understate source strength, and that correcting for that understatement roughly doubles the estimate, to 33,900 (33,000–37,600) [S3][GOV-flag: pollutant-release inventories can under-report; the study treats that as a downward bias in the uncorrected estimate]. Within the then EU-28, the uncorrected range is 9,500–12,100 excess deaths a year, including 1,800–2,260 in Germany, 1,470–1,840 in Poland and 1,270–1,670 in the United Kingdom [S3]. These figures pre-date the 2022 rebound and the later decline; they measure a baseline harm, not the extra deaths in 2022 alone.

For the price shock itself, a 2026 peer-reviewed study — official abstract only — of six Union countries with substantial gas-to-coal switching potential finds that, on the 510 days of the 2021/22 surge when coal was cheaper than gas, coal-fired generation rose by 23%, with a 10% increase in carbon dioxide, 19% in fine particles, 10% in nitrogen oxides and 24% in sulphur dioxide, relative to a model counterfactual in which gas remained cheaper [S6]. The abstract states that the health figures in the paper are illustrative mappings to literature damage factors, not observed health outcomes, and that the results are short-term [S6]. Claims beyond that abstract are not used.

Life-cycle assessment of the Union’s first-year crisis management finds that greenhouse-gas emissions fell 8.6% because of energy savings, but that coal and oil use increased burdens in other categories, including freshwater eutrophication (by up to 66% in the aftermath scenario), particulate-matter formation, acidification and toxicity [S1]. The short-term REPowerEU package, had it been followed as written, would have left greenhouse-gas emissions almost unchanged while worsening several non-climate categories, largely because of extended coal operation [S1].

Mining harm is not only historical. A 2024 peer-reviewed study of the Przemsza basin in the Upper Silesian coalfield (Poland) finds that even as the volume of discharged mine water fell by about 42% between 1991 and 2023, salinity in the river rose sharply in 2021–2023, from an average of about 2,000 to 6,700 microsiemens per centimetre, under drought and low flows [S5]. The authors argue that mine-water rebound after closure does not automatically end long-lasting surface-water impacts, and that salinisation may be amplified by a changing climate [S5].

## Policy and law

The following are cited as records, not as peer-reviewed analysis.

- **REPowerEU (2022) and the 2025 stocktake.** The plan combined savings, diversification and faster renewable deployment, with a 2027 aim of ending residual fossil imports from the 2021 dominant supplier, later restated in a 2025 roadmap [S11][S1][S2]. That aim is now law for gas: Regulation (EU) 2026/261 prohibits imports of Russian pipeline gas and liquefied gas from 18 March 2026, with transition periods for existing contracts ending no later than 1 January 2027 for liquefied gas under long-term contracts and 30 September 2027 for pipeline gas (1 November 2027 where a storage risk is confirmed) [S17]. The 2023 renewable-energy directive raised the binding 2030 Union target from 32% to 42.5%, with a 45% aim [S15]. The 2021 “Fit for 55” proposal had already sought 40%, so the REPowerEU-era increase was a smaller step than the earlier jump from 32% to 40% [S2]. The Commission reports that almost half of Union electricity (47%) now comes from renewables [S11][GOV-flag: the share is an official performance claim in a progress narrative].
- **Gas storage.** Regulation (EU) 2022/1032 required underground storage on a member state’s territory to reach 80% of capacity by 1 November 2022 and 90% by 1 November in subsequent years, with a cap that can limit a state’s filling obligation to 35% of its average annual consumption over five years, and with limited credit for stored liquefied gas [S12]. Regulation (EU) 2025/1733 extended the filling obligation to 31 December 2027 and made it more flexible: the 90% target may now be met at any point between 1 October and 1 December, and a member state may deviate by up to 10 percentage points in difficult conditions (with further limited deviations allowed) [S16].
- **Demand reduction.** Member states committed to cut gas consumption by at least 15% from 1 August 2022; the arrangement was later extended to 31 March 2025 [S7].
- **Coal import ban.** A Union ban on hard-coal imports from Russia entered into force in August 2022 [S8][S9].
- **United Kingdom winter 2022/23 contingency.** Official letters and the statutory security-of-supply report, as above [S13][S14].
- **Household price relief.** Eurostat records that taxes and levies as a share of the household gas price fell to 13.7% in the second half of 2022, reflecting subsidies and allowances, then rose again as those measures were reduced [S10][GOV-flag: governments have an incentive to show that relief lowered bills; classification of taxes and subsidies is a policy choice].

## Ethical analysis

Three trade-offs, not one, structure the 2022 shock.

First, **security versus health and local environment.** Holding or restarting coal plants was a legally available, technically familiar way to replace scarce gas. It shifted harm onto people living with coal pollution and mine-water discharges, including in countries that were not the most exposed to pipeline-gas cuts. The opened health study predates the shock, but it shows that the harm of a coal fleet is already large at ordinary operation, and that official emission inventories likely understate it [S3]. The abstract-only price-shock study, and the life-cycle study, both find that the 2021/22 switch raised pollutants and non-climate burdens [S6][S1]. A “temporary” return is ethically easier to defend if it is actually temporary, geographically limited, and paired with demand reduction. Official generation and delivery data show that the Union rebound was brief [S8][S9]. That does not erase the harm in the rebound year, nor the mining and water burdens that continue after mines close [S5].

Second, **one dependency versus another.** Cutting pipeline-gas and coal imports reduced a concentrated political risk. It also raised liquefied-gas imports, including from the United States and, still, from Russia, and it left nuclear fuel and reactor-service dependence only partly addressed [S7][S4][S11]. Diversification that is counted as “security” on a single fuel can hide residual exposure in another fuel, in enrichment and fuel fabrication, or in the minerals and grid equipment that a faster renewable build requires [S1][S2][S4]. The ethical claim that “the transition and security point the same way” holds only if those new concentrations are named and managed.

Third, **affordability and who pays.** Household gas prices, including taxes, rose sharply from the second half of 2021, reached 0.1163 euros per kilowatt-hour in the first half of 2023, and a series high of 0.1244 euros in the second half of 2024 [S10][GOV-flag: as above]. Prices excluding taxes peaked in the second half of 2022 at 0.0971 euros per kilowatt-hour [S10][GOV-flag: as above]. Emergency tax cuts and allowances reduced the tax share in 2022, then receded [S10]. High prices also helped cut demand and, in the life-cycle study, greenhouse-gas emissions [S1]. The same prices punished households and firms with little ability to switch or to stop using heat. Untargeted support can blunt that hardship; it can also slow the price signal that delivered savings and, as the policy analysis in [S2] notes, can reverse earlier fossil-subsidy reform. The opened official price series do not, by themselves, identify the fairest instrument. They do show that the shock was large and that relief was a political choice, not a law of the market.

A further issue is **honesty in official success accounts.** Import shares, storage filling and renewable electricity percentages are useful records. They are also performance claims. Each official figure used above is flagged. Peer-reviewed work should carry the causal interpretation: savings, not the planned coal increment, drove the first-year emissions drop; the renewable-target increase in REPowerEU was modest next to the earlier “Fit for 55” step; solar has run ahead of wind; and offshore wind remains constrained by cost, grids and permitting [S1][S2].

No organisation or named person is accused here of a legal offence. Supply cuts, import bans, plant-life extensions and damaged pipelines are treated as recorded events. Responsibility for the 2022 damage to the Nord Stream pipelines has not been determined in the sources opened for this case [S4].

## Solutions

Each proposal is tied to a failure found above and rated Strong, Moderate, Limited or Very limited. **UNTESTED** marks a proposal that has not been causally evaluated in the sources opened here.

1. **Treat demand reduction as a first security instrument, not as an afterthought.** The first-year emissions drop came from savings, not from the planned coal increment, and some of those savings may not last if they depended on a mild winter or on curtailed industry [S1]. Rating: **Strong** for the 2022 episode as a way to cut gas use and greenhouse gases together; **Moderate** for durability, because the opened study itself warns that some drivers were temporary.

2. **Keep any coal reactivation strictly time-limited, and do not rewrite a coal exit around a temporary rebound.** Health and life-cycle evidence shows why a familiar fuel is a costly reserve [S3][S1][S6]. Official data show that a brief rebound is possible [S8][S9]. Rating: **Strong** as a constraint on policy; the United Kingdom’s official wording of a temporary, last-resort extension [S13] is the right form, but whether every Union extension stayed inside that form is **UNTESTED** here.

3. **Fill storage against a legal target before winter.** Regulation 2022/1032 addresses the pre-2022 weakness of a resilience policy that did not prevent extreme concentration of supply [S12][S2]. Rating: **Moderate** as a binding, observable rule; causal evidence that the 80/90% targets, rather than high prices or a mild winter, averted shortage is **UNTESTED** in the opened peer-reviewed set.

4. **Build wind, solar, interconnectors and grids at the same time.** Solar has already accelerated; onshore and especially offshore wind have not matched the same pace, and grid and permitting constraints are a stated reason [S2]. Rating: **Moderate**. A still faster path is partly **UNTESTED** at the scale of the 2030 targets.

5. **Diversify nuclear fuel and fabrication for water-water energetic reactor fleets, with a long lead time.** Dependence is real and switching has been done, but slowly [S4]. Rating: **Moderate**. Security outcomes of the post-2022 alternative-fuel contracts are not independently evaluated here (**UNTESTED** as to speed and completeness).

6. **Avoid locking in new long-lived liquefied-gas import capacity beyond a shrinking residual demand.** Diversification reduced pipeline risk; it also created a large new supplier share and, in the policy literature opened here, a recognised lock-in and emissions risk [S2][S1][S7]. Rating: **Moderate** as a caution. The “right” terminal capacity for a 2030–2040 residual is **UNTESTED** in the opened set.

7. **Target hardship relief, and put a sunset on emergency fossil-price subsidies.** Official price data show both a severe shock and a temporary tax-share collapse [S10]. Rating: **Limited** as a design principle supported by the price record and by the policy analysis in [S2], but **UNTESTED** here as a causal comparison of instruments.

8. **Plan mine-water and river salinisation as part of a coal exit, not as an afterthought.** Closure of mines does not, on the opened Polish evidence, automatically restore rivers [S5]. Rating: **Moderate** for the documented mechanism; basin-wide remedies remain partly **UNTESTED**.

9. **Count critical-mineral and grid-equipment concentration as an energy-security item, with the same honesty applied to gas in 2021.** The life-cycle study shows burden-shifting into minerals, water and toxicity [S1]. Rating: **Limited**, because no opened official minerals balance is assessed here; most implementation paths are **UNTESTED** in this case.

## Gaps

- No opened peer-reviewed causal evaluation of the 2022 storage regulation, or of how much of the 2022/23 winter margin was due to the legal fill target rather than to prices, weather or demand reduction.
- No opened official Euratom or customs series for Union uranium, conversion and enrichment shares, so those figures are not reported.
- The United Kingdom is covered by official winter records and by the 2015-based health estimates, but no opened peer-reviewed study isolates the 2022 United Kingdom coal contingency or the household-bill shock.
- A full official critical-minerals balance was not opened.
- Health costs of the 2021/22 switch are taken only from an official abstract; the authors warn that the health numbers in that paper are illustrative.
- Residual Russian liquefied-gas imports, and possible routing through third countries, are not audited in the opened peer-reviewed set.
- Some widely quoted 2022 coal-tonnage figures could not be confirmed on the official pages opened for this case and are not used.
- This case excludes data from, and co-authors affiliated with, the country reserved for a separate case. That rule may omit otherwise relevant studies.
