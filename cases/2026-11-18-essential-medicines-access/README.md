# Affordable Access to Essential Medicines: Patents, Prices and Production

*Ethics case study. Research and writing only. Every number is tagged to a numbered source in SOURCES.md, written as [S#]. Status claims are dated "as of 18 November 2026". Figures are quoted as the source gives them and are not recalculated.*

---

## 1. Why this case matters

A medicine that exists but cannot be bought, found or afforded does no good. Access to essential medicines is the meeting point of three systems that pull in different directions:

- a **patent system** that rewards invention with temporary exclusivity;
- **health systems and households** that must pay for treatment, often out of pocket;
- an **industrial geography** that concentrates production in a few countries.

The case asks how far a society may override or reshape private rights in medicines to save lives now, without weakening the incentives that produce the next generation of medicines.

| Supply-chain mark-ups | Wholesale 2% to 380%; retail 10% to 552% | [S3] |
| Financial hardship from out-of-pocket health spending (2022) | 2.1 billion people, 26% of the global population | [S4], [S5] |
| Of whom: impoverishing spending / large spending | 1.6 billion / 450 million | [S5] |
| People lacking coverage for essential health services (2023) | 4.6 billion | [S4] |

**Measurement note.** The 2025 WHO/World Bank monitoring report uses a *revised* financial-hardship indicator. "Large" out-of-pocket spending now means spending above 40% of a household's *discretionary* budget, i.e. total budget minus the cost of basic needs [S5]. These figures are not directly comparable with older "catastrophic spending" series that used different thresholds. Medicines are one part of out-of-pocket spending, not the whole of it; the report does not split hardship by medicine spending in the pages checked.

The WHO/HAI survey evidence [S3] shows that patents are only part of the story. Many of the medicines missing from public clinics were off-patent generics, and much of the price gap came from mark-ups, taxes and weak procurement. Any honest analysis has to hold both problems in view: monopoly pricing of new medicines and supply-system failure for old ones.

## 3. Patents and the TRIPS flexibilities

### 3.1 The legal framework

- **Doha Declaration (November 2001).** WTO ministers agreed that the TRIPS Agreement "can and should be interpreted and implemented in a manner supportive of WTO members' right to protect public health and, in particular, to promote access to medicines for all" [S6]. It confirmed that "each member has the right to grant compulsory licences and the freedom to determine the grounds upon which such licences are granted", and gave least-developed countries a transition period for pharmaceutical patents (then until 1 January 2016) [S6].
- **Article 31bis.** The "paragraph 6 system" lets a country produce generics under a compulsory licence *for export* to countries that cannot manufacture them. It was agreed as an amendment in 2005 and entered into force in 2017, when two-thirds of members had accepted it, the first amendment of any WTO agreement since 1995 [S7], [S8]. The WTO's TRIPS database cites the text "as amended on 23 January 2017" [S10]. Members that have not yet accepted the amendment remain covered by an interim waiver [S8].

### 3.2 Thailand, 2006 to 2008

Between 2006 and 2008 Thailand's Ministry of Public Health granted government-use licences for seven patented drugs [S12]. A systematic review's table lists seven Thai licensing events covering efavirenz (two strengths), lopinavir/ritonavir, clopidogrel and the cancer drugs docetaxel, erlotinib and letrozole [S13].

**Health effects.** A peer-reviewed assessment over five years estimated [S12]:
- 84,158 additional patients received the seven drugs;
- 12,493 QALYs were gained, valued at USD 132.4 million;
- the efavirenz licence gave the greatest benefit, and benefits varied widely between drugs.

**Trade pushback.** In its 2007 Special 301 Report the US Trade Representative elevated Thailand to the Priority Watch List [S14]. The report cited longstanding copyright and enforcement concerns. It also said that "in late 2006 and early 2007" Thailand had announced compulsory licences, and that while the US "acknowledges a country's ability to issue such licenses in accordance with WTO rules, the lack of transparency and due process exhibited in Thailand represents a serious concern" [S14]. The same Thai study found that exports of the three products affected by the US withdrawal of trade preferences (GSP) fell, but Thailand's overall exports kept rising, and it found no relationship between the licences and foreign direct investment, 2002 to 2008 [S12].

### 3.3 Brazil, 2007

On 4 May 2007 Brazil issued a compulsory licence for the antiretroviral efavirenz for public, non-commercial use [S15]. Generic versions were first bought from Indian manufacturers, and production then moved to the public laboratory Farmanguinhos [S15]. The authors report projected savings written as "US$ 236.8 until 2012"; the abstract does not state the unit, which is presumably millions [S15].

### 3.4 Evidence on price effects across countries

A systematic review of 24 compulsory-licensing events with price data found a mean price reduction of 66.2% to 73.9%, depending on whether the minimum or maximum reported price change is used [S13]. Individual reductions ranged from 6.7% (atazanavir in Brazil, 2007) to 98% (a lamivudine/nevirapine/zidovudine combination) [S13]. The same authors caution that a licence is useless if no alternative supplier exists, and that a country's long-run bargaining strength depends on building its own pharmaceutical capability [S13].

### 3.5 India: section 3(d), Nexavar and Glivec

- **Section 3(d)** of India's Patents Act, as quoted in the judgment, excludes "the mere discovery of a new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy of that substance", among other things [S17]. In the Glivec case the Supreme Court of India held that for a medicine, "the test of efficacy can only be 'therapeutic efficacy'" [S17].
- **Novartis v. Union of India (1 April 2013).** The Court held that the beta crystalline form of imatinib mesylate "fails in both the tests of invention and patentability" under sections 2(1)(j), 2(1)(ja) and 3(d), and dismissed Novartis's appeals with costs [S17].
- **Nexavar (sorafenib), March 2012.** India's first compulsory licence let a domestic generic company make and sell Bayer's patented sorafenib, used for advanced liver and kidney cancer. The licensee was to charge no more than Rs 8,880 for a month's supply (120 tablets) and pay a 6% royalty on net sales, quarterly [S16]. The source is the accepted manuscript of a law-journal article, not the order itself; the later appeal history is not covered by any source verified here and is left out.

### 3.6 The 2022 MC12 decision on COVID-19 vaccines

At the 12th Ministerial Conference (June 2022), WTO members adopted a decision that clarified the scope for members to limit the exclusive effect of patents in order to support equitable access to **COVID-19 vaccines only** [S8]. It approved only a limited part of the waiver India and South Africa had originally proposed, after 20 months of negotiation [S11]. A peer-reviewed analysis describes it as running for a defined period (five years, renewable) rather than being tied to WHO's emergency status [S11].

**Limits, as of 18 November 2026:**
- **No extension to therapeutics or diagnostics.** The decision required members to decide on extending it. On 13 February 2024 the TRIPS Council reported that "consensus could not be reached on the extension" [S9], and the WTO's current TRIPS and health page still says members "could not reach consensus" [S8].
- **Uptake.** I could not open the WTO's annual-review documents for this decision (the document server returned empty pages). Secondary search summaries say no eligible member had sent a communication under it through 2024. This is **unverified** and should not be published as fact without the primary WTO document.

## 4. The Medicines Patent Pool

The Medicines Patent Pool (MPP), set up in 2010, negotiates *voluntary* licences with patent holders so that generic firms can supply low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [S20]. It is the cooperative counterpart to compulsory licensing.

| MPP-reported impact, 2010 to end-2025 | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doses supplied by licensees | 62.14 billion, to 148 LMICs | [S18], [S19] |
| Products licensed | 44, from 22 innovators | [S18] |
| Additional patient-years treated (versus no MPP) | 6.7 million | [S18], [S19] |
| DALYs averted / HIV virological failures averted | 1.4 million / 540,000 | [S18], [S19] |
| Saved by the global community | US$2.6 billion | [S18], [S19] |
| "Theoretical expenditures avoided" | US$12 billion | [S18], [S19] |

**Independent-ish evidence.** A 2017 peer-reviewed estimate put MPP's direct savings on HIV medicines at USD 2.3 billion (net present value) by 2028, a cost-benefit ratio of 1:43 [S20]. Its authors include MPP-affiliated researchers, so it is not fully independent. Both sets of figures are modelled counterfactuals, not observed savings, and depend on assumptions about what prices would have been without MPP.

## 5. Pricing: insulin and cancer medicines

### 5.1 Insulin

Insulin is a century old, yet WHO's 2021 report on insulin found [S21]:
- three multinational companies control more than 90% of the insulin market;
- one in two people who need insulin for type 2 diabetes do not get it;
- three in four people with type 2 diabetes live outside North America and Europe but account for less than 40% of insulin revenue;
- analogue insulins cost at least 1.5 times as much as human insulin, and in some countries three times as much, though human insulin is in general as effective.

**United States, 2023.** In the US, the Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare cap limits cost-sharing to no more than $35 for a one-month supply of each covered insulin product under Part B and Part D [S25], [S26]. In March 2023 all three major manufacturers announced cuts:

| Firm | Date | Announcement | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eli Lilly | 1 March 2023 | List price of Humalog and Humulin cut 70% (effective Q4 2023); $35 monthly out-of-pocket cap for commercially insured patients | [S22] |
| Novo Nordisk | 14 March 2023 | US list prices of several insulins cut by up to 75% from January 2024 | [S24] |
| Sanofi | 16 March 2023 | Lantus list price cut 78%; $35 out-of-pocket cap for commercially insured patients | [S23] |

These are list-price cuts in one high-income market. They say little about prices in LMICs, and list prices in the US differ from net prices after rebates. Lilly's own release says government rules exclude people in federal insurance programmes from its $35 scheme, and points to the Medicare Part D cap instead [S22].

### 5.2 Cancer medicines

- **Price and value.** A study of 65 cancer drugs found US monthly treatment costs were a median 2.31 times (IQR 1.79 to 3.17) those in four European countries. For solid tumours it found no significant link between cost and clinical benefit, except in France on one benefit scale [S27].
- **WHO Model List, September 2025.** WHO's Expert Committee lists only cancer medicines "proven to prolong life by at least 4-6 months" [S28]. In 2025 it added pembrolizumab as first-line monotherapy for metastatic cervical, colorectal and non-small cell lung cancer, with atezolizumab and cemiplimab as therapeutic alternatives for the lung indication [S28]. It also added GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide) and tirzepatide for some adults with type 2 diabetes [S28]. The lists now hold 523 medicines for adults and 374 for children [S28].

Listing creates a moral expectation of affordability but does not change prices by itself. Adding high-priced immunotherapies is a signal to buyers and suppliers, not a guarantee of access.

## 7. The WHO Pandemic Agreement and the PABS annex

- The World Health Assembly **adopted** the WHO Pandemic Agreement on **20 May 2025** [S36].
- The agreement opens for signature only once its **Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing (PABS) annex** is adopted, and enters into force 30 days after 60 countries ratify it [S36].
- **Status as of 18 November 2026:** the PABS annex is **not yet adopted**. The ninth meeting of the Intergovernmental Working Group (2 to 13 November 2026) closed with member states "reaffirming their commitment to finalizing the Annex for its consideration by the World Health Assembly in May 2027" [S37]. Formal talks resume 11 to 15 January 2027, with further rounds on 15 to 19 February and 15 to 25 March 2027 [S37].
- The annex is meant to tie the rapid sharing of pathogens and sequence data to "rapid and equitable access to the resulting products, including vaccines, diagnostics and therapeutics" [S37].

So, eighteen months after adoption, the agreement's central access bargain is still being negotiated, and the agreement cannot yet be signed.

## 8. Fair counterarguments

### 8.1 Innovation needs a return

Patents fund research that is costly and mostly fails. The two most-cited cost estimates differ sharply:

| Study | Sample | Estimate | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DiMasi, Grabowski and Hansen (2016) | 106 drugs from 10 firms | $1,395m out-of-pocket; $2,558m capitalised; $2,870m with post-approval R&D (2013 dollars) | [S38] |
| Wouters, McKee and Luyten (2020) | 63 of 355 new products approved by the FDA, 2009 to 2018, publicly available data | Median $985.3m (95% CI 683.6 to 1,228.9); mean $1,335.9m (95% CI 1,042.5 to 1,637.5) (2018 dollars) | [S39] |

Wouters et al. note that prior estimates ranged from $314 million to $2.8 billion [S39]. Even the lower estimates are large. The disagreement is about how large, not whether R&D is expensive. Neither figure tells us what price is *justified*, because prices are set by what markets will bear, not by cost.

### 8.2 Tiered pricing and parallel trade

Danzon and Towse argue that differential pricing (lower prices in poorer markets) is efficient and fair, but is undermined if cheap medicines leak back to rich markets through parallel trade, or if rich countries reference poor-country prices [S40]. They suggest confidential rebates, keep compulsory licensing as a fallback, and accept that diseases with no high-income market need other subsidies [S40]. This is a serious argument for secrecy in pricing, and it runs straight against the transparency case in section 9.

### 8.3 Do compulsory licences deter investment?

The evidence is limited and mixed in context:
- Thailand: no relationship found between its licences and foreign direct investment, 2002 to 2008 [S12].
- A historical study of US compulsory licensing of enemy-owned chemical patents after the First World War found that it *increased* domestic invention by about 20% [S41]. This is a different industry and era, so it does not settle the question for modern pharmaceuticals.
- The systematic review warns that wider compulsory licensing in large markets "may undermine private investments in R&D" [S13]. This is a concern raised in the literature, not a measured effect.

A fair reading: small-market licences for public-sector use show no measurable investment penalty in the studies found, but a pattern of licensing in large markets could change firms' behaviour.

### 8.4 Alternatives that reward innovation differently

- **Pull funding.** A $1.5 billion Advance Market Commitment for pneumococcal vaccines supported three vaccines; more than 150 million children were immunised and an estimated 700,000 lives saved [S42].
- **Not-for-profit R&D.** DNDi reports 17 treatments for six diseases since 2003, with a goal of 25 new treatments by 2028 [S43].
- **Prizes and delinkage.** Prize models that pay for outcomes instead of monopoly prices are widely proposed, but this study found no verified large-scale evaluation to cite, so no effect sizes are claimed.

## 9. A web and technology angle: open price data

Price secrecy weakens buyers. Two public resources show what transparency can look like:
- **WHO MI4A (Market Information for Access to Vaccines).** Its vaccine purchase database holds prices, volumes, manufacturers, procurement routes and contract details [S44]. The 2026 dataset, released 14 September 2026, covers purchases from 2005 to 2025 reported by 182 countries [S45]. Country names are anonymised for confidentiality [S45]. This is a compromise between transparency and the confidential-pricing concerns in section 8.2.
- **MSF Access** (formerly the Access Campaign) publishes price and policy analysis. On 3 November 2026, at the WHO Fair Pricing Forum, MSF called for stronger government action on prices, transparency and binding access conditions [S46].

**Design notes for builders** (proposals, not claims about existing tools):
- publish open, machine-readable price data with clear units, dates, currency and source;
- show net-versus-list caveats prominently;
- version datasets so that historical comparisons survive revisions, as with the financial-hardship indicator in section 2;
- anonymise where needed, but say so, as MI4A does.

## 10. Ethical analysis

| Value | Pulls toward | Tension |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Right to health and rescue | Overriding patents and price controls when people die for lack of a medicine | Can reduce future medicines if overused |
| Fair return for innovation | Strong patents, premium prices | Many cannot pay; prices often unrelated to value [S27] |
| Justice between countries | Tiered pricing, local production, benefit-sharing | Tiered pricing needs secrecy [S40]; local production needs scale and regulation |
| Transparency and accountability | Open price data | May collapse tiered pricing |
| Rule of law and legitimacy | Using TRIPS flexibilities through due process | Trade pressure can deter lawful use [S14] |

**Main trade-offs.**
1. **Now versus later.** Compulsory licences delivered real gains in Thailand and large price cuts across 24 events [S12], [S13]. The cost is uncertain future innovation. The evidence found does not show a measurable investment penalty from small-market licences, but this is an absence of evidence, not proof.
2. **Transparency versus differential pricing.** Open prices help buyers and accountability; confidential rebates may keep low prices possible in poor markets. A middle path is aggregated or anonymised transparency (as MI4A does) plus public reporting of net prices to governments.
3. **Sovereignty versus cooperation.** Compulsory licensing asserts national authority; voluntary licensing through MPP builds cooperation and has reported large modelled savings [S18], [S20]. They work best together: credible compulsory licensing strengthens voluntary negotiations.
4. **Local manufacturing versus efficiency.** Regional production improves resilience, as COVID-19 showed. But it is expensive and slow, and facilities without assured demand and regulatory recognition may sit idle. The AMA and the AVMA address regulation and demand, but outcomes are not yet measurable (status as of 18 November 2026).
5. **Patents are not the only barrier.** Low availability and high mark-ups on off-patent generics [S3] mean that procurement, supply chains and public financing matter as much as IP for most essential medicines.

## 11. Policy proposal (short)

1. **Make lawful flexibilities usable.** Countries should keep clear, quick domestic procedures for compulsory and government-use licences, with transparent criteria and due process, answering the procedural criticism in [S14] while protecting the right confirmed at Doha [S6].
2. **Attach access conditions to public money.** Publicly funded R&D should carry licensing terms, for example a default offer to the MPP for LMIC markets, and affordability commitments.
3. **Mandate net-price reporting.** Governments should share net purchase prices in an open, anonymised format modelled on MI4A [S44], [S45], extended from vaccines to essential medicines.
4. **Fund pull mechanisms and not-for-profit R&D** for neglected and low-margin needs, building on AMC and DNDi experience [S42], [S43].
5. **Fix the basics.** Pooled procurement, mark-up regulation and essential-medicine coverage in benefit packages, aimed at the availability and mark-up failures in [S3] and the financial hardship in [S4], [S5].
6. **Finish the pandemic bargain.** Conclude the PABS annex with enforceable benefit-sharing so that the Pandemic Agreement can open for signature [S36], [S37], and resolve the WTO extension question for therapeutics and diagnostics [S8], [S9].
7. **Back regional manufacturing with demand.** Pair African production targets [S29] with advance purchase commitments and AMA regulatory reliance [S30], [S31], so new plants have buyers and approvals.

## 12. Limits of this study

- The WTO's primary documents on the MC12 decision (its text and annual reviews) could not be opened; claims about its duration come from a peer-reviewed secondary source [S11], and its uptake is marked unverified.
- MPP impact figures are self-reported and modelled [S18], [S19]; the peer-reviewed savings estimate has MPP-affiliated authors [S20].
- The Nexavar terms come from a law-journal manuscript, not the official order [S16].
- No primary price-trend data for insulin outside the US were verified.
- Live statuses (PABS annex, Kigali facility, AMA, MC12 extension) are dated 18 November 2026 and will change.
- The 2017 Lancet Commission on essential medicines was checked as a DOI but not cited, because its figures could not be read in full this time.
