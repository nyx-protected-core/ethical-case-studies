# Women in male-majority fields: a full review and global recommendations

Date: 2026-11-19

## Summary

A global review of women's representation in engineering, computing, the trades, surgery, physical science, finance and leadership. Official counts, peer-reviewed evidence on causes and interventions, and recommendations by measure and region. Canadian data are excluded. Nothing here was published as a programme.

## Key takeaways

**The honest picture.** Women are not missing from skilled work. They are the majority in healthcare and education in every official series opened here, and they are at or near parity in much of the life and social sciences. The gaps that remain are concentrated in engineering, many computing jobs, the skilled trades, surgery, and the top posts in medicine, universities and companies. Those gaps are real, they differ by field and by region, and they are not explained by a shortage of capable women in the wider pipeline. They are also not proof, by themselves, that every workplace is discriminating, or that every field must reach a 50% share.

Key figures:

- **17.2%** — US architecture and engineering occupations, 2024. Official CPS published share [GOV-flag]
- **4.3%** — US construction and extraction occupations, 2024. The most male-majority group in this review [GOV-flag]
- **31.4%** — Women among researchers worldwide, 2023. UNESCO Institute for Statistics [GOV-flag]
- **Possible, with major change** — What the opened evidence supports. Some methods work; several flagship programmes do not, or not as advertised

- The student and graduate pipeline has moved further than the practitioner and leadership ranks. In the United States, women were 54.6% of medical students in 2023–24, 45% of full-time medical-school faculty, and 27% of deans [GOV-flag]. Among the 20 largest physician specialties in 2022, women were 24% of general surgeons and 6% of orthopaedic surgeons, and a majority in paediatrics, obstetrics and gynaecology, and dermatology.
- Interests and early study choices help explain which fields are male-majority, but they do not close the file. A large peer-reviewed interest difference lines up with the official occupational pattern: men, on average, lean toward things-oriented work and women toward people-oriented work. That is a group average, not a verdict on any one person, and it is not a finding that hiring is fair.
- Bias in evaluation has been shown in controlled studies, and some specific practices raise women's entry or retention. Same-gender peer mentoring at the college transition has strong, small-scale causal evidence in engineering. Dropping unnecessary prior-experience filters, with a redesigned first course, has strong before-and-after evidence at one computing college. Binding board quotas change the seats they cover, with little measured spillover into the wider pipeline.
- Several widely used programmes have mixed, weak, or null results. A national quasi-experiment of US NSF ADVANCE Institutional Transformation grants found no rise in women's faculty shares relative to comparison universities. The UK Athena SWAN charter changed institutional behaviour, but the best quantitative study opened here did not find an extra employment effect among early adopters. Generic diversity training, in a long firm-level study, was not followed by gains in women's management share and was followed by a decline for Black women.
- The same pattern appears across regions, at different levels. Official researcher shares are highest in Central Asia and in Latin America and the Caribbean, and lowest in Eastern Asia and Southern Asia. Occupational counts for engineering professionals are higher in several Latin American countries, Turkey, Australia and the United Kingdom than in Japan, India, Egypt, Nigeria or the United Arab Emirates. The trades are heavily male in every country with opened data. A dedicated global energy and mining series was not opened.

## How to read this review

The standard used here is equal participation on equal merit. A qualified person should not be shut out, discouraged, or preferred because of sex. That standard runs in both directions. It does not require the same share of women and men in every occupation, and it does not justify excluding men in order to hit a target. Obstetrics and gynaecology, paediatrics, nursing and school teaching have long been female-majority; the same standard would be concerned if qualified men were blocked there.

This is a global review. Canadian data, Canadian research and Canadian-affiliated authors are not used. Laws remain citable. Care work is treated as the responsibility of both parents. Parental leave, where it is discussed, means leave for both parents. This review does not use motherhood, pregnancy or family responsibilities to explain work, pay or merit gaps.

Sources are labelled as peer-reviewed, non-peer-reviewed scholarly, government or intergovernmental data, or secondary. Every government or intergovernmental figure is marked [GOV-flag] beside the figure. Official producers may also be charged with reporting progress on equity or development goals; survey methods, occupational classifications and coverage differ across countries; and professional associations have an interest in the story their numbers tell. The flag marks those incentives and limits. It is not a claim that a figure is false.

Definitions are not interchangeable. "STEM", "scientists and engineers", "science and engineering professionals", "architecture and engineering occupations", "STEM-qualified occupations" and "researchers" are different groups. Medical students are not practising surgeons. Board seats are not chief-executive posts. Apprenticeship starts are not completions. Unadjusted pay gaps are not a measure of unequal pay for the same work. ILOSTAT occupational shares are official labour-force counts under the International Standard Classification of Occupations; they are not the same as US Current Population Survey (CPS) groups, and the US analysis below uses the CPS and National Science Foundation series, not the ILO United States extract, for that reason. No figure in this page was invented. If a source could not be opened, that is marked UNVERIFIED or listed as a gap.

## Engineering

Engineering is the clearest professional example of a long, persistent male majority that is not a general absence of women from technical or scientific work. In the United States in 2024, official CPS Table 11 put women at 17.2% of architecture and engineering occupations [GOV-flag]. The same year's Table 9 counted 613,000 women and 2,940,000 men in that group, or 613,000 of 3,552,000. Dividing those rounded counts gives 17.3%; the official published share is 17.2%, and that is the figure used here [GOV-flag].

The detailed US shares show a gradient, not a single wall. Architecture and industrial engineering are in the mid-20s; civil engineering matches the group average; mechanical, electrical, aerospace and engineering management sit close to one in ten. That gradient matters for cause stories. If the only problem were a uniform hiring bias against women in "engineering," the specialties would not line up so neatly with how close the work sits to design, production, machines and management of technical teams.

The gap is already visible before the first job. Among people with a bachelor's or advanced degree in 2023, men were 83% of those whose highest degree was in engineering [GOV-flag]. Official US science-workforce data for the same year put men at 73% of science and engineering occupations and at 89% of STEM middle-skill jobs, while women were 65% of science-and-engineering-related occupations, a group that includes many health-linked technical jobs [GOV-flag]. Sex differences in the technical workforce are therefore not only a story about university engineering degrees. They are also a story about jobs entered through training, experience and apprenticeships.

Elsewhere, official counts depend on what is being counted. Eurostat's "scientists and engineers" series is broader than professional engineering. In 2024 it counted 7.9 million women in the European Union, 40.5% of that workforce, but only 22.4% in manufacturing, which is the closer official comparator for engineering-heavy work [GOV-flag]. The country pattern inside that series is wide: 50.9% in Latvia and 30.7% in Finland [GOV-flag]. In England in 2023/24, women were 52.2% of all apprenticeship starts and 10.3% of starts in Engineering and Manufacturing Technologies [GOV-flag]. ILOSTAT labour-force counts of "science and engineering professionals" for 2025, unless noted, put women at 28.5% in Australia, 26.5% in the United Kingdom, 33.9% in Brazil, 28.2% in Mexico, 36.5% in Argentina, 31.0% in Turkey, 13.1% in India, 13.3% in Egypt (2024), 9.8% in Nigeria and 9.8% in the United Arab Emirates, and at 13.9% in Japan in 2023 [GOV-flag]. These ILO groups are wider than US "architecture and engineering occupations" and are not comparable one-for-one with the 17.2% US figure.

On causes, the opened evidence is strongest on interests and on the early pipeline, and thinner on hiring inside engineering firms. Su and Rounds (2015), peer-reviewed, report a large average sex difference on a things-versus-people interest dimension (d = 0.93), and a male advantage of d = 0.83 for professional-level engineering interests. The percentage of women in a STEM field was strongly correlated with the size of the interest difference (r = −0.89). Those are group averages. They help explain why official series show women as a majority or near-majority in some scientific and health jobs and a small minority in engineering, and they do not prove that any given hiring process is fair.

What has been measured to change the engineering pipeline is modest in scale and mixed at the institutional level. In a randomised field experiment, 150 incoming women engineering students assigned to a female peer mentor, rather than a male mentor or no mentor, were more likely to keep a sense of belonging, self-efficacy, motivation, and retention in the major through the first two years, the window of greatest STEM attrition (Dennehy and Dasgupta, 2017, peer-reviewed, abstract opened). That is the clearest causal evidence in this review that a specific, time-limited practice raises women's retention in engineering without any claim that men should be pushed out. Limits: one institution, women participants only, modest sample. By contrast, a 2025 national quasi-experiment of NSF ADVANCE Institutional Transformation grants found that receiving those grants was not followed by a higher proportion or headcount of women faculty, including new hires and tenured and tenure-track faculty, relative to multiple comparison groups (Hu and Fernandez, peer-reviewed, abstract opened). Faculty equity programmes of that kind should not be described as a proven method for raising engineering faculty shares.

## Computing and technology

Computing sits between engineering and the broader scientific workforce: clearly male-majority in most official occupation counts, but less so than mechanical or electrical engineering, and more movable in a small number of carefully documented colleges than in the national labour market.

|---|---|---|
| US computer and mathematical occupations, 2024 | 26.4% | CPS Table 11 [GOV-flag] |
| US software developers, 2024 | 20.3% | CPS Table 11 [GOV-flag] |
| US computer programmers, 2024 | 17.8% | CPS Table 11 [GOV-flag] |
| US computer network architects, 2024 | 7.1% | CPS Table 11 [GOV-flag] |
| US college graduates in computer and mathematical occupations, 2023 | 26.5% (1,214,000 of 4,573,000) | National Survey of College Graduates [GOV-flag] |
| Highest degree in computer and mathematical sciences, US, 2023 | Men 70% | NSF Indicators [GOV-flag] |
| England ICT apprenticeship starts, 2023/24 | 36.4% (9,860 of 27,090) | Starts, not completions; England only [GOV-flag] |

The US occupation share of 26.4% in 2024 is almost identical to the 26.5% share among college graduates in computer and mathematical occupations in 2023, which suggests that the national computing gap is not mainly a story of qualified women being locked out after graduation. Software development, at 20.3%, is lower than the broad computer-and-mathematical group; network architecture, at 7.1%, is as male-majority as some engineering specialties [GOV-flag]. England's ICT apprenticeship route is more open, at 36.4% of starts, than its engineering and construction routes, which sit near 10% [GOV-flag]. ILOSTAT counts of ICT professionals for 2025 put women at 23.0% in Australia, 22.2% in the United Kingdom, 27.5% in India, 20.7% in Brazil, 23.7% in Turkey, 17.5% in Argentina, 16.5% in Egypt (2024), 15.1% in Mexico, 14.0% in Chile (2022) and 13.7% in the United Arab Emirates [GOV-flag]. India's ICT share is higher than its science-and-engineering-professional share (13.1%), a reminder that "tech" and "engineering" should not be averaged into one global story.

The strongest opened evidence that a college can change the computing mix comes from Harvey Mudd College. A peer-reviewed 2010 conference paper reports that women were more than one-third of the student body but less than 10% of computer-science majors before 2006. In that year the department introduced a breadth-first introductory course aimed especially at students with less prior experience, first-year trips to a major women's computing conference used as recruitment, and summer research after the first year. In the 2010 evaluation, the share of women majors almost doubled, to 20%; the rise in women choosing the major was statistically significant, while the change in men majoring in computer science was not significant at the same threshold. Students in the new course were also more likely than students in the old course to say it changed how they thought about the subject (75.4% against 46.5%), a gain reported for the class as a whole, not only for women. A later peer-reviewed article, abstract opened here, reports that over seven years the share of women in the major rose from an average near 12% to around 40%, and held at about that level for four years, close to the college share of women, a little over 40%. Limits: one small, selective college; before-and-after, not randomised; the changes came as a package, so the first-course redesign cannot be isolated from the conference visits and research offers; transfer to large public universities is unproven in the sources opened here. The merit principle is intact in one important respect: the barrier that was lowered was prior informal experience, not the academic standard.

Carnegie Mellon University later reported entering computer-science classes that were 48% women in 2016, just over 49% in 2017, and 105 of 211 in 2018, against a national figure, cited by the authors, of 17.9% of US computer-science bachelor's degrees awarded to women in 2016. Those enrolment counts come from a 2019 authors' full-text account, a magazine-column style description, not a peer-reviewed trial. The account says the university dropped a prior programming-background admissions requirement in 1999 while keeping high mathematics and science standards, created multiple first-year entry points, and built a student and faculty organisation around professional and social opportunities. This review treats that history as a descriptive institutional example, not as ranked causal evidence. The package cannot be separated into one cause in the text opened here, and a selective private university is not a template that can be assumed to travel.

The wider evidence on "encouragement" is a warning against treating applications as the outcome that matters. In a randomised trial of 3,945 political-science graduate students, a short personalised encouragement to apply to a technical conference raised applications from both women and men, slightly more for women, but treated women were then accepted at a lower rate than control-group women, which the authors linked to lower rates of asking for supporting letters (Unkovic, Sen and Quinn, 2016, peer-reviewed). Opening a door can expose the next barrier. It is not, by itself, a computing-workforce policy.

## The trades and construction

The skilled trades are the most male-majority field in this review, in every country with opened official data, and they are also among the most dangerous jobs. Any serious account has to hold both facts at once.

Official ILOSTAT counts of building and related trades workers tell the same story in other regions: 3.6% women in Australia in 2025, 2.8% in the United Kingdom, 2.8% in Brazil, 1.4% in India, 1.1% in Argentina, 0.6% in Turkey, 0.1% in Egypt (2024), 0.7% in the United Arab Emirates (2022), 6.7% in Mexico, and 3.4% in Chile (2022) [GOV-flag]. Electrical and electronic trades are in the same band, often between 0.5% and 5%. South Africa publishes these data on an older classification (ISCO-88); in 2025, women were 4.9% of extraction and building trades workers and 5.3% of metal and machinery trades workers under that classification [GOV-flag]. The numbers should not be averaged with professional engineering or with "STEM" as a whole. A country can have a substantial share of women among science and engineering professionals and still have almost no women in the trades.

Interest research again lines up with the official pattern. Su and Rounds report a very large male advantage in interests for mechanics and related fields (d = 1.21), larger than for professional engineering. That is one reason the trades have moved more slowly than computing or medicine, and it is not a reason to treat exclusion, harassment, or badly designed entry routes as irrelevant. Pre-apprenticeship and trades-bridge programmes are widely used. No peer-reviewed causal evaluation of those programmes was opened for this review. They are marked UNTESTED here.

In 2023 the official US Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries recorded 5,283 fatal work injuries. Men accounted for 4,832 of them (91.5%); women accounted for 447 (8.5%). Construction had the most deaths of any industry (1,075), including 421 from falls, slips and trips. Construction trades occupations accounted for 809 deaths; construction and extraction occupations as a group accounted for 1,055 [GOV-flag]. These are the same jobs in which official data put women at about 4%. Men's share of deaths is both a reflection of who holds dangerous jobs and a social-justice fact in its own right. Improving access to the trades for qualified women does not make the death toll among men a side issue. Women were 18.3% of workplace homicides (84 of 458), a higher share than of deaths overall [GOV-flag], which is a different risk pattern and should not be folded into the same sentence as falls from height.

## Surgery and medicine

Medicine is the field in this review where the pipeline has changed furthest, and where the remaining gaps are most clearly about specialty and seniority rather than about entry to the profession.

By 2023, women were the majority of applicants and graduates at US medical schools [GOV-flag]. The faculty share has moved a long way toward parity. The top academic posts have not caught up: deans at 27%, chairs at 25%. Inside the profession, the split by specialty is as sharp as the split between engineering and health in the wider labour market. General surgery has more than doubled its women's share since 2004 and is still at 24%. Orthopaedic surgery and urology have risen from a very low base and remain small minorities. Paediatrics, obstetrics and gynaecology, and dermatology are majority female [GOV-flag]. The under-representation of interest is in surgery and in the top academic and executive posts, not in healthcare work as a whole.

AAMC's official report notes structural, institutional and cultural barriers, including harassment and pay gaps. Nearly one in three women reported gender harassment in academic medicine [GOV-flag]. That figure is official survey evidence from a member association with an interest in reporting equity problems and progress, not a peer-reviewed trial, and it is not a measured estimate of how much harassment contributes to the specialty or leadership gaps. It is still direct evidence that hostile climate is commonly reported in the setting where the leadership gap persists.

Large observational studies do not show that women deliver worse care when they hold these jobs. Among elderly US Medicare patients hospitalised in 2011–2014, those treated by female internists had lower adjusted 30-day mortality (11.07% versus 11.49%) and readmission (15.02% versus 15.57%) than those treated by male internists in the same hospital (Tsugawa and colleagues, 2017, peer-reviewed, abstract opened). These are observational findings. Residual confounding is possible, the differences are small, and they are not a claim that men are poorer clinicians. They do remove any quality-based case for keeping qualified women out of hospital medicine. This review does not use Canadian surgical-outcome studies.

The UK Athena SWAN charter is the main opened test of a sector-wide academic-medicine equity programme, and the evidence is mixed. The charter, established in 2005, is a voluntary framework in which institutions submit gender-equality action plans and may receive awards. In 2011, the National Institute for Health Research said it would shortlist certain research grants only from medical schools with a silver award. A peer-reviewed study of UK medical schools found a marked improvement in women's success between 2004 and 2013, but early adopters did not increase female participation by more than institutions that signed later, and the funding link had no measured career effect in that window (Gregory-Smith, 2018, abstract opened). Qualitative work at a research-intensive university in Oxford found that both women and men often described structural and cultural changes, but also concern that the award could become an end in itself, resentment about perceived positive discrimination, a lingering lack of work–life balance, and a view that the charter cannot by itself remove long-standing power and pay imbalances; many thought the changes would not have happened without the funding link (Ovseiko and colleagues, 2017, peer-reviewed, full text). A realist evaluation in five departments of one medical school found that women staff did a disproportionate share of the Athena SWAN work, with possible costs to those women's own careers, and that early-career researchers had trouble accessing initiatives (Caffrey and colleagues, 2016, abstract opened). A 2020 BMJ analysis of the funding incentive was not available in full in the text opened for this review; any claim about the size of its effect on women's research leadership is UNVERIFIED.

## Physics and mathematics

Physical science and mathematics sit between the near-parity pattern of the life and social sciences and the much larger gaps in engineering. The official US data that were opened for this review are mainly about degrees and about a mixed "life, physical and social science" occupation group, not about physicists or mathematicians as standalone jobs. A more detailed CPS occupation split was not re-opened for this page (the statistical office's table was blocked on this pass) and is not invented here.

| US measure, 2023 unless noted | Women's share | Source and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Highest degree in physical and related sciences | Men 69% (women about 31%) | NSF Indicators, American Community Survey [GOV-flag] |
| Highest degree in computer and mathematical sciences | Men 70% | This mixes computing with mathematics [GOV-flag] |
| Highest degree in biological, agricultural and environmental life sciences | Women 53% | NSF Indicators [GOV-flag] |
| Highest degree in social and related sciences | Women 60% | NSF Indicators [GOV-flag] |
| College graduates working as biological and life scientists | 49.8% (405,000 of 813,000) | National Survey of College Graduates [GOV-flag] |
| College graduates working as social and related scientists | 63.1% (398,000 of 631,000) | National Survey of College Graduates [GOV-flag] |
| Life, physical and social science occupations, 2024 | 50.7% | CPS Table 9; a mixed group, not a physics count [GOV-flag] |

The degree data already show the internal split that official "STEM" headlines hide. Women are a slight majority among people whose highest degree is in the life sciences, and a clear majority in the social sciences. They are a minority, at about three in ten, among those whose highest degree is in the physical sciences [GOV-flag]. Mathematics cannot be read off cleanly from the official computer-and-mathematical degree share, because that group is dominated by computing. Interest research, which can separate the fields, finds smaller average sex differences in mathematics (d = 0.38) and the physical sciences (d = 0.56) than in professional engineering (d = 0.83), and little or no male advantage in biological or medical science (Su and Rounds, 2015, peer-reviewed). That ordering matches the official degree and occupation pattern reasonably well. It is not a complete account of who stays, who is hired, or who becomes a professor.

A related cross-country claim, the so-called gender-equality paradox, says that sex differences in relative academic strengths and in the pursuit of STEM degrees are often larger in countries that rank higher on gender-equality indices (Stoet and Geary, 2018, peer-reviewed). Girls performed similarly to or better than boys in science in two of every three countries in the PISA 2015 data the authors used, and in nearly all countries more girls appeared capable of college-level STEM study than were enrolled. The association they reported between the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Index and a "propensity" of women relative to men to graduate in STEM was r(s) = −0.47 (n = 50). A 2020 corrigendum in the same journal, itself peer-reviewed, clarified that this STEM figure is a propensity — the share of women graduates who took STEM degrees, relative to the same share for men — and equals the percentage of women in STEM only if equal numbers of men and women enrol at university. After that clarification, the propensity ranged from 12.4 in Macao to 40.7 in Algeria, with a median of 25.4. China was not entered as a whole, because the PISA sample was not nationally representative. A later commentary that re-analysed the association with other measures was not opened; its coefficients are not used. The finding is therefore a serious, measurement-sensitive between-country pattern, not a settled law, and not a reason to ignore barriers inside specific workplaces.

## Finance and leadership

Leadership gaps persist even in fields where women are already the majority of the qualified workforce. That is a different problem from the engineering or trades pipeline, and it needs different tools.

In US education, women are 73.4% of the occupation group and 49.9% of postsecondary teachers, but only one in three college presidents, with men still outnumbering women about two to one [GOV-flag]. Associate institutions have the highest share of women leaders. The ACE brief notes differences in career pathways — women presidents were more likely to come from a faculty or academic background — but does not isolate a single cause. Mentoring and leadership-development programmes for aspiring presidents are widely used. This review opened no peer-reviewed trial that estimates their effect on the national share of women presidents. They are marked UNTESTED here.

Binding board rules are the rare leadership intervention with strong evidence, and the evidence has a clear edge. In late 2003 Norway mandated 40% representation of each gender on the boards of public limited companies. A peer-reviewed study in the Review of Economic Studies, opened here as the NBER abstract, finds that women appointed after the reform were observably more qualified than their female predecessors, and that the gender gap in earnings within boards fell substantially. It found no robust evidence that the reform benefited the larger set of women employed in the affected companies, and no clear impact on highly qualified women whose credentials resembled those of board members but who were not appointed. Seven years after the quota was fully in effect, the authors concluded that it had very little discernible impact on women in business beyond its direct effect on the women who entered boardrooms (Bertrand, Black, Jensen and Lleras-Muney, 2019). Official EU monitoring shows a large rise in women's share of the largest listed-company boards between 2012 and 2025, during a period of national quotas, soft measures and then an EU directive [GOV-flag]. The honest reading is straightforward. Binding board rules do what they are written to do: they raise women's share of the seats they cover, and in Norway the women appointed were not less qualified than those they followed. They have not, on the evidence opened here, been shown to transform the wider female managerial pipeline.

Firm-level evidence on internal corporate tools points in the same mixed direction. Kalev, Dobbin and Kelly (2006), peer-reviewed, analysed federal workforce records for 708 US private-sector establishments from 1971 to 2002. Assigning organisational responsibility — for example through a diversity committee — was followed by increases in the predicted managerial share for white women (13.9%), Black women (29.8%) and Black men (10.0%), and a 3.0% decline for white men. Mentoring programmes were followed by a 23.5% increase for Black women and little change for white women (a 0.5% decline). Diversity training was followed by a 5.9% decline in the predicted proportion of Black women in management, and by no change for white women. This is observational firm-level evidence with establishment fixed effects, not a randomised trial, and it is about management composition in large US firms, not specifically about engineering or finance licences. It is still peer-reviewed evidence that legalistic or bias-training tools can fail or backfire, while assigning responsibility can be followed by broader gains, including for men in underrepresented groups.

## Energy and mining

A dedicated global official series on women's share of energy or mining occupations was not opened for this review. That is a gap, not a licence to borrow figures from industry reports that were not checked.

The closest opened official groups overlap only in part. In the United States, construction and extraction occupations were 4.3% women in 2024 [GOV-flag]. That group includes extraction work but is dominated by construction, and it is not a count of petroleum engineers, mine managers or power-plant operators. In South Africa, under the older ISCO-88 classification, women were 4.9% of extraction and building trades workers in 2025 [GOV-flag], again a mixed group. Until a specific energy or mining series is opened, this review does not state a worldwide women's share for those industries, and it does not treat construction-and-extraction figures as if they were an energy workforce statistic.

## Cross-cutting causes

No single cause explains every gap in this review, and the opened evidence is not evenly strong across mechanisms. The useful way to read the literature is to ask, for each mechanism, what it can and cannot account for.

Pipeline, interests and prior study. This is the strongest descriptive cluster. Large average sex differences in vocational interests line up with which STEM fields are male- or female-majority. Official degree data show that the US engineering and physical-science gaps are already present at graduation, not only at the point of employment. Prior informal experience, especially in computing, can act as a non-merit filter, which is why Harvey Mudd's first-course redesign is relevant. The cross-country "paradox" pattern is real in the papers opened here and is sensitive to how STEM graduation is measured. Interests and early study choices help explain why engineering and the trades look different from medicine and the life sciences. They do not measure the share of any national employment gap that is due to hiring bias, pay setting, or attrition.

Recruitment and hiring. Two peer-reviewed experiments show that, in defined tasks, sex can affect evaluation. Moss-Racusin and colleagues (2012) found that US science faculty rated the same laboratory-manager application as more competent and more hireable, and offered a higher starting salary, when it carried a male name rather than a female name; male and female faculty showed the same pattern. Reuben, Sapienza and Zingales (2014) found that, in a laboratory labour market for an arithmetic task on which men and women performed equally well on average, employers were twice as likely to hire a man when they had no information except appearance; disclosing past performance raised the female hire rate from 33.9% to 43.0%, which reduced the disparity but did not eliminate it. These studies are direct evidence of bias in the settings they test. They do not measure how much of the official engineering, trades, or surgical gap they explain, and later academic-hiring experiments sometimes cited as finding a preference for equivalently qualified women were not opened here.

Retention, workplace culture and harassment. Same-gender peer mentoring has causal evidence for retention in an engineering major over two years. Official survey evidence from US academic medicine finds that nearly one in three women reported gender harassment [GOV-flag]. That is important climate evidence. It is not a causal estimate of exits from surgery or from academic leadership. National Academies and industry-body commentary on mid-career exits was not used as a numbered source in the technical-workforce case this review draws on, and those claims are not imported here as if they had been opened as peer-reviewed trials.

Pay. Unadjusted official US full-time weekly earnings in 2023 were lower for women than for men in architecture and engineering (women's median 87.7% of men's), in computer and mathematical occupations (82.4%), and in construction and extraction (80.2%) [GOV-flag]. The statistical office states, on the same page, that these comparisons are on a broad level and do not control for many factors that may be important in explaining earnings differences. An unadjusted median gap is consistent with unequal pay for the same work, with different specialties, hours, experience, or bargaining, or with some mix of these. It does not, by itself, isolate discrimination.

Networks and sponsorship. Mentoring helped some groups and not others in the Kalev firm-level study, and female peer mentors helped incoming women engineers in the Dennehy and Dasgupta experiment. Presidential mentoring programmes, and informal sponsorship into dean, chair, or chief-executive posts, were not causally evaluated in the sources opened here (UNTESTED).

Stereotypes. The hiring experiments above are evidence that stereotypes can affect evaluation even when the file or the task is held constant. Interest differences can themselves be shaped, over time, by stereotypes; the opened papers do not isolate that loop, and this review does not pretend they do. Safety engineering offers a related, but distinct, point about design defaults. In US crash data for 1998–2008, the odds of severe injury for a belt-restrained female driver were 47% higher (95% confidence interval 28% to 70%) than for a belt-restrained male driver in a comparable crash (Bose, Segui-Gomez and Crandall, 2011, peer-reviewed, abstract opened). That is a reason for sex-specific design and regulation of vehicles, instruments and protective equipment. The paper does not measure, and this review does not claim, that changing the sex mix of the engineering workforce would close the injury gap.

## North America, except Canada

The United States is the best-measured country in this review, and its official pattern is consistent across household surveys and science-workforce surveys. Women were 17.2% of architecture and engineering occupations and 26.4% of computer and mathematical occupations in 2024, 4.3% of construction and extraction occupations, 75.8% of healthcare practitioners and technical occupations, and 73.4% of education occupations [GOV-flag]. Among college graduates, women were 18.1% of engineers and 26.5% of computer and mathematical scientists, and near parity or in the majority in life-science and social-science jobs [GOV-flag]. Leadership lags the pipeline in medicine and in higher education: 54.6% of medical students, 27% of deans, 33% of college presidents [GOV-flag].

What has measurably worked here is local and specific: same-gender peer mentoring in an engineering college; a redesigned computing entry route at one college; orchestra screens in a defined performance market; and, in firms, assigning responsibility rather than relying on training. What has not, on the opened national evidence, is the flagship federal faculty-transformation grant model. Board quotas are not the US institutional starting point; structured hiring and accountable selection, plus serious enforcement against harassment, are a better fit than importing a Norwegian-style seat rule for a market in which most firms are not under a single company-law regime.

Mexico, in official ILOSTAT counts for 2025, had women at 28.2% of science and engineering professionals, 15.1% of ICT professionals, 6.7% of building trades workers, 62.9% of health professionals, and 40.3% of chief executives, senior officials and legislators, the last of those from a relatively small occupational count [GOV-flag]. The engineering-professional share is higher than the US architecture-and-engineering occupation share, but the groups are not the same, and Mexican ICT and trades shares remain clearly male-majority. Tools that travel best are those that remove non-merit filters in education and hiring, not a single imported quota.

## Europe

Europe has the only opened official time series in which women's share of a technical workforce category has risen sharply in raw numbers, and the only opened causal evaluation of a binding board quota. Eurostat counted 7.9 million women among people employed as scientists and engineers in the EU in 2024, up from 3.4 million in 2008, or 40.5% of that broad workforce and 22.4% in manufacturing [GOV-flag]. Finland, often ranked high on gender-equality indices, sat at 30.7% on that series; Latvia sat at 50.9% [GOV-flag]. That spread is a description of official counts, not by itself a proof of the paradox literature, though it is one of the patterns that literature tries to explain.

England's official apprenticeship data for 2023/24 show how little the all-subject average tells you: women were 52.2% of all starts, 36.4% in ICT, 10.3% in engineering and manufacturing, and 10.0% in construction [GOV-flag]. ILOSTAT counts for the United Kingdom in 2025 put women at 26.5% of science and engineering professionals, 22.2% of ICT professionals, 2.8% of building trades workers, 74.9% of health professionals, and 27.1% of chief executives, senior officials and legislators [GOV-flag]. Turkey, in the same official series, had a relatively high science-and-engineering-professional share (31.0%) and a very low building-trades share (0.6%) [GOV-flag].

The practical European lesson is to use hard rules only for defined seats, and to be honest about spillover. Board quotas and the later EU directive have moved listed-company boards. They have not been shown to rebuild the managerial pipeline. Athena SWAN shows that a funding-linked charter can change institutional attention without a clear extra effect on women's careers in the early window studied. Peer mentoring, first-course redesign in computing, and structured evaluation are better candidates for universities and employers than another layer of awards. Trades policy should be evaluated properly, because the official apprenticeship shares have not, on their own, established that current outreach works. Parental leave designed for both parents is already common in much of the region; this review did not open a study that ties those leave rules to women's share of engineering or surgery, and it does not treat leave design as an explanation of those gaps.

## East Asia

Official researcher data, not occupation data, are the only opened comparable regional series for Eastern Asia as a whole. In 2022, women were 23.5% of researchers in Eastern Asia, the lowest regional share in the UNESCO Institute for Statistics release opened here, against a global figure of 31.1% that year and 31.4% in 2023 [GOV-flag]. A 2026 UIS paper reports 26.6% for "East Asia and the Pacific" in 2023, a different regional grouping that should not be compared directly with the 2022 Eastern Asia figure [GOV-flag]. In both versions, East Asia sits well below Latin America, Central Asia and North Africa on researcher shares.

Japan is the only East Asian country for which a recent ILOSTAT occupational extract was obtained. In 2023, women were 13.9% of science and engineering professionals, 69.6% of health professionals, 14.5% of administrative and commercial managers, and 45.2% of all employed people [GOV-flag]. Several occupation groups, including ICT professionals and the building trades, were not published in that extract. Official ILOSTAT extracts for China and the Republic of Korea for 2022–2025 returned no data in the query used for this review; those country occupational shares are a gap, not a zero.

The opened evidence does not support a claim that East Asian gaps would close automatically with higher national gender-equality scores. If anything, the paradox literature, read with its corrigendum, is a reason not to expect that. What may travel are methods that do not depend on a Western university charter: dropping non-merit prior-experience filters in computing, structured evaluation, and peer mentoring at the point of entry to engineering programmes. Board quotas are a weaker fit in systems where listed-company law and informal career sponsorship differ from Norway's. This review has no opened East Asian trial of those methods and should not pretend otherwise.

## South Asia

Southern Asia had 26.9% women among researchers in 2022, below the world average [GOV-flag]. The 2026 UIS paper reports the same 26.9% for a broader "South and West Asia" grouping in 2023 [GOV-flag]. India is the only South Asian country with an opened recent occupational extract, and it is large enough to matter on its own.

In official ILOSTAT counts for 2025, women were 31.2% of all employed people in India, 13.1% of science and engineering professionals, 27.5% of ICT professionals, 44.1% of health professionals, 1.4% of building trades workers, 0.6% of electrical and electronic trades workers, and 11.6% of both chief executives, senior officials and legislators and administrative and commercial managers [GOV-flag]. Two features stand out. First, ICT is substantially more open to women than engineering-professional work, the reverse of some Latin American and North African extracts, and close to or above several high-income ICT shares. Second, leadership and the trades remain heavily male. Health is much less female-majority than in the United States, Australia or the United Kingdom, so the usual "women already dominate care work" contrast is weaker here and should not be imported uncritically.

Recommendations that fit this pattern, if they are evaluated rather than copied, are computing entry routes that do not demand informal prior experience, structured hiring in engineering organisations, and a serious look at trade-apprenticeship design. Board quotas would touch only a thin layer of formal listed firms. No opened Indian trial of these methods was reviewed here. Researcher-share figures should not be used as if they were engineering-workforce figures.

## Middle East and North Africa

Official researcher data and official occupational data tell different stories in this region, and both should be kept in view. In 2022, women were 43.2% of researchers in Northern Africa and 35.8% in Western Asia, both above the world average of 31.1% [GOV-flag]. That is a research-personnel statistic, not a count of engineers, technicians or tradespeople.

Occupational extracts are more mixed, and in some countries they are shaped by overall female labour-force participation. In Egypt in 2024, women were 22.7% of all employed people, 13.3% of science and engineering professionals, 16.5% of ICT professionals, 53.2% of health professionals, 0.1% of building trades workers, and 21.3% of chief executives, senior officials and legislators [GOV-flag]. In the United Arab Emirates in 2025, women were 19.7% of all employed people, 9.8% of science and engineering professionals, 13.7% of ICT professionals, 52.7% of health professionals, and 11.7% of chief executives, senior officials and legislators; building trades were 0.7% women in 2022 [GOV-flag]. A large male migrant workforce is part of the UAE context and is a reason not to read those shares as if they were a simple story about women's education. Turkey, often grouped with Europe in official statistics but relevant here as a regional comparison, had a much higher science-and-engineering-professional share (31.0%) and still almost no women in the building trades (0.6%) in 2025 [GOV-flag]. Official ILOSTAT extracts for Tunisia, Morocco and Saudi Arabia for 2022–2025 returned no data in the query used here.

The Stoet and Geary corrigendum listed Algeria at the high end of their STEM-propensity range (40.7). That is a propensity among graduates, not an occupational share, and it should not be quoted as the share of Algerian engineers who are women. The practical implication is still useful: in parts of this region, the binding constraint is not always the supply of women with science training. It may be the translation of that training into engineering work, trades work, and leadership in the private sector. Structured hiring and the removal of non-merit filters are the opened methods most worth testing. Board quotas will do little where listed-company boards are not the main locus of economic power. This review opened no regional trial.

## Sub-Saharan Africa

In 2022, women were 33.4% of researchers in Sub-Saharan Africa, slightly above the world average, in a region that official UIS data also show has a very low density of researchers overall, around 91 per million inhabitants [GOV-flag]. Representation among a small research workforce is not the same as a large engineering or trades workforce with a healthy female share.

Two national occupational extracts were obtained. In Nigeria in 2025, women were 50.0% of all employed people, 9.8% of science and engineering professionals, 46.5% of health professionals, 1.3% of building trades workers, and 18.5% of chief executives, senior officials and legislators in 2024 [GOV-flag]. ICT professionals were not published in that extract. In South Africa in 2025, the labour-force survey still uses ISCO-88, so computing cannot be split out of the professional science group: women were 44.2% of all employed people, 25.1% of physical, mathematical and engineering science professionals (a group that includes computing under that older classification), 66.2% of life-science and health professionals, 4.9% of extraction and building trades workers, 5.3% of metal and machinery trades workers, and 38.5% of corporate managers [GOV-flag]. Official ILOSTAT extracts for Kenya for 2022–2025 returned no data in the query used here.

The Nigerian engineering-professional share is among the lowest in this review; the South African mixed professional-science share is closer to European occupational figures, on a classification that is not strictly comparable. In both countries the trades are heavily male. Recommendations that fit a small formal engineering sector are not board quotas. They are training pathways that do not waste women who already study science, structured hiring in the formal firms and public utilities that do exist, and evaluated trades-entry programmes. Peer-mentoring evidence comes from a US college and would need local testing. This review opened no Sub-Saharan causal evaluation of those methods.

## Latin America

Latin America and the Caribbean had 45.3% women among researchers in 2022, one of the two highest regional shares, alongside Central Asia at 46.5% [GOV-flag]. Official occupational extracts for 2025, and for Chile in 2022, show a related but more complicated picture in the workforce.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Argentina, 2025 | 36.5% | 17.5% | 1.1% | 68.3% | 58.6% (small occupational count) |
| Brazil, 2025 | 33.9% | 20.7% | 2.8% | 68.9% | 26.9% |
| Mexico, 2025 | 28.2% | 15.1% | 6.7% | 62.9% | 40.3% (small occupational count) |

Engineering-professional shares in these countries are higher than Japan's 13.9%, India's 13.1%, or the US architecture-and-engineering occupation share of 17.2%, though the last of those is a different group and should not be used as a straight ranking. ICT is more male-majority than engineering-professional work in Argentina, Chile and Mexico, the reverse of the Indian pattern. The trades are heavily male everywhere. Some leadership shares sit on small occupational counts and should be read with care; Argentina's 58.6% figure is a particularly small group (about 40,000 people in the official extract) and is not a finding that Argentine corporate leadership is majority female.

If the goal is to open the remaining male-majority fields, Latin America is not starting from a weak researcher pipeline. The tighter problems, on the opened counts, are computing occupations, the trades, and the translation of professional shares into executive posts in large firms. Board rules may have more purchase here than in much of Sub-Saharan Africa, because there is a formal listed-company sector, but the Norwegian spillover evidence still applies: seats can change without the pipeline changing. Structured hiring, computing-entry redesign, and evaluated trades programmes are the better fit for the gaps that remain.

## Oceania

Australia is the only Oceanian country with both an opened official national STEM monitor and a full ILOSTAT occupational extract. Official ILOSTAT extracts for New Zealand for 2022–2025 returned no data in the query used here, which is a gap for the region.

The Australian government's STEM Equity Monitor, drawing on the Australian Bureau of Statistics Labour Force Survey, reports that in 2025 women made up around 300,000 employees in a STEM-qualified workforce of about 1.8 million, or 16%, up from 15% in each year from 2021 to 2024 and from 11% in 2005 [GOV-flag]. From 2015 to 2025 the number of women in those occupations almost doubled, from about 154,200 to 296,100; the number of men rose by about 26%, from 1,234,800 to 1,553,400. In occupations where most of the workforce has university STEM qualifications, women's share rose from 19% to 24% over that decade. In VET STEM-qualified occupations — the official examples include electricians, motor mechanics, cabinetmakers and aircraft maintenance engineers — women were only 3% in 2025, down from a peak of 4% in 2024 [GOV-flag]. The department has a policy brief to monitor STEM equity, which is the reporting incentive flagged here; the underlying labour-force counts are still official statistics. In the same years, women have been about 50% of people in non-STEM occupations and 75% of health occupations [GOV-flag].

ILOSTAT counts for Australia in 2025 put women at 28.5% of science and engineering professionals, 23.0% of ICT professionals, 3.6% of building trades workers, 3.3% of electrical and electronic trades workers, 73.6% of health professionals, 32.3% of chief executives, senior officials and legislators, and 48.1% of all employed people [GOV-flag]. The 28.5% professional-engineering-and-science figure and the 16% STEM-qualified-occupation figure can both be right, because the monitor's STEM-qualified group includes a large VET-trades block that the professional group does not. Australia therefore looks, at once, closer to European professional shares and as male-majority as other countries in the trades.

Recommendations that fit are close to the European and North American set: peer mentoring and first-course redesign in universities; structured hiring; evaluated trades-bridge programmes, which are especially relevant given the 3% VET figure and are still UNTESTED in the sources opened here; and parental leave already designed for both parents, without treating leave as an explanation of the remaining gaps. Board-level rules would touch listed companies and would not, on the Norwegian evidence, be a trades policy.

| NSF ADVANCE Institutional Transformation grants | Null on women's faculty shares in the opened national quasi-experiment | That the programme had no possible climate effect, or that earlier campus accounts prove a national gain |

## Gaps

- A dedicated official global series on women in energy and mining occupations was not opened.
- Detailed US CPS shares for physicists, mathematicians, petroleum engineers and mining occupations were not re-opened (the table was blocked on this pass) and are not invented.
- Official ILOSTAT extracts for China, the Republic of Korea, New Zealand, Kenya, Tunisia, Morocco and Saudi Arabia returned no data in the 2022–2025 query used here.
- Later re-analyses of Goldin and Rouse's orchestra study were not opened.
- The full text of the 2020 BMJ analysis of the NIHR Athena SWAN funding incentive was not available in the pages opened; effect sizes from that paper are UNVERIFIED.
- No peer-reviewed causal evaluation of pre-apprenticeship or trades-bridge programmes was opened.
- No opened study in this review estimates the effect of pay transparency, or of parental leave for both parents, on women's share of engineering, the trades, surgery or executive posts.
- A systematic review of diversity-training and anonymous-curriculum-vitae experiments was not completed.
- Claims that mixed-sex teams automatically produce more innovation were not assessed and are not made here.
- Official armed-forces representation figures were not opened.
