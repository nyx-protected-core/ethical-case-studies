# Trade deals that bring more people into good work

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. It asks how trade agreements and the economic policies around them can raise labour-force participation and job quality, for the betterment of people on both sides of a deal. Figures come from the sources listed at the end and were checked in November 2026. Proposals marked "original" are this study's own suggestions, not positions of any cited author or institution. This is not legal or investment advice.*

## The ethical question

Trade has lifted hundreds of millions of people out of poverty, and it supports a large share of the world's jobs. It has also hollowed out whole towns, pushed some people out of the labour force for good, and rewarded factories that cut corners on safety and pay.

The ethical question is: **how should trade deals and the policies around them be designed so that more people, in all the countries involved, can find and keep decent work, and so that those who lose out are not left behind?**

Three narrower questions follow:

1. Is a trade deal ethical if it raises national income but leaves particular regions and workers worse off for decades?
2. Should trade agreements carry binding labour standards, or are those a disguised form of protectionism against poorer countries?
3. Can trade be used to draw rival or isolated economies into a more cooperative relationship with the rest of the world, and on what terms?

## Scale of the problem

**Work is stable on the surface, weak underneath.** The ILO's *Employment and Social Trends 2026* projects global unemployment steady at 4.9 percent in 2026, but estimates a much larger "jobs gap" of 408 million people who want paid work and cannot get it. Nearly 300 million workers live in extreme working poverty, and 2.1 billion are in informal employment, often without basic rights or social protection (ILO, 2026).

**Large groups are kept out.** Women make up only two-fifths of global employment and, in the ILO's words, are 24.2 percent less likely than men to take part in the labour force. Global youth unemployment is 12.4 percent, and about 260 million young people (20 percent) are not in employment, education or training (ILO, 2026). The gap is especially wide in parts of the Arab States region: in the Gulf Cooperation Council economies, women's participation in 2025 was 39.5 percent against 86.7 percent for men, and in non-GCC Arab economies 10.8 percent against 66.1 percent (UN News, 2026, reporting ILO data). In China, urban youth unemployment reached 17.8 percent in mid-2025 (UN News, 2026, reporting ILO data).

**Trade matters for jobs.** Around 465 million jobs worldwide are linked to foreign demand, and in low- and middle-income countries these jobs tend to offer better conditions and higher productivity than average. The ILO warns that slowing trade growth and policy uncertainty are weakening trade as an engine of job creation (ILO, 2026).

**Integration pays, on average.** The World Bank's *World Development Report 2020* estimates that a 1 percent increase in a country's participation in global value chains is associated with more than a 1 percent rise in income per person in the long run, much more than conventional trade (World Bank, 2019).

## Root causes

### The gains are broad, the losses are concentrated

Classical trade theory says that opening trade raises total income but creates winners and losers. The newer evidence shows the losses are larger, longer-lasting and more local than economists once assumed.

- **United States.** Rising imports from China between 1990 and 2007 caused higher unemployment, lower labour-force participation and lower wages in local labour markets that housed competing manufacturers, and explained about a quarter of the decline in US manufacturing jobs over the period. Spending on unemployment, disability and other transfers rose sharply in those areas (Autor et al., 2013). A decade after the shock, wages and participation in affected areas were still depressed (Autor et al., 2016). A separate study links the sharp fall in US manufacturing employment after 2001 to the removal of the threat of tariff increases on Chinese imports (Pierce & Schott, 2016).
- **India.** After the 1991 trade liberalisation, rural districts whose industries were most exposed saw slower falls in poverty, especially among the least mobile and poorest households, and especially in states where rigid labour laws made it harder for workers to move between sectors (Topalova, 2010).
- **Brazil.** Regions that faced larger tariff cuts suffered growing declines in formal employment and earnings for more than 20 years; the earnings effect after 20 years was about three times the effect after 10 (Dix-Carneiro & Kovak, 2017).

The common thread is **immobility**: people cannot easily move to where new jobs are, and new investment does not flow quickly to the places that lost the old jobs.

### Who gains depends on design

Trade can open doors for groups previously shut out of paid work, but not automatically.

- In **Bangladesh**, the growth of the garment industry drew young women into paid work and raised girls' school enrolment, because the new jobs rewarded literacy and numeracy. The authors find this effect on girls' education larger than that of a large government programme designed to encourage it (Heath & Mobarak, 2015).
- In **Indonesia**, regions more exposed to cuts in tariffs on imported inputs saw higher female work participation and less time in unpaid domestic work, because cheaper inputs expanded sectors that employ many women (Kis-Katos et al., 2018).
- In **Brazil**, by contrast, trade liberalisation reduced both men's and women's participation, especially among the low-skilled. The gender gap narrowed only because men lost more, not because women gained (Gaddis & Pieters, 2017).
- Across developing countries, firms in global value chains employ a higher share of women (33 percent) than other firms (24 percent), and women in high-export sectors are more likely to have formal jobs (World Bank & WTO, 2020).

### Short-term jobs can crowd out long-term skills

In Mexico, the expansion of low-skill export manufacturing raised school dropout: for every 25 jobs created, one student left school after grade 9 rather than continuing to grade 12 (Atkin, 2016). Trade can raise participation today at the cost of skills tomorrow unless education policy keeps pace.

### Hidden bias in trade rules

No country sets tariffs by gender, but tariff structures can still discriminate. The World Bank and WTO found that products mainly consumed by women face a higher tariff burden; tariffs on women's apparel were about US$2.77 billion higher than on men's clothing (World Bank & WTO, 2020).

## What has worked

**Social insurance that matches openness.** Dani Rodrik found that more open economies tend to have larger governments, consistent with public spending acting as insurance against external shocks (Rodrik, 1998). The lesson is that openness has been politically sustainable where it was paired with protection for those exposed to its risks.

**Retraining that is long enough to matter.** A meta-analysis of over 200 evaluations of active labour market programmes found that effects are close to zero in the short run but become more positive two to three years after a programme ends. Training and other human-capital programmes have the largest medium-term effects, and effects are larger for women and for people who were long-term unemployed (Card et al., 2018). Programmes judged after a few months will look like failures even when they work.

**Pressure on working conditions without large job losses.** Anti-sweatshop campaigns in Indonesia raised real wages by about 10 to 20 percent in targeted foreign-owned and exporting textile, footwear and apparel plants, with no significant overall loss of employment, though smaller plants faced a higher risk of closure (Harrison & Scorse, 2010).

**Factory-level monitoring and advice.** An independent assessment of Better Work, the ILO and IFC programme in garment factories, found improvements in working conditions and that better conditions went with higher productivity; for example, reduced verbal abuse and supervisor training were linked to higher output (Better Work, 2016).

**Labour provisions in trade agreements are now normal.** A dataset of 483 preferential trade agreements signed between 1990 and 2016 covers 140 different labour provisions, showing how common they have become; their depth is shaped by trade-union strength and countries' skill profiles (Raess et al., 2018).

## Ethical tensions

**Aggregate gain versus concentrated loss.** A deal that raises average income but leaves particular towns depressed for decades fails people who did nothing wrong. The evidence from the US, India and Brazil (Autor et al., 2013; Topalova, 2010; Dix-Carneiro & Kovak, 2017) makes it hard to defend the old assumption that displaced workers simply move on.

**Labour standards versus protectionism.** Binding standards protect workers from abuse and stop a race to the bottom. But if standards are set at rich-country levels, or enforced selectively, they can shut poorer countries out of the markets that offer their best path to development. The ethical line is between core rights that every worker is owed (freedom from forced and child labour, freedom of association, non-discrimination, safe work) and wage levels that should reflect local productivity.

**Jobs now versus skills later.** Factory jobs can draw young people out of school (Atkin, 2016) or into school (Heath & Mobarak, 2015), depending on what skills they reward. Trade policy and education policy have to be designed together.

**Participation versus quality.** Raising participation by pushing people into informal, unsafe or extremely low-paid work is not betterment. With 2.1 billion people already in informal work (ILO, 2026), the goal must be decent work, not any work.

**Engagement versus pressure.** Trade with authoritarian or rival states can create shared interests, contacts and incentives for cooperation. It can also fund repression and create dependencies that are used as leverage. There is no general answer; the ethical test is whether each specific agreement improves the lives and rights of the workers on both sides.

## Original proposals

These are this study's own suggestions.

1. **A "participation impact statement" for every major trade deal.** Before signing, governments would publish a forecast of effects on employment and participation by region, sex, age and skill level, and commit to measuring the actual results at three, five and ten years. The long, local effects documented by Autor et al. (2013) and Dix-Carneiro and Kovak (2017) show why national averages are not enough.

2. **Adjustment funding written into the deal.** Earmark a fixed share of the tariff revenue or projected gains from a deal for affected regions: wage insurance for displaced workers, two- to three-year training (the horizon over which Card et al., 2018, find gains), relocation support, and incentives for new investment in hit regions. This is the modern form of Rodrik's (1998) insurance bargain.

3. **Core labour rights with support, not just sanctions.** Make the core rights binding in every agreement, but pair them with funded factory-level programmes like Better Work (Better Work, 2016) and a phased timeline for lower-income partners, so that standards lift conditions instead of closing markets.

4. **Remove "pink tariffs" and gender barriers at the border.** Review tariff schedules for products mainly made or bought by women, simplify customs for small traders, and tackle harassment at border crossings, as recommended by the World Bank and WTO (2020). Pair these with input-tariff cuts in sectors that employ many women, following the Indonesian evidence (Kis-Katos et al., 2018).

5. **Tie export growth to schooling.** Where trade deals are expected to create many low-skill jobs, fund incentives that keep young people in school (for example, combined work-study schemes, conditional support for school completion, and employer-funded training), to avoid the dropout trade-off Atkin (2016) documents.

6. **Trade as a bridge to cooperation, with conditions that serve workers.** Sector-specific agreements in areas of mutual benefit (renewable energy components, food, medicines, logistics) can give rival economies a stake in stable relations. Such agreements should include the same core labour rights, transparency on forced labour in supply chains, and adjustment funding, so that engagement benefits ordinary workers rather than only state-linked firms. Areas with large participation gaps, such as women's work in parts of the Gulf and youth employment in China (UN News, 2026), are natural places for cooperation on skills and labour-market programmes.

## Risks

- **Captured adjustment funds.** Money for displaced workers can be diverted to well-connected firms. Funds need independent audit and public reporting.
- **Standards used as protectionism.** Labour clauses can be enforced selectively against rivals. Independent, multilateral monitoring (through the ILO) reduces this risk.
- **Forecasts that are wrong.** Participation impact statements could be manipulated or simply mistaken. Publishing methods and measuring outcomes afterwards keeps them honest.
- **Engagement that entrenches abuse.** Trade with states that use forced labour or repress unions can make firms complicit. Supply-chain transparency and the right to suspend preferences for proven forced labour must stay available.
- **Retreat from trade.** With trade policy uncertainty already weighing on jobs (ILO, 2026), poorly designed protections could slide into protectionism that costs the poorest workers most.

## Recommendation

Trade remains one of the most powerful tools for bringing people into work, especially women and young people in developing economies, but its gains are not automatic and its losses are deep and local. Every major trade agreement should be judged by its effect on decent work across regions and groups, not just on national income. That means binding core labour rights backed by real support, adjustment funding written into the deal and lasting long enough to work, tariff reviews that remove hidden bias against women, and education policy that keeps pace with new jobs. Used this way, trade can also give rival economies a shared stake in stable, cooperative relations, as long as the benefits reach workers on both sides.
