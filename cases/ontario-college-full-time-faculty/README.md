# Ethical case study

## Full-time faculty in Ontario's public colleges: the case for a staffing floor, conversion paths and the funding to pay for them

Status date: 18 November 2026. Each factual claim carries a source tag `[S#]` that points to SOURCES.md, where every source has a public link and a note on how it was checked. Items marked **(unverified)** could not be confirmed from a text opened for this study. Items marked **(our arithmetic)** are simple calculations from cited figures. When a working paper and a published article differ, the version used is named. Union and employer documents are advocacy sources, and the text says so where they are used. This case is one of three on Ontario's public colleges. The companion cases cover academic senates and digital textbook fees.

## 1. The ethical question

Most of the people who teach in Ontario's public colleges are not full-time faculty. They are hired term by term as partial-load, part-time or sessional instructors. Some of them choose that arrangement. Many do not. The question is whether this mix is fair: to the instructors, to the students they teach, and to the public that funds the colleges. If it is not, what should replace it, and who should pay?

This case argues that colleges should move to **more full-time faculty, measured by the share of teaching they do, with a published floor, clear conversion paths for long-serving contract instructors, and dedicated funding**. It also sets out the strongest arguments against that position. Those arguments are not trivial. The research on student outcomes is mixed, colleges are in a financial crisis, and some part-time instructors bring industry expertise that a full-time job would not keep.

## 2. Who teaches in Ontario colleges

### 2.1 The employment categories

The collective agreement for college academic staff defines the categories precisely [S1]:

- **Full-time** professors, instructors, counsellors and librarians are paid on a salary scale. Their workload is set through a Standard Workload Formula, with a cap of 44 hours in any week for up to 36 weeks that include teaching contact hours [S1].
- **Partial-load** teachers teach "more than six and up to and including 12 hours per week on a regular basis" [S1]. They "shall not receive salary or vacations", and they are paid an hourly rate for each teaching contact hour [S1]. From 1 October 2024, a partial-load professor at step 5 earned $100.57 per teaching contact hour, rising to $105.14 from 1 October 2026 [S1].
- **Part-time** teachers teach six hours a week or less. **Sessional** appointments last no more than 12 months in any 24-month period [S1]. Until 2025, both groups were outside the full-time and partial-load bargaining unit.

For comparison, a full-time professor at step 5 earned an annual base salary of $75,271 from 1 October 2024, and the scale tops out at step 21 at $130,490 [S1].

### 2.2 The numbers

No public source gives a clean, current, system-wide count of who teaches what share of college courses. The best available figures come from three sources that measure different things.

**The Workload Taskforce (2024).** This joint taskforce was convened under a 2022 arbitration award and chaired by a neutral chair. It reported that in 2022 the full-time and partial-load bargaining unit had "over 7,500 full-time and over 6,700 partial load members" [S2]. Partial-load numbers in the October staffing survey rose from 4,251 in 2011 to 6,720 in 2022 [S2]. In the same week of each October, total teaching contact hours assigned to partial-load faculty rose from 44,221 to 70,057, an increase of about 58% **(our arithmetic)** [S2]. The average partial-load assignment stayed at about 10.2 to 10.4 hours a week, and 48% of partial-load teachers in 2022 had a full 12-hour assignment [S2]. The taskforce also reported that average weekly teaching contact hours for full-time faculty fell from 12.64 in Fall 2011 to 12.01 in Fall 2022, while hours attributed to complementary functions rose from 12.2 to 13.58 [S2]. It noted that the main workload data system holds no information about partial-load faculty [S2].

**The union's 2021 system report.** Based on provincial staffing data, the faculty union reported head counts of 7,667 full-time academic staff and 15,423 part-time academic staff in 2015–16, and 7,806 and 16,548 in 2018–19 [S3]. Over that period, full-time faculty grew by 1.8%, part-time faculty by 7.2% and full-time administrators by 8.8% [S3]. The report says "over 70% of the faculty teaching students are on short-term contracts" and that the union represents "close to 17,000 contract faculty in a year as members move in and out of unionized PL status", compared with about 7,800 full-time faculty [S3]. These are head counts across a year, so one person teaching in two terms is counted once but a person teaching one course is counted the same as a person teaching four. **(our reading)** Head counts overstate contract instructors' share of *teaching* compared with a measure based on hours.

**The 2025 certification.** Part-time and sessional faculty voted on unionization in 2017. After years of litigation, the ballots were counted in April 2025. The employer council said the Ontario Labour Relations Board certified the union on 14 April 2025 for a unit of about 12,750 employees, with about 3,600 votes cast and more than 50% in favour [S4]. The union said more than 16,000 workers were covered and 88% voted in favour [S5][S6]. **(unverified)** The difference between 12,750 and 16,000 probably reflects different counting dates or definitions. We could not resolve it.

**An estimate of teaching share (our arithmetic).** In an October week in 2022, partial-load faculty taught about 70,000 contact hours [S2]. Over 7,500 full-time bargaining-unit members averaging about 12 contact hours would teach at most about 90,000 hours. That number is too high, because full-time counsellors and librarians do little classroom teaching [S2]. On these figures, partial-load faculty alone taught **roughly 40–45%** of weekly contact hours taught by the two groups together. Part-time and sessional teaching would push the non-full-time share higher still. This is an estimate with wide error bars. It is not a statistic any source publishes.

**One college.** A Durham College student newspaper reported in March 2025 that about a quarter of that college's faculty were full-time [S7]. It is a single head count reported by student journalists. We use it only to show that individual colleges may be far from any system average.

### 2.3 How the mix changed with international enrolment

The July 2025 arbitration award sets out the financial background [S8]. In the four years before the pandemic, international tuition revenue rose 152%, from $696 million to over $1.7 billion. "As student enrolments grew, so too did the numbers of partial load faculty – full-time has remained relatively stable" [S8]. Between 2012–13 and 2020–21, domestic enrolment fell 15% and international enrolment grew 342% [S8]. In 2022–23, an estimated 76.6% of college tuition revenue came from international students [S8].

When the federal government cut study permits, the colleges reversed course. By spring 2025, more than 600 programs had been cancelled or suspended, or their cancellation or suspension announced. Four colleges had closed campuses or announced closures. Nineteen colleges reported current and planned staff reductions of more than 8,000 employees [S8]. The award states that partial-load faculty, whose numbers grew with international enrolment, were "the hardest hit" [S8]. The colleges' own association later reported $1.4 billion in cost cuts, more than 600 program suspensions and more than 8,000 positions eliminated [S9]. The union put total job losses close to 10,000 [S10].

**(our reading)** This history is the core of the ethical problem. Colleges used a contract workforce as a shock absorber for volatile revenue. When revenue grew, teaching grew through contracts. When revenue fell, those contracts disappeared first. That arrangement may be prudent for an institution, but it moves the risk of a public policy decision onto the lowest-paid and least protected instructors.

## 3. The rules that already exist

The collective agreement already states a preference for full-time work, with broad exceptions:

- Art. 2.02: "The College will give preference to the designation of full-time positions as regular rather than partial-load teaching positions", subject to "operational requirements as the quality of the programs, their economic viability, attainment of the program objectives, the need for special qualifications and the market acceptability of the programs" [S1].
- Art. 2.03 A: a similar preference for regular rather than sessional positions, and the college "will not abuse sessional appointments by failing to fill ongoing positions as soon as possible" [S1].
- Art. 2.03 C: if a college keeps a full-time position staffed with sessional appointments beyond one full academic year, it must designate the position as regular full-time and fill it [S1].
- Art. 2.03 D: grievances under 2.02 and 2.03 A "cannot rely on staffing which occurred from September 1, 2014 to December 20, 2017" [S1].
- Art. 26.10 E: a partial-load teacher with at least eight months of service in the last four academic years has hiring priority for a course they have taught before, if the college decides to staff it as partial-load [S1].

**(our reading)** These clauses protect a *preference*, not an *outcome*. "Economic viability" lets a college justify almost any partial-load assignment, because partial-load teaching costs less per contact hour. The conversion rule in 2.03 C applies to sessional appointments that fill a full-time position. Nothing converts a run of partial-load assignments into a full-time job, however long it lasts. The priority in 26.10 E keeps a partial-load teacher in a course. It does not lead to a full-time position.

In the 2025 round, the union proposed staffing measures. The employer said this was not the time to entertain "staffing proposals – incredibly – to hire new employees" [S8]. The award focused on severance for full-time employees, automatic enrolment of partial-load faculty on the priority registry and benefits improvements, and it dismissed proposals it did not specifically address [S8].

## 4. What the research says about students

The research comes mostly from the United States and mostly compares tenure-line with non-tenure-track faculty. That distinction does not map neatly onto Ontario's full-time versus partial-load split, because Ontario colleges have no tenure. The findings are mixed, and the mix is itself informative.

### 4.1 Evidence that heavy reliance on contract instructors harms students

- **Graduation.** Ehrenberg and Zhang (2005) used US institution-level panel data and found that greater use of part-time and full-time non-tenure-track faculty "does adversely affect graduation rates at four-year colleges", with the largest effect at public master's-level institutions [S11].
- **Community college completion.** Jaeger and Eagan (2008) found that California community college students experienced "a significant yet modest negative effect" on completing an associate's degree from exposure to part-time faculty [S12].
- **First-year retention.** In a state university system, "high levels of exposure to part-time faculty in the first year of college are consistently found to negatively affect student retention to the second year" (Jaeger and Eagan, 2011) [S13]. Eagan and Jaeger (2008) studied part-time instruction in large first-year "gatekeeper" courses and urged administrators to give "careful consideration" before cutting costs this way [S14].
- **Later courses.** Xu (2019) used community college transcript data and found that part-time adjuncts "are associated with higher grades in contemporaneous courses but have negative impacts on subsequent course performance" [S15]. Ran and Xu (2019) found that non-tenure-track faculty have positive effects on current course grades but negative effects on subsequent courses. The negative effects were "stronger for non-tenure-track faculty hired through temporary appointments than for those hired with long-term contracts" [S16]. This is important for Ontario. The harm appears to come mainly from temporary, insecure appointments rather than from being non-tenured as such.
- **Teaching practice.** Baldwin and Wawrzynski (2011) used US national faculty survey data and found that "the teaching practices of part-time contingent faculty differ in important ways" from other faculty, while those of full-time contingent faculty "more closely parallel" tenure-line faculty [S17].

### 4.2 Evidence that points the other way

- **Northwestern University.** Figlio, Schapiro and Soter (2015) found "consistent evidence that students learn relatively more from contingent faculty in their first-term courses". This result is driven by "the bottom quarter" of tenure-line faculty having lower value added [S18]. Northwestern's full-time teaching-track lecturers are a very different group from Ontario's per-course instructors.
- **Occupational fields.** Bettinger and Long (2010) found that adjuncts "often have a small, positive effect on enrollment patterns, especially in fields related to particular occupations" [S19]. Many Ontario college programs are exactly that kind of field.
- **Canadian evidence on instructor characteristics.** Hoffmann and Oreopoulos (2009, published version) used administrative data on first-year students at "a large Canadian university" from 1996 to 2005. Their comparisons included part-time adjuncts, full-time teaching lecturers and tenure-line professors. They found that "objective characteristics such as rank and salary" do not predict instructors' influence on students, and that "the importance of college instructor differences is small, but important outliers exist" [S20].
- **A caution on grades.** Carrell and West (2010) found that professors "who excel at promoting contemporaneous student achievement" can "harm the follow-on achievement of their students in more advanced classes" [S21]. So studies that use only same-course grades can mislead in either direction.

### 4.3 What the research supports

**(our reading)** The research does not support a claim that full-time instructors are always better teachers. It supports three narrower claims:

1. Heavy *exposure* to part-time, temporary instructors, especially in first-year and gateway courses, is associated with lower retention, completion and later-course performance in several US settings [S12][S13][S15][S16].
2. The harm is concentrated in *temporary, insecure* appointments rather than in non-tenure status as such. Long-term contract faculty look much more like tenure-line faculty [S16][S17].
3. Part-time instructors with current professional practice can add value in occupational fields [S19].

None of these studies uses Ontario college data. We found no published study that links Ontario college students' outcomes to the employment status of their instructors. The provincial data needed for such a study (who taught which section) are not published (see section 7.4).

## 5. The fairness argument for instructors

Student outcomes are not the only test. There is a separate question of fair treatment.

**Equal pay for comparable work.** A partial-load professor is paid an hourly rate for each teaching contact hour and gets no salary or vacation [S1]. The union's 2021 report states that contract faculty "are paid solely for their hours spent teaching during the semester, and not for any of the required associated preparation and evaluation, for work beyond the end of the semester, or for additional student support" [S3]. The employer would answer that the hourly rate is set to reflect associated work. **(our arithmetic)** If a full-time professor at step 5 ($75,271) taught 12 contact hours a week for 30 to 36 weeks, their salary would work out to about $174 to $209 per contact hour. The partial-load step-5 rate is $100.57 [S1]. The comparison is rough. Full-time salaries also pay for curriculum development, student advising, committee work and other complementary functions [S2], and benefits differ. Still, the gap suggests that per-course work costs colleges much less per hour of teaching, which is why it is used.

**Choice versus constraint.** The union report cites a 2018 national survey of more than 2,600 contract academic staff. In it, 53% said they were seeking a full-time job, 25% said they did not want one, and the rest were undecided [S3]. **(unverified)** We read this survey result only as the union report quotes it, not in the original survey. If roughly half want full-time work, the system is not mainly serving instructors' preferences.

**Hidden academics.** Brownlee (2015) studied Canadian *universities* and used access-to-information requests because institutions did not publish data on contract faculty [S22]. Ontario colleges show a similar gap in data. The main workload data system does not track partial-load faculty [S2]. A workforce that is not measured is easy to expand without public debate.

## 6. Fair counterarguments

**"Colleges cannot afford it now."** This is the strongest objection. Colleges have cut programs, closed campuses and laid off thousands of staff [S8][S9]. In 2021–22, Ontario's per-student funding for colleges was $6,891, 44% of the level in the rest of Canada [S23]. The colleges' association says operating grants are $7,700 per student below the national average and projects a structural deficit of more than $5,200 per student [S9]. A full-time floor without new money would mean fewer course sections, larger classes or more program closures. **Response:** this argues for phasing and funding the floor, not for abandoning it. The past decade shows what happens without a floor. When money arrived, teaching grew through contracts. When it left, contract instructors were cut first [S8].

**"Flexibility serves students."** Enrolment in a program can swing sharply from one year to the next. Contract instructors let colleges open and close sections quickly. **Response:** a floor set as a share of teaching hours, with room for a flexible margin, keeps that capacity. It only stops flexibility from becoming the default.

**"Industry experts don't want full-time jobs."** In applied programs, a practising nurse, engineer or chef who teaches one course may be exactly what students need, and the agreement itself recognizes "the need for special qualifications" [S1]. Research also finds small positive effects of adjuncts in occupational fields [S19]. **Response:** that is a reason to protect a clearly defined professional-practice category, not to rely on per-course hiring for core teaching. The 25% of contract staff who say they do not want full-time work [S3] should be able to keep their arrangement.

**"Full-time faculty are not always better."** Figlio, Schapiro and Soter [S18] and Hoffmann and Oreopoulos [S20] suggest that employment status or rank is a poor predictor of teaching quality. **Response:** agreed. The case for full-time positions rests on continuity, institutional participation (curriculum work, advising, governance) and fair employment, not on a claim that each full-time instructor is a better teacher. Ran and Xu's finding that stable contracts make the difference [S16] supports secure employment, which a full-time floor provides.

**"This belongs in bargaining, not policy."** Staffing has been bargained for decades. **Response:** staffing ratios also affect the quality of a publicly funded service. The Postsecondary Education Quality Assessment Board already requires that at least half of a student's experience in a college degree program be in courses taught by faculty holding the terminal credential, and that teaching loads be specified [S24]. Quality standards about who teaches are therefore already public policy for degrees. Extending a version of that principle to diplomas is a policy decision, not only a bargaining one.

## 7. Original proposals

These proposals come from this study. They are not drawn from any cited source.

### 7.1 A staffing floor measured by teaching, not head count

- **Metric.** The share of teaching contact hours (or course sections) in credit programs taught by full-time faculty, published each year by college and by program area. Head counts mislead, because a person teaching one course counts the same as a person teaching four (section 2.2).
- **Floor.** A system-wide target of **60% of credit teaching contact hours by full-time faculty**, with a minimum of **50% at every college**, to be reached over six academic years. This is a judgment, not a figure taken from any source. It is set above our rough 2022 estimate for the full-time share (section 2.2), so it requires real change, but it leaves a large margin for flexibility and industry expertise.
- **Gateway courses.** A higher target (for example 70%) for first-year and gateway courses, where the research on retention is most consistent [S13][S14][S15].
- **Degree programs.** Keep and enforce PEQAB's existing credential rules [S24].
- **Exemptions.** A defined **professional-practice instructor** category for people with a primary current occupation outside the college, teaching no more than a set number of hours. Their hours would count toward a separate margin of no more than 20%, not against the floor.

### 7.2 Conversion paths

- **Partial-load conversion.** Extend the logic of art. 2.03 C to partial-load work. Where substantially the same teaching load (for example, at least 9 contact hours a week in the same program area) has been staffed through partial-load contracts for four of six consecutive terms, the college must post a full-time position. It must give the incumbent and other long-serving registered partial-load teachers first consideration on a fair, published standard.
- **Recognition of service.** Count partial-load service toward step placement and probation on conversion, building on the service rules already in art. 26.10 [S1].
- **Pathway positions.** Create a multi-year full-time contract category for new programs. It would convert to regular full-time once the program has run through one full cohort. This answers the employer's concern about committing permanent staff to unproven programs.
- **Layoff fairness.** When cuts are unavoidable, publish the effect on the full-time teaching share by college. The floor should not be met by cutting contract instructors while leaving their courses to be taught by overloaded full-time faculty.

### 7.3 Funding

- **A dedicated, multi-year conversion fund** paid through the province's multi-year agreements with colleges. Colleges would receive it for each verified conversion and recover it if the floor is not met.
- **Base funding first.** The financial evidence points to structural underfunding of domestic teaching [S9][S23]. Without new base funding, the floor would mostly show up as course closures. Government should pair the floor with base funding increases. The colleges' own costing evidence shows that high-cost programs in fields such as electromechanical engineering already carry per-student funding gaps of $4,000 to $6,300 [S9].
- **No new reliance on volatile revenue.** Colleges should not count international tuition toward the floor's funding, because the last cycle showed how quickly it can vanish [S8].

### 7.4 Data

- Publish, by college and program area, each year: head counts and contact hours by employment category (full-time, partial-load, part-time, sessional, professional practice).
- Extend the joint bargaining data committee's work to include partial-load and part-time faculty, as the Workload Taskforce recommended for partial-load workload data [S2].
- Make anonymized section-level data available to independent researchers, so that Ontario-specific evidence on student outcomes can be produced.

## 8. Risks of the proposals

- **Fewer sections.** If funding does not follow, colleges may cut courses or raise class sizes to meet the ratio.
- **Gaming.** Colleges could reclassify instructors as "professional practice" to escape the floor. The category needs a tight definition and an audit.
- **Overload.** Colleges could meet the floor by loading full-time faculty with more teaching hours, eroding the very continuity and advising time the floor is meant to protect. The existing workload limits must hold [S1].
- **Insider advantage.** Conversion preference for incumbents may block newer and more diverse candidates. Using published criteria and open competition, with service credited but not decisive, limits this.
- **Rigidity.** A college in a declining region may not be able to meet 50%. The rules should allow a time-limited, published exemption tied to a recovery plan.

## 9. Recommendation

The evidence supports **more full-time faculty in Ontario colleges, measured by share of teaching, with a phased floor, real conversion paths and dedicated funding**. The case does not depend on a claim that full-time instructors are always better teachers; the research does not show that. It rests on three findings. Heavy reliance on temporary instructors in early courses is associated with worse student persistence and later performance in many studies. Secure appointments appear to remove much of that harm. And the current system has made contract instructors bear the risk of the province's funding choices and the colleges' enrolment strategy. A floor of 60% system-wide and 50% at each college, a protected professional-practice margin, conversion after sustained partial-load service, and conversion funding tied to verified results would correct that while preserving flexibility and industry expertise.

## 10. Unverified or uncertain items

- **Teaching share.** No source publishes the share of teaching contact hours by employment category. Our estimate of roughly 40–45% for partial-load faculty alone (section 2.2) is our arithmetic from Workload Taskforce figures and could be off by a wide margin [S2].
- **Certification numbers.** The employer cited about 12,750 employees and the union more than 16,000. We could not reconcile them [S4][S5][S6].
- **2018 survey of contract academic staff.** We read the result only as the union report quotes it [S3].
- **Durham figure.** This is a single head count in a student newspaper, not an official statistic [S7].
- **Union head counts.** The 2021 union report figures are head counts drawn from provincial data by an advocacy organization. We did not obtain the underlying provincial tables [S3].
- **US evidence.** Every outcome study cited is from US institutions, except one Canadian university study [S20]. Transfer to Ontario colleges is assumed, not shown.
- **2017 award.** The 2017 arbitration award PDF could not be downloaded directly. Its staffing language was checked in the current collective agreement [S1].
- **Partial-load pay coverage.** Whether the partial-load hourly rate fairly covers preparation and evaluation is disputed between the parties. We report the union's claim [S3] and the structure of the rate [S1] without resolving it.

## 11. What this does not replace

This is an ethics case study based on public documents. It is not a costing, and the proposed ratios are judgments to be tested, not results of a model. It is not legal advice on the collective agreement or a substitute for bargaining. It is not a review of staffing at any particular college. Anyone acting on these proposals should obtain college-level data and a proper costing, and consult the faculty, students, colleges and Ministry affected.
