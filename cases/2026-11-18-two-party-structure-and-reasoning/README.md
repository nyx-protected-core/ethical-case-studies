# Two big parties, one narrow debate: party structure, polarisation and reasoning in Canada and the United States

Status date: 18 November 2026. Numbers in square brackets point to the source list. Every source was checked on the status date on the institution's own page, official results file, or public article page. **(our arithmetic)** marks calculations from official totals. **(our proposal)** marks this study's suggestions. **UNVERIFIED** marks unconfirmed claims. The case takes no party side. Examples of poor reasoning come from both main camps. Public figures appear only from official or published records.

## Summary

Politics in the United States really does collapse, at the national level, into a contest between two parties. In the official 2024 presidential count, Republican Donald J. Trump won 77,302,580 votes (49.80 percent) and Democrat Kamala D. Harris won 75,017,613 (48.32 percent), or 98.12 percent of the 155,238,302 votes recorded **(our arithmetic)** [1]. The next largest totals were Jill Stein 0.56 percent, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. 0.49 percent and Chase Oliver 0.42 percent [1].

Canada is often described in the same way, as Conservatives versus Liberals. That is only half right. Those two parties dominate the fight to form government, and first-past-the-post voting gives them a larger share of seats than of votes. But Canada is not a two-party system in the American sense. In 2021 the Conservatives led the popular vote while the Liberals won the most seats and formed a minority government; the NDP, Bloc Québécois, People's Party and Greens together took nearly a third of the vote [2][3][4]. In 2025 the two large parties took a much bigger combined share of the vote, but the House was still a minority parliament, with the Bloc, NDP and Greens still winning seats [5][6][7].

The second part of the hypothesis also has a real core, and it needs a precise wording. The best-supported claim is not that people have no real disagreements. It is that hostility between party camps has risen, especially in the United States; that many people now vote more against the other side than for their own; that partisans systematically misread how extreme and how typical the other side is; and that once a question is tagged as a team question, people often judge arguments by whether they help their side [11][12][13][14][15][16][17]. The same patterns appear, in a milder form, in Canada [12][18].

The hypothesis overreaches if it treats two-party structure as the only cause, or all conflict as a failure of logic. Affective polarisation rose more in the United States than in any of the other eleven rich democracies in a four-decade comparison, but several countries with different electoral systems showed smaller rises and it fell in Britain, which also uses plurality voting [12]. For Canada that comparison is inconclusive: its trend is small and not statistically significant, and it turns negative when only the two largest parties are counted [12]. A separate Canadian study does find a rise [18]. Some disagreements are genuine conflicts of value. Tools that improve reasoning exist, but they are demanding, and Canadian electoral-reform votes have usually rejected change or missed a legal threshold [19][20][21][22][23][24][25][26][27].

## The hypothesis

The view this case tests is that politics in Canada and the United States collapses into two main camps (Conservatives versus Liberals; Republicans versus Democrats), and that current debates are often illogical, driven by identity and insult rather than argument.

## Why plurality voting favours two dominant parties

Duverger's proposition is that "the simple-majority single-ballot system" — plurality rule in single-member districts — "favours the two-party system" [8][9]. Two mechanisms are at work. The mechanical effect is that a party whose support is spread thinly wins fewer seats than its vote share would suggest under a proportional rule. The psychological, or strategic, effect is that voters desert candidates who look hopeless, and that serious politicians avoid launching campaigns that voters will treat as hopeless [9][10].

Cox's *Making Votes Count* restates this as a more careful ceiling, the "M + 1" rule: in a district that awards M seats, no more than M + 1 candidates or lists are viable if voters are trying to make their votes count and have a shared sense of who is ahead. When M is 1, the ceiling is two. The rule does not guarantee exactly two parties. Uncertainty, regional loyalties, or voters who care about more than winning the current seat can weaken the squeeze [10].

A two-party pattern is therefore not a mysterious cultural failing. It is the predicted result of the voting rule both countries use for their main national legislatures, and that the United States also uses, with an Electoral College overlay, for the presidency.

## Canada is not a two-party system — and what that shows

If Duverger were a strict national law, Canada would look like the United States. It does not.

First, two parties still dominate government formation. In 2021 the Liberals and Conservatives together won 66.4 percent of the valid vote and 82.5 percent of the seats **(our arithmetic from [2][3][4])**. In 2025 they won 85.1 percent of the vote and 91.3 percent of the seats **(our arithmetic from [5][6][7])**.

Second, the voting system still manufactures a two-camp picture that is sharper than the vote. In 2021 the Liberals formed government while finishing second in the popular vote. The NDP's 17.8 percent of the vote produced only 7.4 percent of the seats. The People's Party's 4.9 percent produced none [2][3][4]. That is the mechanical effect in plain numbers.

Third, parties with a regional base can break the national two-party pattern. The Bloc Québécois won 32 seats in 2021 with 7.6 percent of the national vote (32.1 percent in Quebec) and 22 seats in 2025 with 6.3 percent nationally (27.7 percent in Quebec) [3][4][6][7]. Cox's district-level rule explains this: a party can be one of the top two options in many districts in one region, almost absent elsewhere, and still win seats. The NDP has historically done something similar in parts of the West and in urban ridings, though in 2025 its vote share fell to 6.3 percent and its seats to seven [5][6][7].

The "two big camps" claim therefore fits how governments are formed, and how first-past-the-post maps votes into seats. It does not fit the ballot or the House. Canada is better described as a two-bloc system with a multi-party vote, which also means its problems of insult-driven debate cannot be blamed on the mere existence of only two parties.

## Canadian attempts to change the voting system

If the voting rule is part of the problem, Canada has not been short of attempts to change it. The record is one of carefully designed proposals, followed by failure at the ballot box or abandonment in office.

In 2004, British Columbia's Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform recommended replacing first-past-the-post with a single transferable vote system it called BC-STV [28]. At the 17 May 2005 referendum, 57.69 percent of valid votes were "yes," and "yes" won a majority in 77 of 79 districts. The law required at least 60 percent of the valid vote province-wide, and a majority in at least 48 districts, to bind the government. The district threshold was met; the 60 percent threshold was not [21]. A second referendum in 2009 produced only 39.1 percent support [22]. In 2018, official results on the first question were 845,235 votes (61.30 percent) for first-past-the-post and 533,518 (38.70 percent) for a proportional system. Among those who ranked three proportional options, mixed-member proportional finished first [22][23].

Ontario asked voters in 2007 whether to keep first-past-the-post or adopt mixed-member proportional, the system recommended by the Ontario Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform, established in 2006 [24][29]. Official results were 2,714,020 votes (63.18 percent) for first-past-the-post and 1,581,741 (36.82 percent) for mixed-member proportional, with majorities in 102 districts and five districts respectively [24].

Prince Edward Island used a preferential ballot in a 2016 plebiscite on five systems. After the final round, mixed-member proportional had 19,418 votes (52.42 percent) and first-past-the-post 15,869 (42.84 percent), with turnout of 36.5 percent of 102,464 eligible electors [25]. A 2019 referendum then asked a yes-or-no question on mixed-member proportional. Official totals were 42,372 "no" (51.74 percent) and 39,516 "yes" (48.26 percent) of 81,888 votes counted [26].

At the federal level, the Liberal Party's 2015 platform said: "We are committed to ensuring that 2015 will be the last federal election conducted under the first-past-the-post voting system." It promised an all-party committee to review ranked ballots, proportional representation, mandatory voting and online voting, and legislation within 18 months of forming government [27]. On 1 February 2017, the Prime Minister's mandate letter to the new Minister of Democratic Institutions stated that the House committee, outreach by members of Parliament, and the engagement of 360,000 people through mydemocracy.ca had not produced "a clear preference for a new electoral system, let alone a consensus," that a referendum without a clear preference or question "would not be in Canada's interest," and that "Changing the electoral system will not be in your mandate" [30]. The idea that the voting system narrows debate is not one that Canadian institutions have never considered. Citizens' assemblies have sometimes accepted it; voters or governments have then declined to enact it.

## Primaries, safe seats and ranked-choice experiments in the United States

The United States adds two features that Canada, at the federal level, does not share in the same way: party primaries that select nominees, often in low-turnout contests, and legislative districts drawn through a political process that can be used to protect incumbents or parties.

Closed partisan primaries restrict the nomination contest to voters already registered with that party. The U.S. Election Assistance Commission (23 December 2025) says that 20 percent of states, and the District of Columbia, have a closed partisan primary. Another 44 percent have open primaries or primaries open to unaffiliated voters; 26 percent have partially closed or partially open rules; and 10 percent use other modes, including top-two or top-four systems in which all voters see all candidates and a small number advance, even if they are from the same party [31]. The concern, still a hypothesis rather than a settled fact, is that a closed, low-turnout primary can reward candidates who energise a party's most committed supporters, narrowing the general-election choice.

Gerrymandering is often blamed for the same narrowing. The ethical problem is real: when the politicians who will run in a map also draw the map, they can pack or spread opponents, and both major parties have done this where they control redistricting. But the claim that gerrymandering is the main cause of congressional polarisation is weakly supported. Nolan McCarty, Keith Poole and Howard Rosenthal found little evidence of a strong causal link. In their account, polarisation is driven mainly by the fact that Democrats and Republicans now represent the same kinds of districts very differently, not by which districts each party holds. Simulations of more "neutral" maps produced polarisation levels close to the actual ones [32]. Gerrymandering can still distort seat shares. It is a weaker explanation for why the two camps dislike each other than popular commentary suggests.

Two states have tried to loosen the binary at the ballot itself. In 2020, Alaska voters approved Ballot Measure 2, creating a top-four primary and ranked-choice voting in the general election. Official results were 174,032 "yes" (50.55 percent) and 170,251 "no" (49.45 percent) [33]. Maine first used ranked-choice voting in the 12 June 2018 primaries. Under Maine's constitution, as interpreted in a 2017 advisory opinion of the state's Supreme Judicial Court, general elections for governor and the state legislature must still be decided by plurality, so ranked-choice voting is used in state primaries and in general elections for federal offices, including the presidency from 2020 [34]. These are genuine alternatives to a simple plurality general election. This case does not treat them as proven cures for polarisation, and the Alaska margin was narrow enough that the reform itself remains contested.

## Affective polarisation: dislike, not just disagreement

Political scientists use "affective polarisation" for the gap between warm feelings toward one's own party and cold feelings toward the other, as opposed to disagreement on policy. Shanto Iyengar and colleagues' 2019 review is the standard statement of the American evidence: ordinary Democrats and Republicans increasingly dislike and distrust one another; they describe the other party's members as hypocritical, selfish and closed-minded; and they are less willing to socialise across party lines. The review traces this to partisanship as a social identity, not only to clashing issue positions [11].

Levi Boxell, Matthew Gentzkow and Jesse Shapiro compared feeling-thermometer trends in twelve OECD countries over about four decades. In their baseline estimates, the United States had the largest rise. Five other countries — Switzerland, France, Denmark, Canada and New Zealand — had smaller positive trends, and only Denmark's was statistically significant. Canada's baseline trend is 1.5 points per decade, with a 95 percent confidence interval of −0.7 to 3.8 points (test against zero: *p* = 0.126); it is significantly smaller than the United States trend (*p* = 0.005), and when only the two largest parties in each survey are counted it changes sign, to −3.3 points per decade [12]. Six countries — Japan, Australia, Britain, Norway, Sweden and (West) Germany — became less polarised [12]. In the United States, the in-party minus out-party thermometer gap rose from 27.4 points in 1978 to 56.3 points in 2020 in their calculations, a change of 1.08 standard deviations relative to the 1978 spread, with a trend of 5.6 points per decade [12].

Richard Johnston, using Canadian Election Study feeling thermometers from 1988 to 2021, finds that affective polarisation in Canada is real and has increased, inside and outside Quebec, even though some relationships have become warmer (Liberal and NDP identifiers toward each other, for example). American categories cannot be copied blindly: Canada is a multi-party parliamentary system with a lasting split between Quebec and the rest of the country, and a large group of non-partisans who often remain relatively indifferent rather than hostile [18]. In his account, shifts in affect track shifts in the policy gaps among the parties, with suggestive evidence that policy helps drive affect [18].

The comparative pattern is the most important limit on a simple "two parties cause hatred" story. Britain uses plurality voting and, in this comparison, became less affectively polarised. Canada has more than two parties; in Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro's twelve-country estimates its trend is small, not statistically different from zero, and much smaller than the United States', and it reverses sign when only the top two parties are counted [12]. Johnston's Canadian Election Study analysis does find that affective polarisation rose [18]. So Canada shows that a multi-party ballot does not prevent hostility, but the cross-country data are at least as consistent with some protection as with none. The United States is an outlier in the size of the increase. The voting rule alone does not explain the whole pattern [11][12][18].

## Negative partisanship: voting against, more than voting for

Alan Abramowitz and Steven Webster call the same shift "negative partisanship": people may not feel much warmer about their own party, but they feel much colder about the other, and that hostility now organises voting down the ballot. Using American National Election Studies data, they show a large negative shift in feelings toward the opposing party since the 1980s among supporters of both major parties, including leaning independents; rising party loyalty and straight-ticket voting; and a closer match between presidential results and results further down the ballot [13]. Their later paper describes Americans as increasingly aligned against one party rather than affectionately attached to the other [14].

This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the hypothesis. A public that votes mainly to stop the other side will treat politics as a zero-sum identity fight. The papers assign the shift to both Democrats and Republicans, not to one camp only [13][14].

## Motivated reasoning and identity-protective cognition

Charles Taber and Milton Lodge, in two experiments on affirmative action and gun control, found that people rate arguments that fit their prior view as stronger than arguments that do not; that they counter-argue contrary points and accept supporting points too easily; and that, when free to choose what to read, they seek confirming material. These habits polarise attitudes further, especially among people with strong priors and high political knowledge [15]. 

Dan Kahan and colleagues describe a related pattern as identity-protective cognition: people use their reasoning ability to protect beliefs that are bound up with their group. In a widely cited experiment, highly numerate people did better than others at reading a table when it was presented as a study of a skin-rash treatment. When the same numbers were presented as a study of a gun-control ban, answers became politically polarised — and the polarisation was larger, not smaller, among the most numerate [16][17]. More data, or more sophisticated partisans, will not automatically improve debate on questions that have become team badges.

That numeracy experiment should not be treated as untouchable. A preregistered replication published in *Cognition* in 2021 reported that it did not find good evidence for the motivated-numeracy effect [35]. The wider findings on motivated skepticism and partisan misperception do not depend on that one design.

## Misperceptions of the other side

Partisans do not only dislike each other. They also miscount each other.

Douglas Ahler and Gaurav Sood found that Americans greatly overestimate how many party supporters belong to groups that fit the party's stereotype. In their examples, people thought 32 percent of Democrats were LGBT (about 6 percent were) and that 38 percent of Republicans earned more than $250,000 a year (about 2 percent did). The errors were larger for the other party than for one's own, but people were not particularly accurate about their own party either. When they were told the actual figures, they saw the other party's supporters as less extreme and felt less socially distant from them [36].

More in Common's 2019 *Perception Gap* report, based on a YouGov survey of 2,100 U.S. adults after the 2018 midterm elections, is a research report from an organisation whose stated aim is to reduce polarisation, not a peer-reviewed article, and is labelled as such here. It found that Democrats and Republicans imagined almost twice as many people on the other side held extreme views as really did: on average they believed that 55 percent of the other side's views were extreme, against about 30 percent in the study's own coding. People who followed the news "most of the time" were almost three times as inaccurate as those who followed it "only now and then." Posting about politics, and among Democrats postgraduate education, were associated with a wider gap. The wider the gap, the more likely people were to describe opponents as "hateful" or "brainwashed." The size of the gap depends on which issues and wordings are chosen [37].

If you believe the other party is mainly composed of its most stereotypical members, contempt feels like realism. The data say that is often a mistake, and that the mistake is shared across party lines [36][37].

## Common fallacies in televised and online debate

These habits appear in both countries and in both main camps. They are listed here because they are symmetrical enough to be treated as a shared civic problem.

**False dilemma.** A complex choice is flattened into two extremes. American examples include treating any gun rule as a confiscation plan, or any resistance to a new rule as indifference to mass killing; and treating any border enforcement as cruelty, or any asylum claim as a racket. Canadian examples include treating any carbon price as economic destruction, or any doubt about a particular climate policy as climate denial; and treating any criticism of a Liberal government as imported culture war, or any public-health rule as a dictatorship. The voting system encourages this habit by making election day look binary even when the issue is not.

**Composition error, or taking the worst for the typical.** A slogan or a clip of an extreme speaker is treated as the other party's programme: "defund the police" as the Democratic platform; a harsh Republican remark on abortion or immigration as what every Republican voter wants; the most strident convoy rhetoric as the Conservative programme; a Liberal or NDP minister's weakest wording as what all their voters believe. Ahler and Sood, and the Perception Gap report, measure the same error in figures [36][37].

**Ad hominem and identity labels.** The other side is named as a kind of person — racist, fascist, socialist, traitor, "anti-science," "elite," "fringe" — instead of being answered on the claim. This is the everyday form of identity-protective cognition [16][17].

**Whataboutism and motivated shifting of standards.** A criticism is answered only by pointing to a wrong on the other side, or the same tactic is defended when one's own side uses it and condemned when the other side does. Both American parties have used the first move on political violence, corruption and presidential conduct. Both Canadian camps have used it on ethics rules, spending and protest tactics. Taber and Lodge's "disconfirmation bias" is the laboratory version of the second habit [15].

None of this means the underlying disputes are imaginary. It means the public argument often never reaches them.

## What improves reasoning

James Fishkin, Alice Siu, Larry Diamond and Norman Bradburn report on "America in One Room," a deliberative poll held just before the 2020 presidential primary season. A stratified random sample of 526 registered voters, recruited by NORC, spent a long weekend deliberating on five major issues, with briefing materials and moderated discussion. A control group of 844 answered the same questions without the deliberation. Of 47 policy proposals, 26 met the authors' test for extreme partisan polarisation. Among partisans who started at the most extreme ends of those 26 items, there was two-sided depolarisation on all 26. Feeling-thermometer ratings of the other party rose 13 points among Democrats and 14 points among Republicans; the control group's thermometers were essentially unchanged [19]. People can reason together in a carefully designed setting; the authors treat scale as an open problem [19].

Citizens' assemblies are a cousin of the same idea. British Columbia's 2004 assembly and Ontario's assembly, established in 2006, both produced coherent proposals for a new voting system [28][29]. In both provinces, voters later rejected the change, or, in British Columbia's 2005 vote, supported it just short of a supermajority threshold [21][22][24]. Deliberation can improve a proposal without creating a popular majority.

The Strengthening Democracy Challenge, reported by Jan Voelkel and many colleagues in *Science* in 2024, tested 25 short treatments in an online experiment with 32,059 Democrats and Republicans. In preregistered analyses, 23 of the 25 treatments reduced partisan animosity, by up to 10.5 percentage points. Only six reduced support for undemocratic practices (by up to 5.8 points), and only five reduced support for partisan violence (by up to 2.8 points). Animosity fell most when treatments highlighted sympathetic people with different views, or identities that rivals share. Antidemocratic attitudes fell more often when treatments corrected misperceptions of the other side, or stressed the risk of democratic collapse or elite endorsements of democratic norms. Partisan dislike is not the same problem as willingness to break democratic rules [20]. Ahler and Sood's corrections likewise reduced perceived extremity and social distance [36]. People reason better when they have accurate information about the other side, a structured setting, and a task that is not simply to defeat a rival.

## Counterarguments

**Two-party systems have real advantages.** A plurality system that produces two large parties can make accountability clearer. Voters can more easily tell which team was in power and vote it out. Coalition governments can blur that line, and can give a small party bargaining power out of proportion to its vote. Canada's referendum record shows that many voters, when asked, preferred to keep first-past-the-post [21][22][23][24][26]. A case for reform that ignores those benefits is incomplete.

**Proportional systems have costs of their own.** They can fragment the legislature, lengthen government formation, and shift decisive bargains from the public campaign to post-election negotiations. They can also give small parties a platform without making argument more civil. In Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro's comparison, several countries with more proportional or mixed systems still showed positive trends (statistically significant only for Denmark), and Britain, a plurality country, showed a decline [12]. Changing the formula for seats is not a proven solvent for contempt.

**Canada already has more than two parties, and still has the problem.** If the hypothesis were simply "two parties make debate illogical," Canada's multi-party ballot should have been protective. Johnston finds that affective polarisation rose there too, and that policy distance among the parties is part of the story [18]. The cross-country trend for Canada is weaker evidence either way: it is not statistically significant and its sign depends on whether all parties or only the two largest are counted [12]. The 2025 results, in which the two large parties took more than four-fifths of the vote, also show that a multi-party system under plurality rules can move toward a two-party vote without a formal change in the law [6][7].

**Some sharp debate is about real values, not about logic.** Iyengar and colleagues note the long argument over how divided the American public is on issues, as opposed to how much the parties dislike each other [11]. Johnston links Canadian affect to ideological location [18]. More in Common, even while stressing misperception, also argues that differences in "core beliefs" help explain why people diverge [37]. Disagreement over abortion, guns, the welfare state, national identity, or Quebec's place in Confederation can be heated because the stakes are moral. The ethical target is contempt and misrepresentation, not conflict itself.

**Gerrymandering and closed primaries are easy villains.** Both deserve scrutiny. The work tested here does not show that redrawing maps, or opening primaries, would by itself undo affective polarisation [31][32]. Those reforms should be argued for on fairness grounds, without promising a civic truce they have not been shown to deliver.

**The public has not clearly asked for a new electoral system in Canada.** The 2005 British Columbia result was a majority that fell short of a supermajority rule. Later votes in British Columbia, Ontario and Prince Edward Island favoured the status quo, and the federal government abandoned its 2015 pledge after claiming that no consensus had emerged [21][22][23][24][26][30]. Those results can be criticised, but they cannot be ignored.

## Recommendations

These are this study's own proposals.

1. **Tell the two-party story accurately (our proposal).** In the United States, national politics is a two-party system. In Canada, two parties dominate government and the voting system inflates their seat share, but it is not true that only two parties matter.

2. **Treat contempt and misperception as problems in both camps (our proposal).** The research cited here is not a brief for one side. Public argument should use examples from both camps.

3. **Correct false pictures of the other side (our proposal).** Show people how the other party actually looks and what it actually believes, rather than its most viral members [20][36][37].

4. **Use citizens' assemblies and deliberative polls as advice, and be honest about their record at the ballot box (our proposal).** They improve the quality of a recommendation [19][28]. They are not a substitute for an election, and in the Canadian electoral-reform cases they have not been a reliable way to win one [21][22][24][26].

5. **If electoral rules are changed, change them for reasons the evidence can bear (our proposal).** A proportional or ranked-choice system can be defended as a fairer translation of votes into seats. It should not be sold as a proven treatment for polarisation.

6. **Do not ask reason to do the work of moral agreement (our proposal).** Some conflicts will remain after the facts are straight. The civic standard should be that they are argued without treating the other side as less than fellow citizens.

## Open questions

1. Has Canada's 2025 jump in the combined Liberal-Conservative vote share become a lasting move toward American-style two-party elections, or a one-campaign squeeze of smaller parties [6][7]?
2. Do ranked-choice voting and top-four or top-two primaries reduce affective polarisation, or mainly change nomination dynamics? Official adoption records are clear [33][34]; a causal verdict is **UNVERIFIED** here.
3. Why did affective polarisation fall in some democracies, including Britain, and rise in others with a variety of electoral systems [12]?
4. How long do gains from deliberative polls and short online treatments last once a real campaign begins [19][20]?
5. How should researchers talk about "extreme" views when the coding of that word can itself be contested [37]?
6. How far do laboratory findings on motivated reasoning travel once a 2021 replication has weakened one well-known numeracy result [15][17][35]?
