# Working together and turning swords into tools: the case for global cooperation and for refocusing defence industry on human needs

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. It has two linked parts: (A) why the whole world gains when countries cooperate, and (B) why and how arms-producing companies could shift capacity from weapons toward humanitarian needs. Figures come from the sources listed at the end and were checked in November 2026. Where a source could only be confirmed in a catalogue or summary, this is said in the text. Calculations marked "own calculation" are simple arithmetic on cited figures, not numbers stated by any source. Proposals marked "(this study's own proposal)" are this study's suggestions, not positions of any cited author or institution. The study takes no side with any government.*

## The ethical question

Every year the world spends record sums preparing for and fighting wars, while shared problems that no country can solve alone, such as pandemics, ozone loss, climate change, landmines and hunger, stay underfunded. Meanwhile a large industry earns most of its revenue from weapons, and its income grows when conflicts multiply.

The ethical question is: **given that cooperation produces large shared gains and conflict destroys them, how should states, companies and workers shift resources from preparing for war toward meeting human needs, without leaving people undefended or communities without work?**

Four narrower questions follow:

1. Is it actually better, in measurable terms, for countries to cooperate rather than compete militarily, and what makes cooperation succeed between rivals?
2. Is it ethical for private companies to profit from prolonged conflict, and what responsibilities follow from that profit?
3. Can the skills, factories and engineers of the arms industry be redirected to disaster response, healthcare, clean energy, climate resilience, demining and search and rescue, and what does the historical record say about trying?
4. How can this happen while respecting every state's right to self-defence and the livelihoods of defence workers?

## Scale, with real figures

### The cost of violence and military spending

- **Record military spending.** World military expenditure rose by 2.9% in real terms to US$2,887 billion in 2025, the 11th consecutive annual increase and the highest level SIPRI has recorded. It equalled 2.5% of world GDP, and global spending rose 41% over 2016–25 (Liang et al., 2026).
- **Record arms-industry revenue.** The 100 largest arms-producing and military services companies earned US$679 billion from arms in 2024, up 5.9% in real terms and 26% over 2015–24, also a record (Scarazzato et al., 2025).
- **The total economic cost of violence.** The Institute for Economics and Peace (IEP) estimates the global economic impact of violence in 2025 at US$21.8 trillion in purchasing-power-parity terms, 10.5% of world GDP, or US$2,657 per person, up 3.2% in a year, mainly because of higher military spending. In the ten countries most affected by violence it averaged 23.4% of GDP, against 2.2% in the ten least affected (Institute for Economics and Peace, 2026). The IEP's method is published in a peer-reviewed journal (Iqbal et al., 2019). Its figures are model estimates that include military and internal-security spending as a "cost", so they should be read as an order of magnitude, not a precise loss.
- **Peace gets a sliver.** The same report puts spending on peacebuilding and peacekeeping at US$49.2 billion in 2025, about 0.5% of military spending in PPP terms. Over 181,000 people were killed in conflict in 2025, and 117 million people were forcibly displaced (Institute for Economics and Peace, 2026).
- **Civil war shrinks economies.** In a cross-country study, per-capita GDP fell by about 2.2% a year during civil war relative to what it would otherwise have been (Collier, 1999).
- **Landmines keep killing after wars end.** At least 6,279 people were recorded killed or injured by landmines and explosive remnants of war in 2024 (1,945 killed, 4,325 injured), the highest annual figure since 2020. International mine-action funding totalled US$761 million in 2024 (International Campaign to Ban Landmines – Cluster Munition Coalition, 2025).

### What the money is not doing

- **Sustainable development is off track.** Of 139 SDG targets with enough data, only 35% are on track or making moderate progress. SIPRI researchers estimate that redirecting 7–13% of 2024 global military spending would have covered developing countries' gap in finance for climate adaptation. They also cite scientific estimates that militaries produce roughly 3.3–7.0% of global greenhouse-gas emissions (Tian & Liang, 2025). By own calculation, 7–13% of the US$2.7 trillion spent in 2024 is about US$190–350 billion a year.
- **The SDG investment gap.** UNCTAD estimates that developing countries face an SDG investment gap of about US$4 trillion a year, up from US$2.5 trillion in 2015 (UNCTAD, 2023).
- **Jobs per dollar.** Input–output studies of the United States find that the same spending creates more jobs in civilian sectors. One study estimated US$1 billion of military spending supported about 11,200 jobs, against 16,800 in clean energy, 17,200 in healthcare and 26,700 in education (Pollin & Garrett-Peltier, 2011). A later study for the Costs of War project found 6.9 jobs per US$1 million in defence spending, against 8.4–10.6 in clean energy and efficiency, 9.8 in infrastructure, 14.3 in healthcare and 15.2 in education (Garrett-Peltier, 2017). These are US-only, input–output estimates (see "Ethical tensions" for their limits).

### What cooperation has delivered

- **The ozone layer.** Under the 1987 Montreal Protocol, the ozone layer is on track to return to 1980 levels around 2066 over the Antarctic, 2045 over the Arctic and 2040 for the near-global average. Compliance with the 2016 Kigali Amendment, which phases down HFCs, is expected to avoid 0.3–0.5 °C of warming by 2100 (WMO, 2022). Because ozone-depleting substances are also greenhouse gases, the climate protection the Protocol had already achieved by 2007 was far larger than the first Kyoto Protocol target (Velders et al., 2007).
- **Smallpox.** During the Cold War, the USSR proposed global eradication at the World Health Assembly in 1958, and the United States was at first cautious about the Soviet-led proposal (Bhattacharya & Campani, 2019). WHO intensified the campaign in 1967, and an independent commission of scientists from 19 nations certified eradication in December 1979 (World Health Organization, n.d.).
- **Vaccines.** Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, reports helping vaccinate more than 1.3 billion children through routine programmes from 2000 to 2025 and averting more than 23 million future deaths (Gavi, n.d.); these are the organisation's own figures. An independent modelling study across 112 countries estimated that vaccination against ten pathogens would avert 97 million deaths over 2000–2030 (Toor et al., 2021).
- **Shared science.** CERN's founding convention was signed by 12 states in 1953 and came into force in 1954; it now has 25 member states (CERN, n.d.). ITER brings together China, the European Union, India, Japan, Korea, Russia and the United States to build an experimental fusion device (ITER Organization, n.d.). The International Space Station is run by five partner agencies, and 298 people from 26 countries have visited it (NASA, n.d.).

## Root causes

**Part A: why cooperation is undersupplied even though it pays.**

1. **Global public goods invite free-riding.** A clean atmosphere, disease eradication and peace benefit everyone whether or not they paid, so each state is tempted to let others carry the cost (Kaul et al., 1999). Barrett (2007) shows that how hard a good is to supply depends on its structure: some need one actor's best effort, some depend on the weakest link, and some need everyone's combined effort. Policy has to fit the type of good.
2. **War is costly, yet still happens.** Fearon (1995) shows that because fighting destroys value, there should almost always be a negotiated deal both sides prefer to war. Wars happen anyway because of private information and incentives to bluff, because states cannot credibly commit to keep a deal once power shifts, and because some stakes are hard to divide. Cooperation therefore depends on institutions that share information and make commitments credible.
3. **Short time horizons.** In repeated interactions, cooperation can emerge and hold between self-interested players when the "shadow of the future" is long enough and defection is answered (Axelrod & Hamilton, 1981). When leaders expect few future dealings, or think they can win outright, that logic weakens.
4. **Missing institutions.** Keohane (1984) argues that international regimes make cooperation easier by reducing transaction costs, providing information and linking issues, even without a single dominant power. Ostrom (1990; 2010) found that communities can manage shared resources without either privatisation or a central authority, when rules fit local conditions, users help set them, monitoring is accountable and sanctions are graduated, and when governance is spread across many levels (polycentric).

**Part B: why the arms industry stays focused on weapons.**

1. **Dependence on military sales.** For several of the largest firms, arms are almost the whole business. In 2024, arms made up 91% of Lockheed Martin's revenue, 92% of Northrop Grumman's and 95% of BAE Systems', but 54% of RTX's, 46% of Boeing's and 25% of AVIC's (Scarazzato et al., 2025). The more specialised a firm, the harder conversion is.
2. **Political influence.** In 1961 the outgoing US president warned against "the acquisition of unwarranted influence ... by the military-industrial complex" (Eisenhower, 1961). Melman (1997) argued that the post-war "permanent war economy" turned the US state into the top manager of a vast military industrial system, at the expense of civilian industry. Byrne (2007) argues that many arms makers act not simply as obedient instruments of a state but as politically powerful entities in their own right.
3. **Consolidation instead of conversion.** After the Cold War, the industry became sharply more concentrated, and there were very few successful conversions of weapons plants to civilian work (Dunne & Surry, 2006). US policy that encouraged rapid mergers among the largest contractors undercut dual-use aims and left a more segregated defence base dominated by a few firms (Oden et al., 2000).
4. **Geography and jobs.** Defence spending is spread across many districts, which makes cuts politically costly even when civilian spending would create more jobs (Garrett-Peltier, 2017).
5. **A rising-threat environment.** Spending is now rising in almost every region, partly in response to real wars (Liang et al., 2026). Firms respond to the demand they see.

## What has worked, and what hasn't

### Cooperation between rivals

- **Montreal Protocol.** It combined firm phase-out schedules with a way to keep adding new chemicals, and the ozone layer is now recovering (WMO, 2022; Velders et al., 2007).
- **Smallpox.** A Soviet proposal, adopted through WHO despite early US caution, became a single, well-defined global goal with a clear end point, reached in 1979 (Bhattacharya & Campani, 2019; World Health Organization, n.d.).
- **Big science.** CERN, ITER and the ISS show that rivals can share costs and facilities for long periods (CERN, n.d.; ITER Organization, n.d.; NASA, n.d.). There is no strong evidence that this cooperation spills over into trust on security questions, so it should be valued for itself, not oversold as a peace plan.
- **Trade.** Across a broad class of trade models, the gains from trade can be measured from just two numbers, a country's share of spending on its own goods and how sensitive trade is to costs, and the gains are positive (Arkolakis et al., 2012). The World Bank estimates that a 1% rise in participation in global value chains is associated with more than a 1% rise in per-capita income over time, against about 0.2% for conventional trade (World Bank, 2019). Trade can also be used as a weapon, and its local costs can be severe, so these gains are not automatic.
- **Prevention.** The joint UN–World Bank *Pathways for Peace* study estimated that a system for preventing violent conflict would save on average close to US$5 billion a year, and almost US$70 billion a year in the most optimistic scenario (United Nations & World Bank, 2018).

### Converting defence industry

- **The Lucas Plan (UK, 1976).** Faced with about 5,000 redundancies, shop stewards across Lucas Aerospace's sites drew up an alternative corporate plan of 150 products, including dialysis machines, heat pumps, a mobility vehicle for children with spina bifida and a road–rail bus (Mc Loughlin, 2017). Senior management rejected the plan outright, and the government of the day did not adopt it, partly because it prioritised defence jobs, exports and Britain's military role (Smith, 2014; Mc Loughlin, 2017). The plan failed as a business plan but is still widely cited as a model of worker-led, socially useful design.
- **US post-Cold War drawdown.** Defence-related private employment fell from 3.5 million to 2.1 million between 1987 and 1996, a 40% drop, and more than a million defence-related private jobs were lost in the 1990s (Oden et al., 2000). Large firms mostly consolidated rather than converted. But among 41 smaller defence-dependent firms interviewed in 1993–94, average defence dependency fell from 69% to 50%, and 72% were selling new products or services outside the defence market. Federal conversion programmes, including the Technology Reinvestment Project, helped in specific cases (Oden et al., 2000). Regional efforts worked best where local actors set aside rivalries and gave one agency the lead, but they were too small to change whole regional economies (Oden, 2000). A federal Defense Conversion Commission reported in December 1992 under the title *Adjusting to the Drawdown*; this study confirmed the report through library catalogue records but could not open the full text, so no findings from it are quoted here.
- **The overall record.** Reviews of the "long decade of disarmament" find very few cases of successful conversion of individual plants. Company-level conversion and diversification were more common but also had limited success (Dunne & Surry, 2006; Brzoska, 2007). The lesson is that conversion works better at the level of firms, regions and workers' skills than at the level of individual weapons lines.
- **Dedicated research.** The Bonn International Center for Conversion (now the Bonn International Centre for Conflict Studies, BICC) was founded in April 1994 by a German state government on the initiative of its premier and the then UN Secretary-General, with the conversion of former military resources as its original focus (BICC, 2019).

### Military capability for humanitarian ends

- **Dual-use spillovers are real.** GPS was built by the US military, and its civilian service is now free to all users worldwide; deliberate degradation of civilian accuracy was switched off in May 2000 (GPS.gov, n.d.-a; n.d.-b). The internet grew out of the ARPANET, a defence research network (Leiner et al., 2009). Across OECD countries, a 10% rise in government-funded R&D, of which defence R&D is the biggest part in many countries, brings a 5–6% rise in private R&D, with spillovers across borders and productivity gains (Moretti et al., 2025).
- **Military assets already help in disasters, under rules.** The UN's Oslo Guidelines allow foreign military and civil-defence assets in disaster relief only as a last resort, when civilian means cannot meet a critical need, and under civilian coordination (OCHA, 2007).

## Ethical tensions

1. **Self-defence is a right.** The UN Charter recognises the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs (United Nations, 1945, Art. 51). Countries under attack, such as Ukraine, which spent an estimated 40% of its GDP on its military in 2025 (Liang et al., 2026), cannot be asked to convert their defence industry first. Any conversion agenda has to be mutual and gradual, not one-sided disarmament.
2. **Deterrence works sometimes.** Research finds that deterrence can prevent attacks under some conditions, especially when the defender's military capability and resolve are credible, but the evidence is mixed and contested (Huth, 1999). This means neither "more weapons keep the peace" nor "fewer weapons keep the peace" can be stated as a general rule.
3. **Jobs and communities.** Civilian spending creates more jobs per dollar in US input–output studies, and the authors themselves note that some other methods find larger multipliers for military spending (Pollin & Garrett-Peltier, 2011). The 1990s drawdown left many skilled workers in long periods of unemployment (Oden et al., 2000). A just transition for defence workers is an ethical requirement, not an afterthought.
4. **Growth.** A survey of almost 170 studies finds that more recent work provides stronger evidence that military spending reduces economic growth (Dunne & Tian, 2013). Defence R&D, on the other hand, does generate spillovers (Moretti et al., 2025). Both can be true: a well-designed civilian R&D budget may capture similar spillovers without the costs of weapons.
5. **Profit from war.** Byrne (2007) argues that arms makers should be held responsible for the foreseeable consequences of how their products are used, including civil liability and, for war crimes or human-rights violations, responsibility under international human-rights standards. Others hold that firms supplying lawful governments for legitimate defence are acting ethically. The sharpest tension is when revenue depends on conflicts continuing, which gives firms a financial interest that runs against peace.
6. **Militarising humanitarian work.** Putting defence firms or armed forces into aid can blur the line between humanitarian and military action, which is why the Oslo Guidelines keep military assets as a last resort under civilian control (OCHA, 2007). Humanitarian conversion should strengthen civilian capacity, not militarise it.
7. **Dual use cuts both ways.** Technology built for search and rescue, such as drones or sensors, can be turned back to military use. Conversion does not end that risk; it needs export controls and transparency.

## Original proposals

All the proposals below are **(this study's own proposal)**. None is a position of any cited author or institution.

1. **Conversion and diversification funds (this study's own proposal).** Countries with large arms industries could each set up an independent fund, paid for by a small levy on arms revenues above a baseline, to co-finance civilian product lines in disaster response, medical devices, clean energy, climate resilience, demining and search and rescue. By own calculation, a levy of 1% on SIPRI Top 100 arms revenues would raise about US$6.8 billion a year, roughly nine times total international mine-action funding in 2024 (Scarazzato et al., 2025; International Campaign to Ban Landmines – Cluster Munition Coalition, 2025). The US record suggests the money should go mainly to smaller suppliers and regional programmes, where conversion has worked best (Oden et al., 2000; Oden, 2000).
2. **Humanitarian procurement incentives (this study's own proposal).** Governments and UN agencies could publish multi-year, guaranteed purchase commitments for specific humanitarian equipment, such as mine-clearance systems, flood and wildfire sensing, field hospitals and rapid water purification, open to any firm that meets civilian standards. Defence-procurement scoring could give extra weight to firms with a growing civilian humanitarian line. Delivery would stay under civilian humanitarian coordination, in line with the Oslo Guidelines (OCHA, 2007).
3. **Worker-led conversion plans (this study's own proposal).** Building on the Lucas Plan (Mc Loughlin, 2017; Smith, 2014), large defence firms could be required to fund an alternative-use committee of engineers and workers with paid design time. It would prepare civilian product plans, and management would have to give a formal, published response. This uses the people who know the factories best, and answers the Lucas Plan's main failure: there was no obligation to engage with it.
4. **Tax incentives with clawbacks (this study's own proposal).** Accelerated depreciation and enhanced R&D credits could apply to converting production lines and retraining staff for civilian humanitarian products, with the benefit clawed back if the line returns to weapons production within a set period.
5. **Conflict-exposure disclosure (this study's own proposal).** Listed arms makers could report arms revenue as a share of total revenue (as SIPRI already estimates), sales to states in active armed conflict, and their civilian diversification plans. That would let investors and the public see how dependent each firm is on conflict continuing.
6. **A global public-goods compact (this study's own proposal).** States could pledge a fixed share of any annual increase in military spending to pooled funds for global public goods: pandemic preparedness, climate adaptation, demining and conflict prevention. By own calculation, 1% of 2025 world military spending is about US$29 billion, more than half of all reported spending on peacebuilding and peacekeeping (Liang et al., 2026; Institute for Economics and Peace, 2026). Matching the structure of each good to the right mechanism follows Barrett (2007).
7. **Rival-inclusive science and prevention (this study's own proposal).** New CERN- and ITER-style projects could be open to all states, including rivals, in areas such as early warning for disasters, crop resilience and antimicrobial resistance. Alongside them, a standing prevention fund could act on the *Pathways for Peace* finding that prevention pays for itself (United Nations & World Bank, 2018). Projects should be designed so that withdrawal of one member does not end them.
8. **Regional transition offices (this study's own proposal).** Any region where defence work is concentrated could have a single lead agency for adjustment, ready in advance, since US regions did better when one agency had the lead and local rivalries were set aside (Oden, 2000).

## Risks

- **One-sided disarmament.** If only some countries convert, others may see an opening. Proposals must be mutual, verifiable and gradual (Fearon, 1995; Huth, 1999).
- **Greenwashing and "peacewashing".** Firms might add a small humanitarian line for reputation while weapons sales grow. Disclosure and clawbacks are the defence.
- **Failed products.** Most past plant-level conversions failed commercially (Dunne & Surry, 2006; Brzoska, 2007). Funds should back firms and workers' skills, not force individual factories into markets they cannot serve.
- **Job losses.** Badly handled cuts left skilled workers unemployed for years in the 1990s (Oden et al., 2000).
- **Dual-use leakage.** Humanitarian technology can be adapted for military use. Export controls must still apply.
- **Militarising aid.** Defence-firm involvement must not weaken the civilian character of humanitarian action (OCHA, 2007).
- **Unstable cooperation.** Shared projects can be paused or abandoned when politics change. Designing for resilience matters more than size.

## Recommendation

Treat cooperation as the default and conflict as the costly exception. The evidence shows large, measurable gains from cooperation: the ozone layer is healing, smallpox is gone, vaccines have saved tens of millions of lives, and preventing wars saves money. Meanwhile violence costs about a tenth of world GDP, and peacebuilding receives about half a percent of what goes to the military (WMO, 2022; Toor et al., 2021; United Nations & World Bank, 2018; Institute for Economics and Peace, 2026).

Defence industry should be refocused, not abolished. States keep their right to self-defence, and deterrence sometimes works. But the record shows that conversion succeeds when it is planned before cuts, led by regions and smaller firms, built on workers' own knowledge, and backed by guaranteed civilian demand. It fails when it is left to the largest firms to sort out alone. The most ethical path is a mutual, gradual shift: funded conversion and diversification, guaranteed demand for humanitarian equipment, worker-led plans with a duty to respond, transparency on conflict dependence, and a share of rising military budgets pooled for global public goods that every country, rival or ally, needs.
