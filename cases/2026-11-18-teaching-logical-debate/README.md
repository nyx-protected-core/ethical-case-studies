# Teaching reasoned debate: education that beats insult-driven and outrage-driven argument

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. Figures come from the sources listed at the end and were checked in November 2026. Effect sizes are averages across studies and vary widely between programmes. Proposals marked "(this study's own proposal)" are this study's own suggestions, not positions of any cited author or institution. This is the companion to the case study on universal nuclear disarmament, which argues that crises between nuclear-armed states are shaped by anger, humiliation and fast social media.*

## The ethical question

Public argument in many countries increasingly runs on contempt: insulting the other side, sharing what makes people angry, and treating opponents as enemies rather than fellow citizens with different views. At the extreme, the same habits shape how leaders talk to each other in international crises.

The ethical question is: **what do schools, universities, platforms and public institutions owe citizens in helping them reason well together, and how can that be done without telling people what to think, silencing strong feeling, or pretending that emotion has no place in argument?**

Three narrower questions follow:

1. Should critical thinking and structured debate be a required part of every child's education, given that the evidence shows these skills must be taught explicitly?
2. When social media platforms profit from outrage, what share of the duty to repair public reasoning falls on them?
3. Where is the line between teaching people to spot manipulation and teaching them to distrust everything, or to trust only approved sources?

A note on framing. It is often said that a new generation is more emotional and less logical than earlier ones. **The research does not support blaming a generation.** Reviews and a meta-analysis find that generational differences are small and hard to separate from age and the times people live in (Costanza et al., 2012; Rudolph et al., 2021). What has changed measurably is the environment: the rewards for outrage online, and rising hostility between political camps in some countries. This study focuses on those.

## Scale of the problem

**Rising hostility between political camps.** Political scientists use the term *affective polarisation* for dislike and distrust of the other side, as opposed to disagreement on policy. Shanto Iyengar and colleagues showed that American partisans' hostility to the other party is driven more by group identity than by policy disagreement (Iyengar et al., 2012). A later review found that Democrats and Republicans increasingly describe each other as hypocritical, selfish and closed-minded, and avoid socialising across party lines (Iyengar et al., 2019). Across twelve rich democracies over four decades, the United States saw the largest rise, five countries smaller rises, and six countries declines (Boxell et al., 2024). The problem is serious in some places and not inevitable anywhere.

**Online incentives reward contempt and outrage.**

- In a study of 563,312 social media messages about contested political issues, each additional moral-emotional word increased sharing by about 20 percent. The effect was stronger within like-minded networks than between them (Brady et al., 2017).
- An analysis of 2.7 million posts from news media and US politicians on Facebook and Twitter found that posts about the political out-group were shared about twice as often as posts about one's own side. Each word referring to the out-group raised the odds of a post being shared by 67 percent, an effect several times stronger than negative or moral-emotional language (Rathje et al., 2021).
- In studies of 12.7 million posts from 7,331 users, people who received more likes and shares after expressing outrage expressed more outrage later. Users in politically moderate networks were the most sensitive to this reward (Brady et al., 2021).
- Molly Crockett argues that digital media may amplify moral outrage by exposing people to far more triggering content, lowering the personal cost of expressing anger, and increasing its social rewards (Crockett, 2017).

**Many young people struggle to separate fact from opinion online.** In the OECD's 2018 PISA assessment, only about 8.7 percent of 15-year-olds across OECD countries reached the top reading levels, at which students can distinguish fact from opinion using implicit cues about content or source (OECD, 2019; OECD, 2021). This is a gap in teaching, not proof that young people reason worse than adults; adults are not tested in the same way.

## Root causes

### Reasoning evolved partly for winning arguments

Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber argue that human reasoning evolved mainly to produce and evaluate arguments in conversation. People are good at finding arguments for their own side and at spotting weaknesses in others' arguments, which explains confirmation bias. On their account reasoning works best in groups where people with different views test each other's arguments, and worst when people reason alone or only with allies (Mercier & Sperber, 2011). Insult-driven debate wastes this capacity: it attacks people instead of arguments, so nothing gets tested.

### Group identity, not only ignorance

Iyengar and colleagues find that partisan hostility rests on social identity, so that political disagreement feels like a contest between teams (Iyengar et al., 2012; Iyengar et al., 2019). Gordon Pennycook and David Rand found a more hopeful pattern for misinformation. People who scored higher on a test of reflective thinking were better at telling true from false headlines, even when the false headlines favoured their own party. They concluded that susceptibility to fake news is better explained by a lack of careful reasoning than by partisan motivation (Pennycook & Rand, 2019). If so, prompting and teaching reflection can help.

### Platforms' design

The studies above show that engagement-based feeds reward out-group hostility and outrage (Brady et al., 2017; Brady et al., 2021; Rathje et al., 2021). The causal picture for polarisation is mixed, however:

- Paying people to deactivate Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 US midterm elections reduced political polarisation and news knowledge and increased well-being (Allcott et al., 2020).
- Switching Facebook and Instagram users to a chronological feed during the 2020 US election changed what they saw substantially, but did not significantly change polarisation or political attitudes over three months (Guess et al., 2023).
- Paying Twitter users to follow accounts that posted opposing views did not soften them. Republicans became substantially more conservative, and Democrats' small shift was not statistically significant (Bail et al., 2018).

The lesson is that simply exposing people to "the other side" online is not enough, and may backfire. What seems to matter is the *kind* of contact: structured, respectful, in good faith.

### Critical thinking is rarely taught explicitly

A large meta-analysis of critical-thinking instruction concluded that improvement "cannot be a matter of implicit expectation": students gain most when critical-thinking objectives are made explicit (Abrami et al., 2008). Many school systems still assume students will pick these skills up along the way.

## What has worked

**Explicit critical-thinking instruction.** Philip Abrami and colleagues pooled 117 studies with 20,698 participants and found an average effect size of 0.34 standard deviations. Effects varied a great deal, and the type of intervention mattered: explicit instruction worked better than hoping skills would emerge from ordinary content teaching (Abrami et al., 2008). A second meta-analysis of 341 effect sizes found an average effect of 0.30, with gains at all educational levels and in all subjects. Three features stood out: **opportunities for dialogue, authentic real-world problems, and mentoring** (Abrami et al., 2015).

**Argument mapping.** Tim van Gelder draws six lessons from cognitive science for teaching critical thinking, including that skills need deliberate practice, that transfer to new contexts must itself be practised, and that diagramming arguments helps (van Gelder, 2005). In a comparison of methods at two Australian universities, Charles Twardy found that computer-based argument mapping more than tripled the gains students made on a standard critical-thinking test compared with other methods (Twardy, 2004). These are single-site results and should be replicated in schools.

**Dialogue between students who disagree.** In a multi-year programme, Deanna Kuhn and Amanda Crowell had young adolescents carry out structured written dialogues with peers who held opposing views on social issues. These students later wrote better argumentative essays on new topics, and paid more attention to evidence, than students who had extensive essay practice with whole-class discussion (Kuhn & Crowell, 2011).

**Competitive policy debate.** In Boston's public schools, which serve many low-income students, Beth Schueler and Katherine Larned found that joining the school debate league raised English test scores by 0.13 standard deviations, with gains concentrated in analytical rather than rote skills and largest for students who had been lowest-achieving. There was no harm to maths, attendance or discipline, and debaters were more likely to graduate and go on to college (Schueler & Larned, 2023). Because students choose to join debate, the authors used methods that compare students with themselves over time and with similar non-debaters, but the study is not a randomised trial.

**Intellectual humility.** Intellectual humility means recognising that one's beliefs might be wrong. Mark Leary and colleagues found that people higher in intellectual humility were more attentive to the strength of arguments and less likely to judge politicians who changed their minds as "flip-floppers" (Leary et al., 2017). Tenelle Porter and Karina Schumann found that people higher in intellectual humility were more open to learning about opposing views, and that teaching a "growth mindset" about intelligence increased both humility and openness (Porter & Schumann, 2018). A review in *Nature Reviews Psychology* summarises its predictors and consequences and notes that it can be influenced by context and teaching (Porter et al., 2022).

**Deliberation among ordinary citizens.**

- In James Fishkin's *Deliberative Polls*, a random sample of citizens receives balanced briefing materials, discusses issues in small moderated groups and questions experts. Participants reliably gain knowledge, and their views often change in ways linked to what they learn, without becoming more polarised (Fishkin & Luskin, 2005).
- In "America in One Room", more than 500 registered US voters from across the country deliberated for a long weekend. Compared with a control group, they showed large depolarising changes on policy and large decreases in affective polarisation (Fishkin et al., 2021).
- John Dryzek and colleagues summarise research showing that well-designed deliberation can help citizens avoid polarisation and reach considered judgements (Dryzek et al., 2019).
- The OECD collected 289 cases of representative deliberative processes, such as citizens' assemblies, between 1986 and 2019, and described a "deliberative wave" across democracies (OECD, 2020).
- Ireland's Citizens' Assembly (2016–18), following an earlier Convention on the Constitution, played a significant role in preparing referendums on marriage equality (2015) and abortion (2018) (Farrell et al., 2019).

The philosophical root of this work is Jürgen Habermas's account of *communicative action*, in which people coordinate by giving reasons that others could accept, rather than by force or manipulation (Finlayson & Rees, 2023).

**Prebunking and inoculation against manipulation.**

- In the online game *Bad News*, players take the role of a fake-news producer and learn six manipulation techniques. In a pre–post study with about 15,000 participants, players became better at spotting misinformation (Roozenbeek & van der Linden, 2019).
- Short videos explaining five manipulation techniques, including emotionally manipulative language, false dichotomies, scapegoating and ad hominem attacks, improved recognition of those techniques in six randomised experiments (6,464 people) and a field study on YouTube (22,632 people) (Roozenbeek et al., 2022).
- Warning people in advance about misleading arguments on climate change protected them against those arguments (van der Linden et al., 2017).
- **This evidence is contested.** A reanalysis of five studies of the *Bad News* and *Go Viral!* games, using a method that separates true skill from general scepticism, found that the games made people rate *all* news as less reliable rather than improving their ability to tell true from false (Modirrousta-Galian & Higham, 2023). Prebunking should therefore be measured on discernment, not just on suspicion of false items.

**Checking sources the way fact-checkers do.** Researchers at Stanford taught high-school government students in a whole district to "read laterally", leaving an unfamiliar website to see what other sources say about it. After six lessons, students' ability to judge online sources improved significantly more than in comparison classrooms (Wineburg et al., 2022). A similar intervention improved university students' evaluation of online information (McGrew et al., 2019).

**Emotion regulation.** James Gross's process model describes how people can regulate emotion at different stages, for example by choosing situations, shifting attention, or *reappraising* what a situation means (Gross, 1998; Gross, 2015). In two studies in Israel, Eran Halperin and colleagues trained people in cognitive reappraisal and then showed them anger-inducing information about the Israeli–Palestinian conflict. Trained participants were more supportive of conciliatory policies and less supportive of aggressive ones, and the effect was still present five months later (Halperin et al., 2013).

**Respectful storytelling.** In three field experiments with 6,869 voters, Joshua Kalla and David Broockman found that conversations using arguments alone did not reduce exclusionary attitudes, but otherwise identical conversations that included a non-judgemental exchange of personal stories did, for at least four months. The effects were small (Kalla & Broockman, 2020).

## Emotion is not the enemy

It would be a mistake to frame the problem as "logic good, emotion bad".

- Antonio Damasio's *somatic marker hypothesis* proposes that bodily emotional signals help guide decisions (Damasio, 1996). In a well-known experiment, healthy people began choosing advantageously in a card game, and showed anticipatory stress responses to risky choices, before they could say which strategy was best. Patients with damage to the prefrontal region involved in these signals kept choosing badly even after they knew the right strategy (Bechara et al., 1997).
- A major review of 35 years of research concludes that emotions are powerful, pervasive and predictable drivers of decisions, sometimes harmful and sometimes helpful (Lerner et al., 2015).
- In international politics, Rose McDermott and Jonathan Mercer argue that rational judgement depends on emotion (McDermott, 2004; Mercer, 2010).

So the goal is not to suppress emotion. It is to help people notice their emotions, reappraise when anger or contempt is misleading them, and keep their arguments aimed at ideas rather than at people. Halperin's results show that this kind of regulation can change political judgements (Halperin et al., 2013). Kalla and Broockman's results show that feeling, in the form of personal stories, can open minds that arguments alone did not (Kalla & Broockman, 2020).

## Ethical tensions

**Neutrality versus judgement.** Teaching people to spot weak arguments and manipulation is not the same as teaching them what to conclude. Programmes must teach methods that apply to all sides, or they will be seen, often rightly, as political.

**Scepticism versus cynicism.** Training people to doubt can make them doubt everything, including reliable information (Modirrousta-Galian & Higham, 2023). Good programmes teach people where to put their trust, not only how to withhold it (Wineburg et al., 2022).

**Civility versus justice.** Calls for calm debate can be used to dismiss people with real grievances, whose anger is justified. The research on outrage describes its amplification online (Brady et al., 2021; Crockett, 2017), not whether a particular cause is right. Reasoned debate should make room for strong feeling about injustice while refusing personal attack.

**Reduced hostility is not the whole goal.** Experiments that reduced partisan dislike did not, in themselves, reduce support for undemocratic candidates or partisan violence (Voelkel et al., 2023). Warmer feelings are good, but democratic commitments need to be taught and defended directly.

**Platform responsibility versus free expression.** Companies that profit from outrage bear some duty to reduce its amplification. But design changes can carry unintended effects and raise questions about who decides what counts as "outrage". Evidence on feed changes is mixed (Guess et al., 2023; Allcott et al., 2020).

**Equity.** Competitive debate has often been an activity for well-off schools. The Boston results suggest the greatest benefits go to students who were furthest behind (Schueler & Larned, 2023), so limiting access to debate is itself an equity problem.

## Original proposals

1. **Argument and debate in every school, every year (this study's own proposal).** From the late primary years, every student takes a sequence that combines:
   - explicit critical-thinking lessons, since implicit approaches do not work well (Abrami et al., 2008);
   - argument mapping of real public controversies (van Gelder, 2005; Twardy, 2004);
   - structured written dialogue with classmates who disagree (Kuhn & Crowell, 2011);
   - lateral reading and source checking (Wineburg et al., 2022); and
   - at least one year of team policy debate, with students arguing both sides of a resolution, free of charge and prioritised for low-income schools (Schueler & Larned, 2023).
2. **"Steelman before you strike" as a classroom and debate rule (this study's own proposal).** Before rebutting an opponent, a speaker must restate the opponent's best argument in a way the opponent accepts. Personal insults cost points; changing one's mind in response to evidence earns them. The rule puts intellectual humility into practice (Leary et al., 2017; Porter & Schumann, 2018).
3. **Emotion skills as part of civics (this study's own proposal).** Civics courses include short training in recognising emotions and in cognitive reappraisal when discussing heated topics, based on Gross's model and Halperin's findings (Gross, 2015; Halperin et al., 2013). The aim is to manage emotion, not to deny it.
4. **Platform "reasoning defaults" (this study's own proposal).** Large platforms should:
   - stop weighting replies and shares that contain personal attacks on the out-group as strongly as other engagement, given the evidence that out-group animosity is the strongest driver of sharing (Rathje et al., 2021);
   - show short prebunking clips about manipulation techniques before major elections and crises, measured on whether users get better at *telling true from false*, not only at doubting (Roozenbeek et al., 2022; Modirrousta-Galian & Higham, 2023); and
   - give independent researchers access to data to test these changes, as in the 2020 election studies (Guess et al., 2023).
5. **Standing citizens' assemblies on divisive issues (this study's own proposal).** National and local governments convene randomly selected citizens' assemblies on the most polarised questions, with balanced evidence, moderated small groups and published reasoning, on the Irish and Deliberative Polling models (Farrell et al., 2019; Fishkin et al., 2021; OECD, 2020). Their recommendations would receive a formal government response.
6. **A cross-border student debate network on peace and security (this study's own proposal).** Students from countries in tension, including nuclear-armed states, would debate questions of arms control and disarmament online and in person, under the "steelman" rule. This links this study to its companion on nuclear disarmament: future leaders would learn to argue hard without humiliating each other.
7. **Measure what matters (this study's own proposal).** Education systems add assessments of argument quality, source evaluation and openness to opposing views alongside reading and maths, building on the PISA finding that few students can reliably separate fact from opinion (OECD, 2019).

## Risks

- **Weak implementation.** Average effects of critical-thinking teaching are moderate and vary widely (Abrami et al., 2008; Abrami et al., 2015). Poorly taught courses could deliver little.
- **Backfire.** Exposure to opposing views without structure can harden attitudes (Bail et al., 2018). Programmes should use structured, respectful formats.
- **Blanket cynicism.** Inoculation that simply raises suspicion could make people distrust accurate information (Modirrousta-Galian & Higham, 2023).
- **Political capture.** Governments or platforms could use "reasoning" or "misinformation" labels to suppress dissent. Methods must be transparent, cover all sides and be open to independent review.
- **Debate as performance.** Competitive debate can reward clever point-scoring over truth-seeking. Rules that reward accurately stating the other side and changing one's mind help guard against this.
- **Overpromising on polarisation.** Reducing hostility does not automatically protect democratic norms (Voelkel et al., 2023), and some platform changes have shown little effect on attitudes (Guess et al., 2023).

## Recommendation

Every education system should treat reasoned argument as a core skill, taught explicitly and practised through dialogue, argument mapping, source checking and structured debate, with emotion-regulation skills built in rather than treating feeling as the enemy. The strongest evidence supports explicit instruction with dialogue and real problems (Abrami et al., 2015), structured disagreement between peers (Kuhn & Crowell, 2011), debate programmes that reach disadvantaged students (Schueler & Larned, 2023), and deliberation among ordinary citizens (Fishkin et al., 2021).

The problem is not that one generation feels too much. It is that today's information systems reward contempt (Rathje et al., 2021; Brady et al., 2021) and that schools rarely teach people how to disagree well. Platforms should stop amplifying out-group hostility and should test, in the open, whether their changes improve discernment. Governments should give citizens regular, structured ways to deliberate together. A public that can argue hard without humiliating opponents is also a public that will demand the same restraint from leaders in a crisis.

### Notes on verification

- Every DOI above was resolved through doi.org and its title, authors and year checked against publisher metadata in November 2026. The one web link (the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry) was opened in November 2026.
- Figures quoted from abstracts (for example the 20 percent, 67 percent, 0.34, 0.30 and 0.13 effects) were read from the published abstracts. The 12.7 million posts and 7,331 users figures for Brady et al. (2021) were confirmed in the university's published summary of the study.
- The PISA figure of 8.7 percent refers to students reaching reading Levels 5 and 6, the levels at which the OECD describes students as able to distinguish fact from opinion using implicit cues. It is sometimes reported as "only 9 percent can tell fact from opinion", which oversimplifies.
- Twardy (2004) reports results from one comparison between two universities; argument-mapping effects in schools need further independent testing.
- Evidence on the long-term effects of prebunking games is disputed (see Modirrousta-Galian & Higham, 2023), and this study presents it as such.
