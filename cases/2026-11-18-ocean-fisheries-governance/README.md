# Who gets the fish? Overfishing, illegal fishing, harmful subsidies and forced labour at sea as a global justice problem

Status date: 18 November 2026. Numbers in square brackets point to the numbered list under "Scholarly sources" at the end. Every source was checked on the status date, either by resolving its DOI through the Crossref registry and reading the abstract, or by reading the institution's own public page. Sentences marked **(our arithmetic)** are simple calculations made from cited figures. Sentences marked **(our proposal)** are this study's own ideas and are not claims about what any source says.

A companion case in this collection covers a low-cost position and distress tracker for small fishing boats. That case is about one device. This one is about the rules, money and institutions that decide who fishes, who profits and who pays.

## The ethical question

Wild fish are a shared, renewable resource that can be ruined by a race to catch them first. The people who depend on them most, coastal and small-island communities in the Global South, usually have the least power over how they are fished. Distant-water fleets from richer or more heavily subsidising states can fish their waters, often under-report what they take, and sometimes rely on crews held in forced labour. The question for this case is:

**How should the world share, protect and police marine fisheries so that the food security and income of coastal communities come first, harmful subsidies stop paying for overfishing, illegal catch cannot reach market, and fishers work in decent conditions? And how can that be done by cooperation that gives real weight to the Global South, small island states and middle powers, not by a few large economies setting rules for everyone else?**

## Scale

**State of stocks.** FAO's *State of World Fisheries and Aquaculture 2024* reports that the share of assessed marine stocks fished within biologically sustainable levels fell to 62.3 percent in 2021, so 37.7 percent were fished at unsustainable levels. The sustainable share was 90 percent in 1974 [1]. Weighted by catch, 76.9 percent of 2021 landings from monitored stocks came from sustainable stocks, because well-managed large stocks such as major tunas do better than the average [1]. FAO's 2025 *Review of the state of world marine fishery resources* used a new method with 2,570 disaggregated stocks and estimated 64.5 percent sustainable and 35.5 percent overfished for the same base year, with the overfished share still rising by about 1 percent a year [2]. The two numbers are not a trend; they are two methods applied to 2021.

**Management makes the difference.** For scientifically assessed stocks, which make up about half of reported global marine catch, abundance on average is rising and close to target levels. Regions with less developed fisheries management have, on average, harvest rates three times higher and half the abundance [3]. A study of 4,713 fisheries representing 78 percent of reported catch found that the median fishery is overfished, but that sound management reforms could add more than 16 million tonnes of catch, USD 53 billion in profit and 619 million tonnes of fish in the sea each year compared with business as usual, with the median fishery recovering in under ten years [4]. The World Bank's *Sunken Billions Revisited* estimated that poor management cost the world about USD 83 billion in forgone net benefits from marine fisheries in 2012 [5].

**Illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing.** The first worldwide estimate put illegal and unreported catch at 11 to 26 million tonnes a year, worth USD 10 to 23.5 billion, found a significant correlation between weak governance and illegal fishing, and estimated that total catches in West Africa were 40 percent higher than reported catches [6]. That estimate is now old (it reviews data up to the mid-2000s) and is best read as a baseline. A later study estimated that 8 to 14 million tonnes of unreported catch may be traded illicitly each year, with gross revenues of USD 9 to 17 billion, and losses to countries' tax revenues of USD 2 to 4 billion [7]. For six West African states (Mauritania, Senegal, The Gambia, Guinea-Bissau, Guinea and Sierra Leone), losses to illegal fishing were estimated at USD 2.3 billion a year between 2010 and 2016, while only USD 13 million a year was recovered through monitoring, control and surveillance; illegal catch was equal to 65 percent of the legal reported catch [8].

**Subsidies.** Global fisheries subsidies were estimated at USD 35.4 billion in 2018, of which USD 22.2 billion were "capacity-enhancing" subsidies such as fuel subsidies that encourage more fishing. The top five subsidisers (China, the European Union, the United States, the Republic of Korea and Japan) provided 58 percent of the total [9]. Without subsidies, as much as 54 percent of present high-seas fishing grounds would be unprofitable at current fishing rates; the authors note that much of the fishing by the largest fleets would be unprofitable "without subsidies and low labor costs" [10].

**Visibility.** Satellite tracking of more than 70,000 industrial fishing vessels showed industrial fishing in more than 55 percent of the ocean's area [11]. But a 2024 study combining satellite imagery, vessel GPS data and machine learning found that 72 to 76 percent of the world's industrial fishing vessels are not publicly tracked, with much of that fishing around South Asia, Southeast Asia and Africa [12].

**Food and jobs.** In 22 West African countries, an estimated 6.7 million people depend directly on fishing for food or livelihood, and re-estimation roughly doubled the landed value of small-scale catches to USD 3.5 billion a year compared with official figures [13]. Researchers estimated that 845 million people, about 11 percent of the world's population, are poised to become deficient in zinc, iron or vitamin A if fish-catch declines continue [14].

**Forced labour at sea.** At the national level, the Global Slavery Index (used as a proxy for labour abuse) correlates with weak fisheries governance among major fishing countries, and countries with documented labour abuse at sea tend to have more subsidised distant-water fishing and poorer catch reporting [15]. A global risk mapping found that 57 percent of assessed ports were associated with labour abuse or IUU fishing risk, and 82 percent of trips ending in those ports were linked to such risks; at-sea risk was driven by flags with poor control of corruption and by foreign ownership of vessels [16]. This links directly to human trafficking: crews recruited through debt and deception are the same people the anti-trafficking cases in this collection try to protect.

## Root causes

1. **Open access and the race to fish.** Where nobody holds a secure, limited right, each boat gains by catching fish before others do. Marine fisheries now need twice the effort of the 1950s to catch the same quantity [15].
2. **Subsidies that keep excess fleets at sea.** Capacity-enhancing subsidies, especially for fuel, lower the cost of fishing further away and longer, and their share of total subsidies has risen [9]. They make fishing profitable on high-seas grounds and in distant waters where it otherwise would not be [10].
3. **Weak monitoring in coastal states.** Many West African states lack maritime domain awareness, trained staff, money for patrols and cooperation with neighbours, which leaves existing surveillance ineffective [17]. Higher fines are associated with less severe illegal fishing, but almost none of the lost value is recovered [8].
4. **Unequal access deals.** In West Africa, European Union and Chinese fleets reported only 29 percent and 8 percent respectively of their estimated catches between 2000 and 2010. The EU paid host countries about 8 percent of the landed value of its catch and China about 4 percent, and the two performed similarly on illegal fishing and sustainability [18].
5. **Flags of convenience, foreign ownership and transshipment.** Vessels flagged to states with poor control of corruption, or owned from abroad, carry higher risks of both illegal fishing and labour abuse [16]. Vessels can also obscure their identity and reflag, and reflagging happens in a few ports, mainly by fleets with high foreign ownership; about 17 percent of high-seas fishing is by potentially unauthorised or internationally unregulated vessels [19].
6. **Migrant crews with no bargaining power.** Research on the Thai fishing sector argues that slavery and trafficking framings drew needed attention to criminal recruitment, but that lasting change needs reform of migration rules so that migrant fishers can change employers and bargain for better conditions [20].

## What has worked

**Port state measures.** The FAO Agreement on Port State Measures (PSMA) is the first binding international agreement to specifically target IUU fishing. It works by preventing vessels engaged in IUU fishing from using ports and landing their catch [40]. It entered into force on 5 June 2016 and, as of 2026, has 86 parties, including the European Union acting for its member states, so it covers 112 states [21][22]. The parties include coastal states across the Global South and the Pacific (for example Senegal, Ghana, Indonesia, Peru, Chile, Fiji, Palau, Tuvalu and Vanuatu) and major fishing and market states including China, Japan, the Republic of Korea, the Russian Federation, Saudi Arabia, the United States and the European Union [21]. The port is where risks concentrate, which is why port measures are named as a promising response to both IUU fishing and labour abuse [16].

**WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies.** Adopted at the 12th WTO Ministerial Conference in June 2022, it entered into force on 15 September 2025, when two-thirds of WTO members had accepted it [23]. It is the WTO's first agreement focused on the environment. It bans subsidies to vessels or operators engaged in IUU fishing, subsidies for fishing an overfished stock, and subsidies for fishing on the unregulated high seas [24]. A WTO Fish Fund pays for technical help to developing and least-developed members [23]. The agreement does not yet discipline subsidies that cause overcapacity in general; those negotiations continue [23]. Under Article 12, the agreement terminates automatically if comprehensive disciplines are not adopted within four years of entry into force, unless the General Council decides otherwise [24], which points to September 2029 **(our arithmetic)**.

**The High Seas Treaty (BBNJ Agreement).** The Agreement on marine biological diversity of areas beyond national jurisdiction was adopted on 19 June 2023 and entered into force on 17 January 2026, 120 days after the sixtieth ratification on 19 September 2025 [25][26]. It covers waters beyond national jurisdiction, about half of the planet's surface, and makes possible marine protected areas on the high seas, rules on marine genetic resources, environmental impact assessments, and capacity building and technology transfer for developing countries [41]. At entry into force it was binding on 81 parties, including China, Japan, France and Brazil; the United States had signed but not ratified [27]. The treaty does not itself regulate fishing quotas, which stay with regional fisheries bodies, but its high-seas protected areas and capacity-building provisions matter for fisheries.

**Indonesia's crackdown on foreign IUU fishing.** Indonesia's aggressive policies against IUU fishing by foreign-flagged vessels reduced total fishing effort by at least 25 percent. The study estimates that this approach could raise catch by 14 percent and profit by 12 percent, compared with a 15 percent catch cut and a 16 percent profit cut under typical management reforms; in other words, stopping illegal foreign fishing can let stocks recover without short-term losses for local fishers [28]. In 2017 Indonesia also became the first country to publish its vessel monitoring system (VMS) data through Global Fishing Watch [29].

**Pacific small island states taking control: the Vessel Day Scheme.** The Parties to the Nauru Agreement (PNA), a group of Pacific island parties managing purse-seine tuna fishing in their exclusive economic zones in the western and central Pacific [31], replaced fleet-capacity limits with a cap on fishing days that distant-water fleets must buy. A legal analysis describes it as the largest and most complex fishery management arrangement of its time, one that "puts power firmly in the hands of Pacific Island States", against resistance from distant-water fishing nations [30]. The PNA reports that revenue to its members rose from about USD 60 million in 2010 to close to USD 400 million in 2015 [31]; that figure is the PNA's own and was not independently audited for this study. The Pacific Islands Forum Fisheries Agency is the region's wider fisheries cooperation body; an Australian National University publication reviews its forty years of regional cooperation [32].

**West African regional surveillance.** The Fisheries Committee for the West Central Gulf of Guinea (FCWC), with Benin, Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Liberia, Nigeria and Togo, runs a Regional Monitoring, Control and Surveillance Centre in Tema, Ghana. It combines a regional VMS with AIS and lets members without their own VMS monitor vessels in their waters; members keep control over sharing data on their own vessels [33]. It was funded through an EU project with technical partners [33]. Independent evidence on its effect on illegal catch was not found for this study.

**Latin American transparency and rights-based management.** Peru introduced individual vessel quotas in its anchoveta fishery with the aim of reducing the race to fish [34]. Peru became the first Latin American country to publish VMS data through Global Fishing Watch in 2018, and nearly 2,000 industrial and artisanal vessels have since been publicly visible [35]. Chile signed an agreement in 2019 to publish its VMS data after a law required tracking information to be public [36].

**Labour standards at sea.** The ILO Work in Fishing Convention, 2007 (No. 188) sets standards for fishers' conditions of service, safety and health, medical care and social security, and lets ratifying states inspect foreign fishing vessels in their ports against these standards [37]. It entered into force on 16 November 2017 [38]. As of October 2026 it had 27 ratifications, including Thailand, Senegal, Namibia, Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, Indonesia and the Solomon Islands, against 113 for the Maritime Labour Convention [37][39].

## Ethical tensions

1. **Coastal sovereignty versus open access.** Coastal states have the right to manage their own waters, but many earn badly needed revenue by selling access to foreign fleets. Low access fees and under-reported catches mean the deal can transfer food and wealth out of poor countries [18]. Banning foreign access outright could cut revenue unless local capacity grows.
2. **Subsidy reform versus small-scale fishers' livelihoods.** Many governments, including in the Global South, subsidise fuel for poor fishers. Cutting harmful subsidies helps stocks [9][10] but can hurt the poorest boats first unless support is shifted to forms that do not add fishing pressure. The agreement's Fish Fund pays for technical help to developing and least-developed members [23], but it does not by itself replace lost income for poor fishers.
3. **Transparency versus safety and privacy.** Public vessel tracking exposes illegal fishing [11][29], but small-scale fishers may fear that their fishing grounds become visible to competitors, and crew locations could matter for their safety. The companion device case addresses that tension at the level of a single boat.
4. **Enforcement versus due process.** Strong enforcement, as in Indonesia, worked [28], but harsh measures can fall on crews who were themselves exploited. Crew rescued from an illegal vessel may be victims of trafficking, not offenders [20].
5. **Market pressure versus who sets the rules.** Import bans and catch-documentation rules from large markets can push improvements, but they can also be designed by importers without the voice of the countries most affected. Rules made inside multilateral bodies such as FAO, WTO and ILO, where small states vote, have more legitimacy.
6. **"Trafficking" framing versus labour rights framing.** Calling all abuse "slavery" attracts attention and money, but may lead to raids and rescues instead of the migration and labour reforms that change conditions for most fishers [20].

## Original proposals

Everything in this section is **this study's own proposal**. None of it is claimed by the sources.

1. **A "first claim" principle for coastal food security (our proposal).** Access agreements with distant-water fleets should include, as a standard clause, a protected zone and catch share for small-scale and subsistence fishers, set before any foreign quota, with catch reported by species and landing port. This builds on evidence that small-scale fisheries feed millions in West Africa [13] and that access deals return little value [18].
2. **Pooled port-state inspection for fish and for fishers (our proposal).** States that are parties to both the PSMA and ILO C188 should train port inspectors to check catch documents and crew conditions in the same visit, sharing results across a regional group (for example the FCWC or the PNA). Risks for IUU fishing and labour abuse are concentrated in the same ports [16], and both treaties already allow inspection of foreign vessels [21][37].
3. **A subsidy swap fund (our proposal).** Large subsidisers should commit a share of the money freed up by the WTO disciplines to the WTO Fish Fund and to regional surveillance centres in coastal developing states, so that subsidy reform directly pays for monitoring in the places most harmed by illegal fishing. West Africa loses an estimated USD 2.3 billion a year and recovers about USD 13 million [8].
4. **Public tracking as a condition of access (our proposal).** Any foreign vessel buying fishing rights in another state's waters should be required to make its VMS position public, as Indonesia, Peru and Chile have done for their own fleets [29][35][36], and to keep a single unique vessel identifier for life to prevent identity changes [19]. Small-scale domestic boats should be exempt unless they choose to join.
5. **South-South and middle-power coalitions to finish the WTO rules (our proposal).** Coastal developing states, small island states (whose Vessel Day Scheme shows what collective bargaining can win [30][31]) and middle powers should work as a bloc in the WTO talks on overcapacity subsidies before the 2029 deadline, and should seek the support of all large fishing nations, including China, which has joined both the PSMA and the BBNJ Agreement [21][27].
6. **Crew-protection firewall (our proposal).** When a vessel is detained for IUU fishing, its crew should be screened as possible trafficking victims by labour or social authorities before any criminal process, and given wages owed and safe repatriation, consistent with the labour-rights framing in [20].

## Risks

- **Displacement.** Stronger rules in one region can push illegal vessels to weaker ones; the global risk study warns that coordination is needed to avoid risk displacement [16].
- **Sunset of the WTO agreement.** If comprehensive disciplines are not agreed within four years, the agreement terminates [24].
- **Uneven ratification.** C188 has only 27 ratifications [37], and some large fishing and port states have not joined the PSMA or ratified BBNJ [21][27].
- **Old baselines.** The global IUU estimate dates from 2009 [6]; current figures are uncertain and any reported "progress" should be checked against new data.
- **Self-reported success.** Revenue figures for the Vessel Day Scheme come from the PNA itself [31], and no independent evaluation of the FCWC surveillance centre's effect was found.
- **Harm to small-scale fishers.** Badly designed subsidy cuts, tracking rules or enforcement can fall hardest on poor fishers rather than on industrial fleets.
- **Data and power.** Public vessel data could be used by stronger fleets to find fish, not only by regulators to find offenders.

## Recommendation

1. Treat ocean fisheries as a food-security and labour-rights issue, not only an environmental one, and give coastal communities in the Global South first claim on their own fish.
2. Widen and use the treaties that already exist: more parties to the PSMA, the WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies, the BBNJ Agreement and ILO C188, with funding for implementation in developing states [21][23][25][37].
3. Finish WTO disciplines on overcapacity subsidies before the 2029 sunset, and redirect freed money to monitoring and to non-harmful support for small-scale fishers [9][23][24].
4. Make industrial fishing visible: public VMS for vessels fishing in foreign waters or on the high seas, and lifetime vessel identifiers [12][19][29].
5. Copy what small and middle states have proven: the Pacific's collective control over access [30], Indonesia's action against foreign IUU fleets [28], West Africa's shared surveillance [33] and Latin American transparency [35][36].
6. Inspect for fish and for fishers in the same port visit, and treat exploited crews as people to protect, not as offenders [16][20].
