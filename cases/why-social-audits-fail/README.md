# When the Audit Says "Compliant": Accountability Failures and How to Fix Them

*An ethics case study. Research only: nobody was contacted in preparing it. Every figure below was checked against the source's own text, and every link and DOI was resolved live on 18 November 2026. The Sources section records how each check was done. Anything that could not be confirmed is marked **UNVERIFIED** or has been left out.*

---

## Part 1. Three candidate problems

### A. Social audits in supply chains that miss forced labour (chosen for the deep dive)

- **Problem.** Brands rely on private "ethical" or "social" audits to show their suppliers respect labour standards. Factories that had passed these audits have killed hundreds of workers, and the research literature finds that audits usually fail to detect or correct the abuses most closely linked to forced labour. These abuses are also concentrated in the lower supply-chain tiers that audits rarely reach.
- **Proposed fix.** Replace buyer-controlled, announced, supplier-paid audits with a **worker-driven** model. Workers write the code. An independent monitor that is not paid by the audited site runs both a confidential complaint line and unannounced audits. Buyers sign binding contracts that cut off non-compliant suppliers, and aggregate results are published.

### B. Corporate pay-equity audits and reporting

- **Problem.** Gender pay-gap audits are often self-run and descriptive, with no follow-up required, so a published number can stand in for real change.
- **Evidence that design matters.** In Denmark, a 2006 law required firms to provide gender-disaggregated wage statistics. Bennedsen, Simintzi, Tsoutsoura and Wolfenzon (2022) found that the law cut the gender pay gap by about **2 percentage points, or 13% relative to the pre-law mean**, mainly by slowing male wage growth, with no effect on firm profitability.
- **Proposed fix.** Make disclosure mandatory and standardised, with independent verification and a required, published remediation plan wherever an unexplained gap exceeds a set threshold.

### C. Auditing automated decision systems in public services

- **Problem.** When governments automate decisions about benefits or debts, the usual checks (internal legal review, ombudsmen, audit offices, tribunals) can all fail at once. Carney (2018) analysed Australia's "robo-debt" programme and identified "serious structural deficiencies in the design of accountability and remedial avenues at seven different levels". The later Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme produced a report of more than 990 pages with **57 recommendations**, and a sealed chapter recommending that individuals be referred for civil and criminal prosecution.
- **Proposed fix.** Require a published legality and impact review before deployment. Add an independent algorithmic audit body with powers to demand documents, publish tribunal rulings in redacted form so that patterns become visible, and set a rule that the system stops automatically when tribunals keep overturning its decisions.

**Why A is the deep dive.** It connects most directly to human trafficking and forced labour. Its failures are documented by mass-casualty events and large audit datasets. And there is a working alternative with published results that can be measured against it.

## Part 2. Deep dive: supply-chain social audits and forced labour

### 2.1 Scale of the problem

From the ILO, Walk Free and IOM *Global Estimates of Modern Slavery: Forced Labour and Forced Marriage* (2022), which gives estimates for 2021:

| Forced labour imposed by private agents | **86%** (63% forced labour exploitation, 23% forced commercial sexual exploitation; 14% state-imposed) | Report, Part 1 |
| Forced labour exploitation (private, excluding commercial sex) | **17.3 million** people, including 1.3 million children | Report, Part 1 |

**The money.** The ILO's *Profits and Poverty: The Economics of Forced Labour* (2024) estimates illegal profits from forced labour in the private economy at **US$236 billion a year**, "almost US$10,000 profit per victim". That is a rise of **US$64 billion (37%)** since the 2014 estimate.

Taken together, these two reports show that forced labour sits mainly in the private economy, mainly in the lower supply-chain tiers, and is sustained by coercion through wages. Wage coercion is easy to hide in paperwork and hard to see on a one-day visit.

### 2.2 Why current audits fail

**1. Certified factories have killed their workers.**
- *Ali Enterprises, Karachi, Pakistan (September 2012).* According to the Clean Clothes Campaign's 2019 report *Fig Leaf for Fashion*, the factory was awarded SA8000 certification by RINA on 20 August 2012, "only three weeks before the deadly Ali Enterprises fire" in which "over 250 workers died, unable to escape due to bars on exits and windows". The audit itself was performed by a local subcontracted firm. The report says the audit document "mentions one unit instead of three" and fails to identify a dangerous wooden mezzanine. It also says the assessment "was made by auditors who reportedly had never even visited the building". That last point is a claim the report attributes to other sources, and this study has not verified it independently.
- *Rana Plaza, Savar, Bangladesh (24 April 2013).* The building housed five garment factories. The ILO states that its collapse "killed at least 1,132 people and injured more than 2,500". Other ILO pages give 1,134 to 1,136, and the Clean Clothes Campaign uses 1,134. The CCC report states that amfori BSCI audits by TÜV Rheinland had been carried out at a factory in the building, and that the auditor had described the building as being of "good construction quality".

**2. Audits look in the wrong places.** LeBaron and Lister (2015), drawing on original interviews, find that "retailers are, in fact, auditing only small portions of supply chains, omitting the portions of supply chains where labour and environmental abuse are most likely to take place". They argue that growing trust in audit metrics "ends up concealing real problems". This matches the ILO finding that most forced labour in manufacturing sits in the lower tiers.

**3. Who pays, and who returns, changes what gets found.** Short, Toffel and Hugill analysed **nearly 17,000 audits at nearly 6,000 suppliers**. Auditors reported *fewer* violations when the individual auditor had audited the factory before, when audit teams were less experienced or less trained, when teams were all male, and "when the audits were paid for by the supplier instead of by the buyer". These are textbook conflict-of-interest and capture effects.

**4. Monitoring alone does not fix conditions.** Locke, Qin and Brause studied audits of **more than 800 Nike suppliers in 51 countries (1998–2005)**. They found that "monitoring alone appears to have produced only limited results". Conditions improved considerably only when monitoring was combined with changes to root causes such as production scheduling.

**5. Fraud and coached workers.** The CCC report lists documented forms of audit fraud: "factory managers bribing inspectors; coaching workers on what to say to auditors... producing fake records detailing incorrect wages and working hours; and even installing safety equipment solely for the duration of the audit". It quotes the Fair Labor Association's 2010 annual report: "fake records on wages were found at 40% of suppliers". That 40% figure is quoted second-hand through the CCC; the FLA's original report was not checked. CCC also notes that most audits it reviewed lacked off-site interviews, even though workers "cannot speak openly when on factory grounds".

**6. The system is legitimised rather than tested.** LeBaron, Lister and Dauvergne (2017) note that laws such as the California Transparency in Supply Chains Act (2012) and the UK Modern Slavery Act (2015) have "enhanced the legitimacy of auditing", despite "two decades of evidence that audit programs generally fail to detect or correct labor and environmental problems". LeBaron (2021) reports "serious gaps between corporate social responsibility standards and business practices when it comes to indicators most relevant to forced labor".

**7. Context matters as much as the audit.** Toffel, Short and Ouellet (2015) find that suppliers adhere to codes more often in countries that participate actively in the ILO treaty regime and have stringent labour law and high press freedom. An audit cannot substitute for those conditions.

### 2.3 Proposed audit design: a worker-driven accountability system

This design is modelled on **worker-driven social responsibility (WSR)**, whose first and best-documented example is the **Fair Food Program (FFP)** in US agriculture. Angelini and Curphey (2022) describe the model's core elements:

1. "a code of conduct drafted by workers";
2. "on-site, worker-to-worker education on worker rights";
3. "a 24-hour, multilingual complaint hotline backed by investigators with the power to address allegations";
4. "annual audits that include interviews with no less than 50 percent of workers";
5. "legally binding agreements with corporate buyers creating market consequences for violations, along with a buyer-paid premium" that funds worker bonuses and helps cover suppliers' costs.

The same independent monitor handles both complaints and audits, which gives it a continuous feedback loop. Mieres and McGrath (2021), writing in the ILO's own journal, show that the FFP model adapted to a new group of migrant guest workers, which is directly relevant to the elevated forced-labour risk that migrants face.

**This study proposes generalising the model as eight design rules for any high-risk supply chain:**

| # | Rule | Failure it targets |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The monitor is independent and is never paid by the audited site.** Fees are pooled from buyers through a premium. | Supplier-paid bias (Short et al.) |
| 2 | **Audits are unannounced by default, and auditors rotate** so that the same auditor does not keep returning. Teams are mixed-gender and trained in forced-labour indicators. | Repeat-auditor and all-male team effects; staged compliance |
| 3 | **Worker voice runs all year, not on audit day.** A confidential, multilingual, 24-hour complaint line has investigators who can act, and off-site interviews are the norm. | Coached answers; the one-day snapshot |
| 4 | **Interview a large share of workers**, using the FFP benchmark of at least 50%, and include agency and subcontracted workers. | Sampling only managers' chosen workers |
| 5 | **Map and audit across tiers.** Buyers must disclose supplier lists down to raw-material stages for high-risk inputs, and the monitor samples from the bottom tiers, not only the first. | Auditing "small portions" (LeBaron and Lister); lower-tier concentration (ILO) |
| 6 | **Follow the wages.** Verify actual payments against bank or mobile-money records, check recruitment-fee and debt records, and check that identity documents are not being withheld. | Wage withholding as the main coercion (ILO); fake wage records |
| 7 | **Binding consequences.** Contracts require buyers to suspend suppliers that fail to remediate, and buyers keep paying the premium so that remediation is affordable. | Monitoring without remediation (Locke et al.) |
| 8 | **Publish.** Release supplier lists, programme-level statistics on complaints, resolution times and compliance trends, and anonymised summaries of findings. | Certification used as a shield; no external check |

**Fit with new law.** Public import controls are moving the same way. The EU Forced Labour Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2024/3015) prohibits products made with forced labour from being placed on or exported from the EU market, applying to all products, sectors and operators from **14 December 2027**. In the United States, Customs and Border Protection enforces a *rebuttable presumption* under the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act that goods made wholly or partly in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, or by entities on the UFLPA Entity List, are barred from import. Under regimes like these, a buyer needs evidence that stands up to scrutiny, which a certificate alone does not provide. The design above produces that evidence.

### 2.4 Ethical risks of this design, and how to reduce them

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Retaliation against workers who complain** | The model depends on workers speaking; the most vulnerable (migrants, debtors) have the most to lose | Anonymous intake; investigation by a monitor independent of the employer; retaliation treated as a zero-tolerance violation with market consequences; publishing aggregate rather than per-site complaint data where small workforces would expose who complained (Angelini and Curphey note this tension for small dairy farms) |
| **Surveillance creep** | Hotlines, wage-record checks and traceability systems can turn into worker monitoring | Collect the minimum data needed; keep worker identities with the monitor only; never share them with employers or state immigration authorities; independent data-protection review; deletion schedules |
| **Privacy of wage and identity data** | Payment records and documents are sensitive | Encrypt data; restrict access by role; let workers see and correct their own records |
| **Certification becoming a shield again** | Any badge can be used to deflect liability, as the cases in 2.2 show | Badges expire; findings are published; the burden stays on the buyer; status is revoked publicly when a violation goes unremedied |
| **Costs pushed onto suppliers** | If buyers demand compliance but squeeze prices, suppliers cut corners, and lower tiers bear the cost | Buyer-paid premium; responsible purchasing terms (lead times, prices) written into the binding agreement |
| **Cut-and-run harming workers** | A buyer dropping a supplier can leave workers jobless and still in debt | Require remediation, including repayment of recruitment fees, before any exit; staged consequences |
| **Displacement** | Abuse can move to uncovered suppliers or sectors | Track tiers and supplier lists; extend coverage by sector rather than by individual site |
| **Worker capture or tokenism** | "Worker voice" can be staged by management | Worker organisations, not employers, choose worker representatives; the code is drafted by workers |

### 2.5 How to measure whether it works

Use outcome measures, not counts of activity. Angelini and Curphey warn that "a numerical reduction in grievances... could mean workers have fewer complaints or could be due to a lack of access to, or trust in" the mechanism.

1. **Forced-labour indicators at the worker level**, measured by independent off-site surveys before and after: wage withholding, recruitment debt, withheld documents, restrictions on movement, threats. These are aligned with the ILO indicator categories used in the Global Estimates.
2. **Detection quality.** Compare violation rates found by unannounced, independent audits with rates from legacy audits at matched sites. A *higher* detection rate in the first years is a sign of success.
3. **Complaint-system health.** Track call volume, the share of complaints raised first with employers, time to resolution (the FFP reported 51% of cases resolved within 13 days), and confirmed retaliation cases, which should be zero and must be reported.
4. **Remediation.** Track the share of violations fixed within a deadline, recruitment fees repaid, and back wages recovered.
5. **Coverage.** Track the share of spend and of workers covered down to the raw-material tier.
6. **Displacement checks.** Track indicator trends at non-participating suppliers in the same region and sector.
7. **Evaluation design.** Roll the system out in stages so that later-joining suppliers serve as a comparison group (difference-in-differences). Pre-register outcomes, publish the data in anonymised form, and commission external evaluation.

### 2.6 Bottom line

The evidence does not say auditing is useless. It says auditing that is announced, paid for by the supplier, limited to the first tier and controlled by the buyer is structurally unable to find forced labour, and that its certificates have given false comfort. Accountability works better when the people at risk define the standard, can report safely all year, and are backed by an independent monitor and binding commercial consequences, with results that outsiders can check.
