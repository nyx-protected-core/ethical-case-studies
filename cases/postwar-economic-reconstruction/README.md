# Rebuilding Economies After War: What Makes Peace Last

*An ethical case study. Version 1.0, November 2026. Desk research only; no fieldwork.*

## Summary

Wars rarely end cleanly. Most civil wars today are repeats of earlier ones, so the economic choices made in the first decade after a ceasefire decide whether peace holds. This study reviews the evidence on relapse risk, jobs, institutions and the timing of aid. It then examines four cases: the Marshall Plan in post-1945 Western Europe, Rwanda after the 1994 genocide, Bosnia and Herzegovina after the 1995 Dayton agreement, and Colombia after the 2016 peace accord. For each it sets out what was done, measured outcomes, what worked and failed, and what is still argued over. It closes with cross-case lessons, the ethical risks of reconstruction, and the link between post-conflict disorder and human trafficking.

Main findings, each sourced below:

- Relapse is the central risk. Nearly half of all civil wars are post-conflict relapses [1], and 90% of civil wars in the 2000s occurred in countries that had already had one in the previous 30 years [3].
- Institutions that constrain elites are the strongest predictor of whether a country escapes the conflict trap [2]. Building them takes 15 to 30 years even in the fastest cases [3].
- Aid works best when it rises after the first few years, not when it peaks at the ceasefire and then tapers [4].
- Fast physical rebuilding is achievable, as Bosnia shows, but does not by itself produce jobs, growth or a functioning state [14].
- Post-conflict settings create real trafficking risk. Bosnia became a destination for women and girls trafficked into forced prostitution after Dayton [15], and conflict and displacement are named by the UN as drivers of trafficking vulnerability [22].

## Part 1. What the evidence says about lasting peace

### 1.1 The conflict trap and relapse risk

- Post-conflict peace is fragile. Collier, Hoeffler and Söderbom find that nearly half of all civil wars are due to post-conflict relapses [1].
- In their estimates, economic development substantially reduces the risk of renewed war, but it takes a long time. UN peacekeeping spending also reduces risk: doubling expenditure lowers the risk of renewed war from 40% to 31% [1].
- They find no systematic effect of post-conflict elections on war risk, and recommend promoting elections as intrinsically desirable rather than as a tool for durable peace [1].
- The World Bank's 2011 World Development Report found that 90% of the previous decade's civil wars occurred in countries that had already had a civil war in the last 30 years [3]. More than 1.5 billion people, one in four worldwide, lived in fragile or conflict-affected states or in countries with very high criminal violence [3].

### 1.2 Institutions

- Walter shows that most contemporary civil wars are recurrences, and that strong political and legal institutions, which check executive power and let elites credibly commit to reform, are the primary determinants of whether countries fall back into war [2].
- The 2011 World Development Report argues that institutional legitimacy is the key to stability, and that even the fastest-transforming countries took between 15 and 30 years to move from fragile to functioning institutions [3].

### 1.3 Jobs and reintegration

- In surveys reviewed for the 2011 World Development Report, unemployment and idleness were cited as the primary reasons young people join rebel movements or gangs [3].
- The report names investment in citizen security, justice and jobs as essential to reducing violence [3].
- In Colombia, ex-combatants living in more participatory communities found social reintegration easier and felt less need to organise among themselves [20].

### 1.4 Aid timing and absorption

- Across 27 countries in their first post-conflict decade during the 1990s, Collier and Hoeffler find that absorptive capacity for aid is no greater than normal in the first three years after conflict, but roughly double its normal level for the rest of the decade [4].
- Historically, aid was not on average higher in post-conflict countries and tended to taper off over the decade, the opposite of what the absorption evidence suggests [4].
- Growth was more sensitive to policy in post-conflict societies, and social policies mattered more relative to macroeconomic policies than in other settings [4].

## Part 2. Four cases

### 2.1 The Marshall Plan (Western Europe, 1948 to 1951)

**What was done.**
- The United States transferred about US$13 billion to war-torn Western European countries from 1948 to 1951, roughly US$115 billion in 2010 prices [5][6].
- The transfer was about 2% of US GDP and roughly the same share of the recipients' combined GDP [6].
- Aid came with conditions that pushed European economies towards more market and fewer controls [5].
- A follow-on US Technical Assistance and Productivity Program (1952 to 1958) sent Italian managers on training trips to US firms and supplied advanced machinery [7].

**Measured outcomes.**
- Industrial production in recipient countries rose from 87% of pre-war levels in 1947 to 135% in 1951, a 55% increase in four years, and growth was sustained in the decades that followed [6].
- Italian firms whose managers were trained in the US outperformed comparable excluded firms for at least fifteen years; gains from new machines alone flattened out over time, and management and machines were complementary [7].

**What worked.** Conditions that changed economic policy and the transfer of management know-how appear to have mattered more than the money itself [5][7].

**What did not, and what is contested.**
- De Long and Eichengreen argue the Plan was not large enough to have significantly accelerated recovery by financing investment, rebuilding infrastructure or easing bottlenecks; its main role was setting the policy stage for growth [5].
- The Plan is invoked whenever large aid programmes are proposed elsewhere, but it was a transfer to industrialised countries with existing institutions and skilled workforces recovering from an interstate war [6]. How far it transfers to low-income countries after civil war is a contested analogy, not an established result.

### 2.2 Rwanda (after 1994)

**Context.** More than one million people are estimated to have been killed in the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi, and an estimated 150,000 to 250,000 women were raped [8]. GDP fell by 50.2% in 1994 and life expectancy at birth dropped to 12.2 years that year [9].

**What was done.** A centralised, party-led development strategy under the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF). Booth and Golooba-Mutebi describe RPF-linked holding companies, a military investment company and a public-private consortium that centrally generated and managed economic rents in a way they compare to developmental regimes in Asia and Africa; with qualifications they call Rwanda a "developmental patrimonial state" [13]. Aid was large: net official development assistance was 94.9% of gross national income in 1994 and between 11.5% and 21.0% each year from 1997 to 2019 [9].

**Measured outcomes.**
- Real GDP growth averaged about 7.7% a year from 2000 to 2019 (World Bank data, author's average of annual figures) [9].
- GDP per capita in constant 2015 dollars rose from US$320 in 1993 to US$1,043 in 2024 [9].
- Life expectancy at birth rose from 47.8 years in 2000 to 67.8 years in 2023 [9].
- The share of people living on less than US$3.00 a day (2021 PPP) fell from 82.3% in 2000 to 63.8% in 2016 and 38.6% in 2023 [10].
- Undernutrition fell from 56% of the population in 1997 to 40% in 2005 [3].
- Aid dependence declined: net aid was 8.0% of gross national income by 2022 [9].

**What worked.** Sustained growth, health gains and falling aid dependence over three decades.

**What did not, and what is contested.**
- **Authoritarian stability.** Reyntjens describes Rwanda as a "donor darling" despite being a dictatorship with a dismal human rights record; he argues donors focused on technocratic governance while ignoring deeply flawed political governance, allowing structural violence that exposes the country to the risk of renewed violence [12].
- **Measurement.** Official figures reported a poverty decline between 2011 and 2014. Okito summarises a published debate and argues that all consistent trend estimates show a sharp increase in poverty since 2011, and that the national statistics office's findings do not match its own stated inflation assumptions [11]. The international poverty series above should be read with this dispute in mind.
- Whether Rwanda's model can be separated from its political controls, or copied elsewhere, is unresolved.

### 2.3 Bosnia and Herzegovina (after Dayton, 1995)

**Context.** As many as 250,000 people were killed and over 2 million fled the country or were internally displaced. Over two-thirds of homes were damaged, and by 1994 GDP had fallen to less than 20% of its pre-war level [14].

**What was done.**
- The Dayton agreement created a weak central state with two ethnically based entities that kept political, military and economic authority, plus an international High Representative and a strong international military and police presence [14].
- The World Bank, the European Union and the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development estimated reconstruction needs at US$5.1 billion, and roughly that amount was pledged over 1996 to 1999 by 48 donor countries and 14 international organisations [14].
- The World Bank alone committed US$983 million from 1996 to 2003 [14].

**Measured outcomes.**
- By 2000 most housing, schools, water systems, roads, telecommunications and power had been rebuilt to near pre-war standards [14].
- Measles immunisation of young children rose from 53% to 96% between 1995 and 2007 [3].
- But by 1999 GDP had recovered to only about 60% of pre-war output, and growth slowed to an average of 4.7% in 2000 to 2003 once aid-driven reconstruction ended [14].
- Unemployment stayed high. Official rates were over 40% but known to be overstated; observers placed true unemployment at 20% to 25%, with little sign of decline at the time [14]. ILO modelled estimates put total unemployment at 27.7% in 2015, falling to 10.7% in 2024, and youth unemployment at 65.2% in 2015, falling to 27.3% in 2024 [9].
- Population fell from about 4.46 million in 1991 to 3.16 million in 2024 [9].

**What worked.** Speed and coordination of physical reconstruction, which the World Bank's evaluators judged highly successful [14]. No return to war.

**What did not, and what is contested.**
- The power-sharing structure that ended the war was, in the evaluators' words, a necessary political compromise that created difficult challenges: multiple layers of government, ten cantons in one entity, many parties, and frequent refusal by leaders to cooperate on common issues [14].
- Reconstruction rebuilt buildings faster than it rebuilt an economy or a functioning state [14].
- Trafficking grew in the post-war vacuum (see Part 4).

### 2.4 Colombia (after the 2016 accord)

**What was done.** The Final Agreement between the government and the FARC-EP guerrilla includes chapters on integral rural reform, illicit drugs and verification [16], and provisions on reintegration of former combatants, transitional justice, victims and ethnic communities [17]. It set a goal of distributing 3 million hectares of land and formalising 7 million hectares [17], with a 15-year implementation plan [16].

**Measured outcomes.**
- **Implementation.** The Kroc Institute, which monitors all 578 provisions, found that at the end of the seventh year (November 2023) 32% were complete, 19% intermediate, 39% minimal and 10% not started; it saw no evidence that the remaining 49% could feasibly be completed by the deadline, and rural reform remained one of the least implemented points [16].
- **Reintegration.** By mid-2025, 95% of the 11,166 former combatants active in the reintegration process had completed individual reintegration plans; over 85% of plans included food security and monthly stipends, which the UN notes shows continued reliance on government support [17].
- **Security of ex-combatants.** 470 former combatants had been killed since the signing of the agreement, plus 162 attempted homicides and 54 disappearances [17].
- **Land.** About 217,151 hectares had been adjudicated by May 2025, close to 7% of the 3-million-hectare goal; about 3.3 million hectares had been formalised, 47% of the 7-million-hectare target. Judges had ordered restitution of 815,474 hectares to people dispossessed during the conflict, of which 392,602 had been compensated or handed over [17].
- **Victims.** The national victims' registry listed 10,279,191 victims of the armed conflict by the end of 2025 [18].
- **Displacement continues.** Colombia had 7.3 million people living in internal displacement due to conflict and violence at the end of 2024, among the highest figures in the world [19]. Between January and June 2025, 67,800 people suffered mass forced displacement, a 107% increase on the same period of 2024 [17].

**What worked.** The FARC-EP disarmed and most former combatants stayed in the reintegration programme [17]. Participatory receiving communities helped social reintegration [20].

**What did not, and what is contested.**
- Slow delivery of rural reform and land, the economic heart of the accord [16][17].
- Other armed groups moved into areas the FARC-EP left. The UN reports clashes between several armed groups and continuing displacement [17].
- Peace had unintended economic effects: areas previously controlled by the FARC saw a differential increase in deforestation after the ceasefire, attenuated where state presence and judicial capacity were stronger [21].
- How far implementation should be sequenced or prioritised, and whether it can be completed by 2031, is actively debated [16].

## Part 3. Comparison and lessons

| | Marshall Plan | Rwanda | Bosnia and Herzegovina | Colombia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Type of war | Interstate, industrial economies | Genocide and civil war | Ethnic civil war | Long insurgency, partial peace |
| Aid scale | About 2% of recipients' GDP [6] | Net aid 94.9% of GNI in 1994 [9] | US$5.1bn pledged 1996-99 [14] | Not quantified here; UN verification mission in place [17] |
| Headline outcome | Industrial output +55% in 4 years [6] | GDP per capita about 3x, 1993-2024 [9] | Infrastructure rebuilt by 2000; GDP ~60% of pre-war in 1999 [14] | 32% of provisions complete after 7 years [16] |
| Stability | Sustained growth [6] | Stable regime; critics warn of renewed-violence risk [12] | No return to war; fragmented state [14] | FARC-EP disarmed; other armed groups active [17] |
| Main weakness | Size alone did not drive recovery [5] | Authoritarian rule, disputed data [11][12] | Fragmented institutions, jobs [14] | Land, security of ex-combatants [17] |

**Lessons.**

1. **Policy and know-how beat volume.** The Marshall Plan's lasting effects came from policy conditions and management transfer, not the size of the cheque [5][7].
2. **Time aid to absorption.** Aid should phase in over the first decade rather than peak at the ceasefire [4]. Bosnia's front-loaded reconstruction rebuilt infrastructure quickly but growth slowed when aid fell [14].
3. **Buildings are not institutions.** Physical reconstruction can be done in four years; institutions that constrain elites take 15 to 30 years [3][14], and they are what prevents relapse [2].
4. **Jobs are a security policy.** Unemployment is a stated reason for joining armed groups [3]; Bosnia's long unemployment [9][14] and Colombia's reliance on stipends [17] show how hard this is.
5. **Peace deals that leave land unresolved stay fragile.** Land disputes are named by the UN as a root cause of Colombia's conflict, and land delivery lags most [17].
6. **Security of the people who disarm matters.** The killing of 470 former combatants in Colombia [17] is a direct threat to the credibility of any future deal.
7. **External security plus slow economic recovery.** The cross-country evidence suggests peace depends on an external security presence sustaining a gradual economic recovery, with elections playing a smaller role than often assumed [1].

## Part 4. Ethical risks and the trafficking link

### 4.1 Ethical risks

1. **Aid capture.** Large inflows into weak institutions can be diverted to those who control access. The Bosnian evaluators record tax evasion and corruption remaining serious problems alongside reconstruction success [14].
2. **Elite bargains.** Power-sharing that ends fighting can lock in the leaders of the war. Bosnia's Dayton structure stopped the war but produced layered, uncooperative government [14].
3. **Donor dependence.** Very high aid shares, such as Rwanda's in the 1990s [9] or Colombia's stipend reliance [17], can weaken domestic accountability if they do not taper as capacity grows.
4. **Authoritarian stability trade-offs.** Rwanda's growth came with tight political control [12][13]. Donors face a real dilemma between supporting results and tolerating repression that may itself store up future violence [12].
5. **Trusting the numbers.** When governments control statistics, outside observers may reward progress that cannot be independently verified [11].
6. **Land and victims' reparations.** Colombia registered over 10 million victims [18] and court-ordered restitution remains only partly delivered [17]. Promising reparations that cannot be paid risks a second betrayal; underfunding them risks rewarding those who seized land by force.
7. **Unintended harms of peace.** Ceasefires can open territory to extraction, as with deforestation in former FARC areas [21], or to new armed groups [17].

### 4.2 The trafficking link

**Bosnia and Herzegovina after Dayton.** Human Rights Watch documented that trafficking of women and girls for forced prostitution began to appear in 1995 [15]. Its 2002 report found:
- As of October 2002, the UN mission suspected 227 nightclubs and bars of involvement in trafficking [15].
- In 2001, UN trafficking experts estimated about 25% of women and girls working in nightclubs and bars were trafficked [15].
- The International Organization for Migration helped repatriate 498 trafficking victims between August 1999 and October 2002, some as young as thirteen [15].
- Victims, mostly from Moldova, Romania and Ukraine, were held in debt bondage and had their passports taken [15].
- Victims reported about 70% of clients were local and 30% international, and local organisations believed the presence of thousands of foreign civilians and soldiers was a significant motivating factor for traffickers. The report also documents local police complicity and involvement by members of international missions and contractors [15].

This is the clearest case in this study of a post-war economy, and the international presence meant to secure it, creating a market for trafficking.

**Displacement and trafficking risk.** The UN Office on Drugs and Crime states that as conflicts, climate disasters and displacement converge, some people are pushed directly into trafficking and others are left without homes and prospects at huge risk of it [22]. Detected victims worldwide rose 25% in 2022 compared with 2019, and women and girls were 61% of detected victims [22]. Internal displacement from conflict and violence reached 73.5 million people at the end of 2024 [19].

**Colombia.** Colombia's 7.3 million internally displaced people [19] and its continuing armed-group violence [17] fit the risk pattern UNODC describes [22]. This study found no verified source quantifying trafficking specifically linked to the post-accord period, so it makes no numerical claim.

**Implications for reconstruction.**
- Build anti-trafficking safeguards into peace operations from day one, including enforceable codes of conduct and an end to the legal immunity and impunity that Human Rights Watch found shielded international staff and contractors involved in trafficking [15].
- Treat displaced people's livelihoods, documents and land as anti-trafficking measures, not only humanitarian ones [19][22].
- Protect victims rather than prosecute or deport them, a failure Human Rights Watch recorded in Bosnia [15].

## Part 5. Open questions

1. Can the Marshall Plan's lessons on policy conditions and know-how be applied to low-income countries after civil war without becoming coercive?
2. How should donors weigh strong development results against authoritarian rule, as in Rwanda, when the human rights costs may raise long-run relapse risk [12]?
3. What reliable, independent measurement systems can verify progress where governments dispute critics' figures [11]?
4. How can power-sharing deals include sunset clauses so that wartime bargains do not freeze institutions for decades?
5. What share of Colombia's land and reparations promises can realistically be funded, and how should shortfalls be prioritised among more than 10 million registered victims [18]?
6. How much post-conflict trafficking is driven by international presences themselves, and how can missions be held accountable?
