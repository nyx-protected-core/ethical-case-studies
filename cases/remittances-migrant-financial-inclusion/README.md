# Remittances and Migrant Financial Inclusion: An Ethical Case Study

*Every DOI below was checked in Crossref and every link was opened on 18 November 2026. Figures come from the documents cited. Where sources measure different things (years, country groups, transfer sizes), the text says so. Advocacy and industry sources are labelled. "[PR]" marks peer-reviewed journal articles.*

*Revisions (18 November 2026).* Revised after an independent review. The changes correct facts, fill gaps and tighten wording. The review is not named here.

---

## Summary

Migrant workers send home more money than all official aid combined. They do it through a payments system that still charges the poorest senders the most, that loses banking access whenever banks judge them unprofitable or risky, and that often depends on migration routes where workers start in debt to recruiters. The ethical questions here are about who carries the cost of moving small sums across borders: the migrant in fees and exchange-rate margins, the recipient family when channels close, or the public when illicit money slips through. They are also about whether "financial inclusion" through mobile money and digital credit helps or harms the households it reaches.

The case is set out in seven parts:

1. Costs measured against SDG target 10.c, and the size of flows.
2. Three corridors: the Gulf to South Asia, Russia to Central Asia, and the United States to Mexico and Central America.
3. Mobile money and digital credit, including the dispute over the best-known evidence.
4. Recruitment debt and debt bondage.
5. De-risking by banks.
6. The strongest counterarguments.
7. Proposals, limitations and open questions.

## 1. Costs against SDG 10.c, and the size of flows

### 1.1 The target

SDG target 10.c reads: "By 2030, reduce to less than 3 per cent the transaction costs of migrant remittances and eliminate remittance corridors with costs higher than 5 per cent" [3]. The G8 and G20 had made earlier commitments: L'Aquila (2010), and Cannes (2011) and Brisbane (2014). These "committed to reduce the Global Average Total Cost to 5 percent". The G20 later aligned with SDG 10.c and reaffirmed it in its Roadmap for Enhancing Cross-Border Payments [1].

### 1.2 Where costs stand

The latest World Bank *Remittance Prices Worldwide* (RPW) report I could open and verify is the Q3 2025 issue (data published in September 2025) [1]. RPW measures the total cost of sending the equivalent of US$200, meaning the fee plus the exchange-rate margin, unless stated otherwise.

| Measure (Q3 2025, $200 unless stated) | Cost |
|---|---|
| Global Average | **6.36%** (down from 6.49% in Q1 2025; 9.67% in Q1 2009) |
| Global weighted average | 5.04% |
| International MTO Index | 5.52% |
| Global SmaRT average (cheapest accessible options) | 3.29% |
| Digital services / non-digital services | 4.59% / 7.30% |
| Digital-only MTOs | 3.54% |
| Banks | 14.99% |
| Global Average for $500 | 4.08% |
| Sub-Saharan Africa (costliest region) | 8.46% |
| Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan & Pakistan (cheapest region) | 5.11% |
| South Asia / Latin America & Caribbean | 5.30% / 5.64% |
| Share of corridors below 5% | 83% (17% in Q1 2009) |

Source: [1]. MTO = money transfer operator.

Three things follow.

- **The 3% goal is far off.** The simple global average is more than twice the target. Even the weighted average, which reflects where most money actually goes, is about 5%.
- **The cheapest channels already beat the target, but not everyone can use them.** The SmaRT average (3.29%) and digital-only operators (3.54%) are close to 3%. The gap is about access: who holds a bank account or a smartphone, and who has the documents to open one.
- **Bank-sent remittances are still the most expensive, at nearly 15%.** People who are already "included" in banking are not necessarily getting a good price.

I could not verify any RPW issue later than Q3 2025. Secondary websites quote figures of about 6.0–6.2% for Q1 2026. I have not used them (see section 7.3).

### 1.3 The size of flows, and comparison with aid and investment

The figures below come from three institutions with different bases and years. They should not be added or compared directly with each other.

| Source | Year | Remittances to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) | Comparison given by source |
|---|---|---|---|
| World Bank/KNOMAD Migration and Development Brief 40 [4] | 2023 | $656bn (+0.7%) | "surpassing foreign direct investment (FDI) and official development assistance (ODA)" |
| UN 2026 HLPF SDG 17 factsheet [6] | 2024 | $687bn (up nearly 10%) | DAC ODA in 2025: $174.3bn, a 23.1% real-terms fall; FDI to developing economies: about $877bn (−2%) |
| IFAD, *Sending Money Home 2026* [5] | 2025 | $728.6bn | "more than four times global official development assistance that year and greater than foreign direct investment to those countries" |

Other figures from the same sources:

- **Brief 40** [4]:
  - The largest recipients in 2023 were India ($120bn), Mexico ($66bn), China ($50bn), the Philippines ($39bn) and Pakistan ($27bn).
  - Remittances were 41% of GDP in Tonga and 39% in Tajikistan.
  - The international migrant stock was estimated at 302 million in 2023.
- **Discontinued series.** The World Bank says the KNOMAD partnership was active from 2013 to 2024 and produced 40 Migration and Development Briefs [4]. Brief 40 (June 2024) is therefore the last in the series. Later figures, such as the $687bn for 2024 reported in the UN factsheet [6], come from other publications.
- **IDA countries.** For the poorest (IDA-eligible) countries, a World Bank data blog reports that remittances rose from about $40bn in 2005 to $189bn in 2024. FDI inflows to the same countries were $66.3bn in 2024 [7]. In 2024 India received $138bn and Mexico $68bn [7].
- **IFAD** [5]:
  - 220 million migrants support 1.1 billion relatives.
  - About $233bn reached rural areas.
  - Remittances exceed 10% of GDP in 23 countries.
  - Only 35% of the services it measured in 2025 were fully digital at both ends.
  - IFAD adds that "these private flows cannot replace public investment, social protection or climate finance."

**Ethical reading.** Large totals can mislead in two ways.

- **Remittances are private money.** They are not a development budget. They are wages, sent by people who often work long hours under poor conditions (section 4). Counting them next to ODA as "development finance" risks treating migrants' sacrifice as a substitute for public obligations. The sharp fall in ODA in 2025 sharpens that risk.
- **The measured growth is partly a statistical artefact.** Clemens and McKenzie argue that "a large majority of the recent rise in measured remittances may be illusory -- arising from changes in measurement, not changes in real financial flows". They also argue that the effects on growth are hard to detect because migration has an opportunity cost at home, although "Migration and remittances clearly have first-order effects on poverty at the origin" [8].

The household-level evidence is stronger than the macro evidence:

- **Philippines.** When migrants' host-country currencies rose against the peso after the 1997 crisis, remittances increased. Children's schooling rose and child labour fell [9].
- **El Salvador.** A field experiment offering matching funds for education remittances found that "for each $1 received by beneficiaries, educational expenditures increase by $3.72" [10].

So cost and channel design matter in practice. Each percentage point of fees comes out of household budgets with measurable effects.

## 2. Corridors

The corridor figures below come from the RPW corridor pages (data collected in August–September 2025, "Total Average Third Quarter 2025") [2]. The "$200" and "$500" columns are the RPW's two standard amounts. For Gulf senders these are local-currency equivalents, for example AED 735 and SAR 750. Each corridor covers only the services RPW samples.

### 2.1 Gulf to South Asia

| Corridor | ~$200 | ~$500 | Below 5% cap at $200? |
|---|---|---|---|
| UAE → India | 3.72% | 2.19% | Yes |
| Saudi Arabia → India | 5.59% | 3.07% | No |
| UAE → Pakistan | 2.17% | 1.53% | Yes |
| Saudi Arabia → Pakistan | 5.02% | 2.82% | No (just above) |
| UAE → Bangladesh | 7.72% | 6.53% | No |
| Saudi Arabia → Bangladesh | 10.20% | 7.28% | No |
| Qatar → Nepal | 4.00% | 2.19% | Yes |

Source: [2].

At the country level, the Q3 2025 report gives an average of 5.52% for sending from Saudi Arabia and 5.30% for sending to India [1]. Two points stand out.

- **Bangladesh is the costliest.** At 10.20% from Saudi Arabia and 7.72% from the UAE, these corridors cost about 1.8 to 3.6 times as much as the Pakistan and India corridors from the same senders. The cost is also unstable: Brief 40 reports that the cost of sending $200 from the UAE to Bangladesh rose from 5% in Q4 2022 to 13.6% in Q4 2023, an increase of 172% [4]. On the Saudi corridor the exchange-rate margin alone averages 5.30%. This is the hidden part of the price, the part migrants are least able to compare.
- **Informal channels take over when official exchange rates are out of line.** Brief 40 attributes the sharp drop in remittances to Pakistan, and the slowdown in Bangladesh, in 2023 to "the primacy of informal channels of money transfer triggered by exchange-rate-related challenges in the domestic economy" [4]. Bangladesh's formal flows were about $22bn (+3%). Nepal's rose 17% to $10.8bn, with the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states as the main destination [4].

These are also the corridors where recruitment debt is heaviest (section 4). A worker who paid ten months' wages for a job and then loses 7–10% on each transfer is paying twice for the right to support a family.

### 2.2 Russia to Central Asia

- **Dependence** [4]:
  - In 2023 remittances were about 39% of GDP in Tajikistan, 22% in the Kyrgyz Republic and 15% in Uzbekistan. Much of this came from Russia.
  - Flows to Europe and Central Asia rose 18% in 2022. The share of Russian remittances in total flows to these countries surged to record highs that year, then declined. For Uzbekistan, Russia's share went from around half in 2019–21 to 87% in 2022 and 78% in 2023.
  - Flows then fell 10% to $71bn in 2023, "driven by reduced transfers from Russia to the Central Asian countries, exacerbated by a sharp (39 percent) depreciation of the Russian ruble against the US dollar."
- **Sanctions effects.** Brief 40 notes that "financial institutions in some destination countries have restricted transfers from Russia over concerns of additional sanctions", and that "These factors may have diverted remittance flows to informal channels." [4]
- **Measurement gap.** RPW collected no data on Russia-origin corridors in Q1–Q2 2022. Since Q3 2022 it has collected only publicly available online data for them, and it excludes these corridors from its main analysis (Annex V) [1].

Russia-origin cost of sending $200, from RPW Annex V [1]:

| Corridor | Q4 2021 | Q3 2022 | Q3 2024 | Q3 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Russia → Tajikistan | 2.13% | 2.10% | 4.21% | 2.35% (2 services) |
| Russia → Kyrgyz Republic | 0.90% | 4.14% | 3.16% | 5.90% (5 services) |

These are thin samples, and the method changed in the middle of the series. They show that the Kyrgyz corridor rose from one of the cheapest in the world to above the 5% cap. They do not show why: sanctions, fewer competing providers and exchange-rate effects all fit the data. The Tajik figure moved up and back down on only two sampled services.

Sanctions on Russia have legitimate aims. The ethical point is that their side effects fall on Tajik and Kyrgyz households that have no part in the conflict and depend on these transfers for about a fifth to two-fifths of national GDP. Pushing those flows into informal channels also works against the sanctions' own aim of traceability.

### 2.3 United States to Mexico and Central America

| Corridor | ~$200 | ~$500 |
|---|---|---|
| US → Mexico | 4.54% | 2.99% |
| US → Guatemala | 4.19% | 2.76% |
| US → Honduras | 3.99% | 2.64% |
| US → El Salvador | 3.50% | 1.87% |

Source: [2]. El Salvador uses the US dollar, so the exchange-rate margin there is 0.00%.

- **Costs.** All four corridors are under the 5% cap but above the 3% goal at $200. The Q3 2025 averages are 5.04% for sending from the United States and 4.53% for sending to Mexico [1]. Competition and volume make US–Latin America one of the cheaper corridor groups. Mexico received about $66bn in 2023 and $68bn in 2024 [4][7].
- **Growth.** Flows grew 9.7% to Guatemala and 17.8% to Honduras in 2023 [4].
- **The new US excise tax.** Since 1 January 2026, "a 1% remittance transfer tax applies to remittances sent from the United States to recipients in foreign countries when the sender provides cash, a money order, a cashier's check, or other similar physical instrument" [29]. The sender owes the tax and providers must collect it [29].
  - Because the tax falls only on cash-funded transfers, it falls on the senders least likely to hold accounts.
  - It adds a full percentage point to the cost of a cash-funded transfer. That is a third of the whole 3% cost that SDG 10.c allows, and it is borne by the least-banked group.
  - Supporters can say it pushes people toward traceable account-funded channels. Critics can say it taxes the unbanked for being unbanked. Neither effect has been measured yet.

## 3. Mobile money: inclusion, evidence and debt

### 3.1 The headline evidence

Two papers by Jack and Suri are the most cited evidence that mobile money helps poor households.

- **Risk sharing (2014)** [12]. In Jack and Suri's Kenyan panel, M-PESA adoption rose from 43% to 70% of households. They find that "while shocks reduce consumption by 7 percent for nonusers, the consumption of user households is unaffected". The mechanism is that users receive more remittances from a wider range of senders. Lower transaction costs let family networks act as insurance.
- **Poverty and gender (2016)** [11]. Using variation in access to M-PESA agents, Suri and Jack estimate that M-PESA "increased per capita consumption levels and lifted 194,000 households, or 2% of Kenyan households, out of poverty". The effects were "more pronounced for female-headed households". The authors attribute them partly to women moving "out of agriculture and into business".

### 3.2 The critique and the "reply debate"

Bateman, Duvendack and Loubere (2019, *Review of African Political Economy*) contested the 2016 paper directly. Their summary says: "Our analysis shows that their analysis and claims are extremely problematic" [13]. Their main points, as set out in the article and in their companion blog post [13], are:

- **Method.** They question the identification strategy (comparing areas by agent density), sample attrition and selection.
- **Missing costs.** The paper measures gains in income and savings but ignores rising household debt, including M-Shwari digital loans.
- **Displacement.** It does not count displacement and exit among competing micro-enterprises, so new businesses may replace others rather than add to them.
- **Political economy.** It leaves out the wider political economy of a platform majority-owned by a foreign multinational.

The critique appears in a heterodox political-economy journal and comes from authors with a long-standing critique of microcredit. That does not make it wrong, but readers should know where it sits. The 2016 paper appeared in *Science* and is widely cited by development agencies.

**What I could not find:** a published reply by Suri and Jack. Two searches of Crossref and the open web, and the comments on the authors' blog post, turned up no formal rejoinder. The "debate" is therefore one-sided in the published record. That matters: one of the most-cited pieces of financial-inclusion evidence has a published methodological challenge that, as far as I could verify, has never been answered in print.

### 3.3 Digital credit and debt traps

Mobile money platforms now offer instant small loans. The evidence is mixed:

- **Kenya (M-Shwari).** Suri, Bharadwaj and Jack (2021) [14] find that about a third of eligible users take the loan, that it "does not substitute for other credit", and that borrowing households are "6.3 percentage points less likely to forego expenses due to negative shocks".
- **Malawi.** Brailovskaya, Dupas and Robinson [15] (NBER working paper, not peer-reviewed) describe "short-term, high-interest loans" whose "terms are often opaque" in markets where "consumer financial literacy is low, providing opportunities for predatory lending". They find "no negative effect of access to digital loans on financial well-being, but the majority of borrowers fail to repay on time and incur high late fees". A financial-literacy intervention "increased loan demand, increasing overall default risk".

The ethical point is that "inclusion" is not good in itself. Access to a payment rail that lowers the cost of family insurance [12] is one thing. Automatic, high-cost credit offered on the same rail to users with little bargaining power is another. The research shows both the resilience benefit [14] and the late-fee and default exposure [15], so neither the optimists nor the critics can claim the whole picture.

### 3.4 Stablecoins and crypto

I left these out. I did not find peer-reviewed or official evidence specific enough to support claims about their costs or risks for migrant remittance corridors within this case's verification standard.

## 4. Recruitment debt and debt bondage

### 4.1 What migrants pay before they earn

The ILO and the World Bank developed a survey method through KNOMAD to measure worker-paid recruitment costs, now used for SDG indicator 10.7.1. Preliminary results presented by the ILO in 2017 [16]:

| Destination | Origin | Recruitment cost (US$) | Months of destination earnings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saudi Arabia | Pakistan | 4,395 | 10.6 |
| Saudi Arabia | Ethiopia | 991 | 3.6 |
| Kuwait | Bangladesh | 3,136 | 9.0 |
| UAE | Pakistan | 2,351 | 7.2 |
| Kuwait | India | 1,248 | 2.5 |
| Qatar | India | 1,149 | 2.0 |
| Qatar | Nepal | 1,054 | 3.3 |
| Qatar | Philippines | 480 | 1.1 |
| Kuwait | Sri Lanka | 319 | 0.9 |

Source: [16].

- **Conditions on arrival.** The same presentation reported that 24% of migrant workers lost wages because they were paid less than promised or had other wage problems. Weekly hours averaged 71, and 25% reported having no rest day [16].
- **Variation by route.** The spread is wide. Pakistani workers going to Saudi Arabia paid about ten months' wages; Sri Lankans going to Kuwait paid less than one. The spread suggests that policy and market structure, not the work itself, set the price.
- **Nepal, 2023.** Nepal's first nationally representative survey (2023, by the National Statistics Office with the ILO, published in 2026) found that about one-third of migrant workers paid no recruitment fees but nearly two-thirds incurred costs. On average workers needed 3.3 months of earnings to recover them; women needed about four and a half months. Unregistered brokers were the most expensive route [17].

### 4.2 From debt to forced labour

The ILO's 2024 report *Profits and poverty* [18] states: "To pay for the recruitment fees and related costs to secure a job or placement, many workers incur heavy debt, which may lead to situations of debt bondage."

- **Total illegal profits.** Illegal profits from forced labour are estimated at US$236bn a year, up $64bn (37%) since 2014 [18]. This total excludes recruitment fees. Those are estimated separately at a further $5.6bn [18].
- **Migrants' exposure.** Migrant workers are far more exposed than others. The report cites the 2022 global estimates (ILO, Walk Free and IOM): nearly 14 of every 1,000 adult migrant workers are in forced labour, more than three times the rate among non-migrants [18].

The link to remittances is direct. A worker who borrows to pay a recruiter sends early wages home to repay the lender, not to feed the family. The remittance statistics count this as a "flow". Ethically, it is closer to a transfer from the worker to the recruitment chain.

### 4.3 The norms

- **ILO General Principles and Operational Guidelines for Fair Recruitment** [19]. Principle 7: "No recruitment fees or related costs should be charged to, or otherwise borne by, workers or jobseekers." The document also defines recruitment fees and related costs so they cannot be relabelled.
- **Employer Pays Principle** [20] (an industry and NGO initiative, convened by the Institute for Human Rights and Business). It is "an employer commitment to pay the full costs associated with recruitment". It is endorsed by the Leadership Group for Responsible Recruitment and reflects the Dhaka Principles: "no worker should pay for a job". It is a voluntary commitment by companies. It is not law.
- **Kafala reform in Qatar** [21]. The kafala sponsorship system ties a worker's legal status to an employer. The ILO reported in 2020 that:
  - Law No. 19 of 2020 lets workers change jobs without a No Objection Certificate. Together with the earlier removal of exit permits, this "effectively dismantles the 'kafala' sponsorship system".
  - Law No. 17 set a minimum wage of QAR 1,000 plus food and housing allowances (QAR 300 and QAR 500 where not provided). It was expected to affect about 400,000 workers directly.

  I verified the legal change only through ILO sources. I did not verify enforcement in practice, or comparable reforms elsewhere in the GCC, and I make no claims about them.

## 5. De-risking

### 5.1 The pattern

The FATF defines de-risking as "terminating or restricting business relationships with clients or categories of clients to avoid, rather than manage, risk" [26]. In 2014 it said that "What is not in line with the FATF standards is the wholesale cutting loose of entire classes of customer, without taking into account, seriously and comprehensively, their level of risk" [25]. It also said that "It is a misconception to characterise de-risking exclusively as an anti-money laundering issue" [25].

The World Bank's 2015 G20 survey of the remittance market [23] found:

- **Account closures spread.** The share of MTOs reporting no bank account closures fell from 67% in 2010 to 42% in 2014.
- **Lost access.** 28% of MTO principals and 45% of their agents reported they could no longer access banking services.
- **Drivers.** Banks cited profitability, pressure from correspondent banks, fear of regulatory scrutiny, lack of confidence in MTOs' procedures and reputational risk.
- **Not violations.** "Neither banks nor MTOs cited AML/CFT-related violations or sanctions by MTOs as one of the top 5 reasons for account closures."
- **Costs passed to migrants.** Closures had "quite onerous consequences for the migrants and their families, in terms of costs, both direct (increased fees) and indirect (loss of working hours, travel costs, risks related to cash transportation, etc.)".

A companion World Bank fact-finding summary [24] found:

- 75% of large international banks reported a decline in correspondent banking relationships.
- Over 69% of banking authorities said MTOs and other remittance companies were the most affected clients.

### 5.2 Somalia

Somalia shows how this plays out where no alternative exists. A joint briefing by Oxfam, Adeso and the Global Center on Cooperative Security (an **advocacy** source) [22] gives the following picture:

- **Scale.** Somalia receives about $1.3bn a year in remittances. These make up "between 25 and 45 percent of Somalia's economy" and exceed humanitarian aid, development aid and FDI combined. MTOs estimate that over 80% of small-business start-up capital comes from the diaspora.
- **Merchants Bank of California** was "the principal bank facilitating remittances to Somalia". Somali MTOs said it carried 60–80% of US–Somalia remittance volume. It announced in May 2014 that it would close most Somali MTO accounts, and later announced it would close all of them on 6 February 2015.
- **The bank's approach.** The briefing gives the bank credit for its process. Unlike banks that send form letters, it "held extensive consultations" before acting, and the briefing calls this "an alternative to across-the-board de-risking". The closure is therefore not a story of a careless bank, which makes the structural problem clearer: even a bank that engaged in good faith concluded it could not carry the risk.
- **The risk.** The briefing warns that remittances "could decrease in volume and go underground. This would defeat the object of upholding AML/CFT regulations".

These are advocacy claims. The flow and share estimates come from the coalition and the MTOs, not from an independent statistical agency.

### 5.3 What the FATF found by 2021, and the 2025 update

The FATF's 2021 stocktake of unintended consequences [26] concluded that "profitability concerns are the primary driver" of de-risking, "directly impacted, in some situations, by high compliance costs". AML/CFT rules "are not the main cause of de risking, but can be a related factor."

- **Who is hit hardest.** "Smaller and more financially isolated economies, emerging market economies, as well as conflict zones, tend to be impacted more significantly." Somalia fits all three.
- **Higher costs.** De-risking "can result in increased costs of payments and concentration for correspondent banking and remittance services - although the data are unclear on the extent and impact".

In February 2025 the FATF revised Recommendation 1 [30]. It replaced the word "commensurate" with "proportionate" (a measure that "appropriately corresponds to the level of identified risk and effectively mitigates the risks"), required countries to "allow and encourage" simplified measures in lower-risk cases, and asked supervisors to take risk-mitigation measures into account so as to avoid over-compliance. The effect of this change on de-risking has not yet been evaluated.

## 6. Fair counterarguments

A case study that only criticises banks and regulators would be one-sided. The strongest counterarguments are these.

**6.1 The abuse is real.** Remittance channels can be used for fraud, money laundering and terrorist financing, and some large providers have failed badly.

- **The Western Union settlement.** In January 2017 Western Union settled US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission investigations by agreeing to pay $586m. The company's own announcement says the money is "to be used to reimburse consumers who were victims of fraud". The investigations concerned whether its anti-fraud and AML controls "adequately prevented misconduct by those agents and third parties", mainly from 2004 to 2012 [27]. The settlement included a deferred prosecution agreement, a consent order and three years of independent compliance review [27]. (I cite the company's release because the Justice Department's own release did not load during checking; see 7.3.)
- **Fraud victims are often migrants.** The victims of remittance fraud are often the same migrants and families whom inclusion arguments aim to protect. Weak controls are not pro-poor.
- **The FATF's 2014 caveat.** The FATF noted that the enforcement actions that frightened banks involved "extremely egregious cases involving banks who deliberately broke the law, in some cases for more than a decade" [25].

**6.2 Some risk should not be taken.** The World Bank's fact-finding summary says: "A bank is completely justified not doing business because of business rationale, compliance costs or excessive risk. There are risks that should not be taken – and no policy intervention should try to dictate otherwise." [24] The G20 survey report itself acknowledges that "remittance channels can be a source of money laundering and financing of terrorism (ML/FT) risks" [23]. A rule that forces banks to serve every MTO would just move risk onto banks and their supervisors.

**6.3 Consumer protection costs money too.** US federal law (Regulation E, subpart B) gives remittance senders the following rights [28]:

- **Disclosures** of fees, exchange rate and amount received.
- **Cancellation:** "up to 30 minutes to cancel the remittance transfer at no charge".
- **Errors:** "180 days to notify the remittance provider of a mistake".
- **Scope:** the rules apply to electronic transfers "of more than $15".

These protections are a large part of what makes formal channels better than informal ones. Each one is also a fixed compliance cost.

**6.4 Fixed costs push small providers out.** Western Union said that after five years of increases it spends "approximately $200 million per year on compliance, with more than 20 percent of its workforce currently dedicated to compliance functions" [27]. That is a company source, but it shows the scale. A small diaspora MTO serving one corridor cannot spread costs like these. The FATF found that "high compliance costs" feed directly into the profitability judgments that drive de-risking [26].

The fair conclusion is that the trade-off is real. Strong AML/CFT and consumer protection rules favour large providers. Large providers lower the cost in high-volume corridors (UAE–Pakistan, US–El Salvador). They may not serve thin, high-risk corridors such as Somalia at any price. When small providers lose bank access, the risk that the rules aimed to reduce often grows, because money goes underground [22][25][26].

## 7. Proposals, limitations and open questions

### 7.1 Proposals

1. **Publish and enforce corridor-level exchange-rate margins.** In the costliest corridors, such as Saudi Arabia or the UAE to Bangladesh, a large part of the cost is the exchange-rate margin [2]. Host and origin regulators could require disclosure before payment, as US law already does [28], and could publish the RPW corridor tables in the languages migrants read.
   - *Tension:* disclosure helps only migrants who have alternatives. Where one provider dominates, publishing the price does not lower it.
2. **Make "financial inclusion" mean low-cost payments first, credit second.** The evidence for payment rails (risk-sharing [12]) is stronger and less disputed than the evidence for digital credit [14][15]. Regulators could require clear total-cost disclosure and limits on late fees for mobile loans, and could stop counting loan uptake as an inclusion success.
   - *Tension:* resilience gains from credit are real for some borrowers [14]. Strict caps could cut off those borrowers.
3. **Fund the Employer Pays Principle through enforcement, not pledges.** Recruitment fees cost workers up to about ten months' wages in some Gulf corridors [16] and help produce debt bondage [18]. Destination and origin states could adopt ILO Principle 7 [19] in law, with joint liability for employers and licensed recruiters, and could add worker-paid recruitment cost (SDG 10.7.1) to the same monitoring as remittance prices.
   - *Tension:* if fees are banned in one country but not enforced, they move to unregistered brokers, which the Nepal survey already identifies as the most expensive route [17].
4. **Treat MTO bank access as critical infrastructure in high-dependence corridors.** For corridors such as Somalia and Tajikistan, where remittances are a quarter or more of the economy [4][22], supervisors could set up supervised "safe corridor" arrangements. These would give banks regulatory clarity on what due diligence is enough, in line with the FATF's case-by-case standard [25][26].
   - *Tension:* this asks governments to share risk that banks have chosen not to carry. If something goes wrong, the political cost falls on the regulator.
5. **Assess sanctions for their effects on remittances.** Sanctions regimes affecting Russia-origin flows could be reviewed for their effect on third-country households, and could keep clear exemptions for personal remittances, since restricting them appears to push money informal [4].
   - *Tension:* exemptions can be exploited for sanctions evasion.
6. **Review flat taxes on cash remittances.** The US 1% tax on cash-funded transfers [29] falls on the least-banked senders. Its effect on costs and on informal channels should be measured. If it pushes money informal, it works against both the SDG goal and AML aims.

### 7.2 Limitations

- **Different bases.** The World Bank (2023: $656bn; 2024: $687bn as reported by the UN) and IFAD (2025: $728.6bn) use different years and methods. They are reported side by side, not as a series.
- **Fixed transfer size and sampled services.** RPW measures fixed $200 and $500 transfers through sampled services. Actual costs depend on what migrants actually use.
- **Russia-origin data.** The Russia-origin figures use a different method (online data only since Q3 2022) and very few services, so they show direction, not precise levels [1].
- **Preliminary recruitment figures.** The recruitment-cost table [16] comes from preliminary survey results presented in 2017. The figures are worker-reported and refer to specific survey years and routes.
- **Advocacy figures.** The Somalia figures come from an advocacy briefing [22].
- **Narrow peer-reviewed base.** Most peer-reviewed evidence here comes from Kenya, the Philippines and El Salvador. It may not transfer to the Gulf or Central Asian corridors.

### 7.3 Dropped or unverified

- **RPW after Q3 2025:** no later issue could be opened. Figures of about 6.0–6.2% for Q1 2026 appear only on secondary sites and were not used.
- **A "Migration and Development Brief 41":** cited on a secondary site with a figure for 2024. The World Bank says the series ended at 40. Not used.
- **A published Suri–Jack reply to Bateman et al. (2019):** not found.
- **The 3ie replication of Jack and Suri (2014):** found in Crossref, but I could not read its conclusions, so it is not cited.
- **The US Department of Justice press release on Western Union (2017):** the page returned no readable content, so the company's release is cited instead.

### 7.4 Open questions

1. Will the cheaper digital channels reach the 3% goal by 2030, or will the gap stay at the bank-account and identity barrier?
2. How should remittances paid out of recruitment debt be counted? Are they "development flows" or debt service?
3. Can de-risking be reduced without governments carrying banks' liability, and who should be accountable when a safe corridor is abused?
4. What did the sanctions-related shifts after 2022 do to Central Asian households' total income, not just to measured formal flows?
5. Does flat taxation of cash remittances shift money into accounts, or into informal channels?
