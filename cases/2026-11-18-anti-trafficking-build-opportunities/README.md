# Tools that could be built to help end human trafficking

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. Complexity ratings and time ranges are estimates, not quotes from a partner. Organization descriptions were checked against public sources in November 2026; some tools change owners, go offline, or become commercial products. Inclusion of an organization is not an endorsement, and this study does not say that any named group has agreed to collaborate. Hotline and screening numbers describe reports or official processes, not the true number of people harmed.*

## The ethical question

Technology is often offered as a way to help end human trafficking. Some of it helps. Some of it copies work that already exists, fades when a grant ends, or puts people in danger.

The question here is practical and narrow: what software or hardware could a small independent team realistically build that would reduce harm, especially in Southeast Asia, without copying tools that already work and without creating new risks for the people it is meant to serve?

This is a research starting point, not a project plan, and not a claim that technology can end trafficking on its own. The useful work is more modest. It is to make it less likely that someone accepts a fraudulent job offer, to give frontline workers better language and screening support, to keep small nonprofit tools alive, and — only with the right partners — to improve labour-related information at ports and at sea.

## Safety first

Technology can help, but it can also get people hurt. Tools must be designed with survivor-led and nonprofit input, not invented in a lab and thrown at a vulnerable population. Do not encourage or enable vigilante investigations, rescues, or public “name and shame” lists. Do not build covert beacons, hidden trackers, or mesh radios for people held in scam compounds or on boats: phones and identity documents are often confiscated, and a discovered device can lead to retaliation. Do not collect more personal data than a tool truly needs. Do not present a risk score as proof of trafficking. Check the existing tool map before building, so you are not duplicating work or competing with groups already doing it.

## Findings

### The technology landscape

Tech Against Trafficking (TAT), a coalition convened with BSR and technology-company members, has been the main public mapper of anti-trafficking tools since 2018. Its 2025 landscape refresh counted 265 tools designed specifically to counter trafficking, and deliberately left out general law-enforcement systems such as phone interception. A later, AI-assisted map published with data collected in October 2026 lists 1,000 tools and uses a broader net, including tools whose public sources show a connection to trafficking, modern slavery, forced labour, or online child sexual exploitation. TAT says inclusion is not an endorsement and is not an assessment of quality, safety, or impact. The 2026 map searched English-language public web sources, so smaller or local tools are likely undercounted. The two maps used different methods, so the 2025 and 2026 counts are not a simple growth story.

- **265 tools** were in TAT’s 2025 refresh. Of 305 tools mapped in 2020, 74 later became inactive, mainly from short funding and weak maintenance.
- **84%** of the 2025 set was proprietary. Open-source and open-access tools are still the exception, which makes local adaptation harder.
- **46%** of the 2025 set was English-only. Multilingual coverage beyond French, Spanish, and Arabic is limited. That is a direct problem for migrant workers in Southeast Asia.
- **1,000 tools** appear in the October 2026 map. Most are web platforms. Common functions include investigations, case management and survivor services, and risk monitoring. North America is the largest regional concentration.

The 2025 refresh also found that nearly half of tools (46%) are designed for or used by businesses, and that private-sector organizations made up 44% of tool developers compared with 32% for nonprofits. Worker-engagement tools had grown to 16% of the set, and awareness and education tools to 17%. The field is shifting a little toward prevention and worker voice, but it is still crowded at the business and investigation end, thin in the Global South, and fragile when grant funding ends.

TAT’s December 2024 summit notes add texture that the maps cannot. Participants said end users in the Global South are often left out of design; language barriers and low technical literacy inside nonprofits limit uptake; funding is too short to maintain tools; the marketplace is noisy, so groups reinvent what already exists; and survivor inclusion is still too weak. Those are the conditions any new build has to survive.

### Gaps practitioners have named

The gaps below are ones that nonprofits, researchers, or official reports have stated in public. They are concentrated on Southeast Asia because three problems overlap there: labour trafficking in fishing and other sectors, debt-based recruitment, and forced criminality in online scam compounds in Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos, and nearby countries, with Thailand often used as a transit route.

| Gap | What sources say | Implication for a small team |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prevention at the recruitment stage is weak | A July 2026 Stimson Center brief on cyber-fraud and trafficking frameworks says a foundational gap is preventing recruitment into scam compounds before it happens. Job boards, recruitment portals, and visa processes are often not checked against known fraudulent employers or ad patterns. Generative AI has made fake recruitment more convincing. India’s extra screening for travel to Laos, Cambodia, and Myanmar is cited as an exception, not the rule. | A worker-facing red-flag tool, built with partners, is more realistic than trying to regulate job boards. |
| Language, literacy, and offline use are still afterthoughts | TAT’s 2025 refresh found 46% of tools English-only. Winrock’s 2023 USAID Asia CTIP digital-tool study, based on interviews with people who built safe-migration tools in Asia, recommends free, no-account tools, audio for low-literacy users, simple language, low data use, and offline function. The U.S. State Department’s 2025 Trafficking in Persons (TIP) report on Thailand says interpretation resources are still inadequate and interviewing practice is inconsistent, so labour-trafficking and forced-criminality victims are missed. | Southeast Asian languages, audio, and offline design are not extras. They are the gap. |
| Tools die when the grant ends | TAT: 74 of 305 tools from the 2020 map later went inactive. Winrock: many piloted tools were not sustainable; nonprofit funding is often under five years; more than half of civil-society interviewees said they could not keep a tool running without their tech partner; unclear intellectual property made handover worse. | Maintenance, open licensing, and a written intellectual-property and data plan may help more than a new brand-name app. |
| Open, locally adaptable tools are scarce | 84% of the 2025 TAT set was proprietary. The original Apprise screening idea was openly licensed, then a related product line moved into a commercial supply-chain offering. | Open tools for nonprofits still have a rationale, but only after confirming the need is real and not already served. |
| Fishing work remains high-risk, and inspections lag technology | The 2025 TIP report on Thailand describes debt, document confiscation, unpaid or deducted wages, withheld bank cards, long hours, and violence on some fishing vessels, and calls for stronger enforcement of worker protections. Global Fishing Watch argues that AI vessel tracking can help prioritize inspections, but also that labour inspections in fisheries are still far too rare and that port inspectors often lack labour-inspection training. SEAFDEC and Thailand’s Department of Fisheries began a 2026–2028 study of cheaper tracking for 10 to under 30 gross-tonne vessels; a 27 October 2026 meeting reviewed Japan’s Triton Sphere system. An earlier USAID/Mars “Connectivity at Sea” pilot tested affordable ship-to-shore messaging on a small number of Phuket-area boats. | Hardware or data tools only make sense with fisheries and labour partners. Satellite risk models already exist at global scale. |
| Scam-compound enforcement does not fix the pipeline | IJM describes recruitment through fake white-collar job ads, then confiscation of passports, IDs, and phones. The U.S. TIP report says Thailand is used as a transit country to special economic zones and scam operations next door, and that official complicity remains a problem. UN and nonprofit estimates of how many people are held vary and should be quoted only with their source; the 2025 TIP report, as cited in a 2026 U.S. Congressional Research Service brief, included a nonprofit estimate of at least 150,000 people in at least 350 compounds in Cambodia. | A small team can contribute at the public recruitment and awareness layer, not inside compounds. |
| Design often excludes the people who will use it | TAT’s 2024 summit and its October 2025 survivor-centric guidance, and Winrock’s study, all say end users and people with lived experience should shape the tool throughout its life, not only at a launch event. Winrock also warns against adding a grievance function if the organization cannot actually respond. | No public launch without a partner who can handle referrals, and without a threat model for what happens if the tool fails or is seized. |

## Proposals

The ranking below is an estimate, based on Southeast Asia relevance, stated gaps, safety, feasibility for a small independent team, and the risk of duplicating existing tools. It is not a prediction of impact. Each idea is written so a partner can say yes, no, or “someone already does that.”

### 1. Pre-departure scam-job red-flag Android app

- **Problem it solves:** People are recruited into scam compounds and other trafficking situations through job ads and chats that look professional. IJM and the Stimson brief both describe this pipeline. A worker who can check red flags, in their own language, before they travel, has a chance to stop.
- **Who would use it:** Jobseekers and their families in Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos, Vietnam, Thailand, China, and other source countries, plus outreach staff who already do pre-departure education.
- **Why existing tools do not fully cover it:** Golden Dreams is a ratings and marketplace app, not a focused scam-offer examiner. The STOP APP is for reporting suspicious activity. Commercial job platforms have uneven safety features. Stimson argues that prevention tools have not kept up with online, AI-assisted recruitment.
- **Build complexity (estimate):** An offline first version with partner-written red-flag questions, audio, and official hotline and embassy numbers is low to medium — perhaps a few months of software work after content is agreed. Adding screenshot or chat analysis is harder and should wait. Do not train a public “scam classifier” until partners and a threat model exist.
- **What the work involves:** An Android app calling a simple programming interface; a modest content-update backend; careful build and release hygiene. The hard part is language, legal wording, and referral quality, not the code.
- **Ethical and safety risks:** A clean bill of health could give false confidence. Traffickers can test the app and rewrite ads. If a phone is later searched, an installed anti-scam app or saved case history could put someone at risk. Collecting ads may include personal data. Naming a real company as a trafficker can be defamatory and wrong. The app should explain uncertainty, avoid automatic police reports, and keep little or no user data.
- **Partner type:** Migrant community organizations and survivor-led groups for content; a worker-voice or safe-migration nonprofit to confirm you are not cloning Golden Dreams or a local campaign already in the field.

### 2. Offline rights and referral audio pack

- **Problem it solves:** The TIP report on Thailand flags missing interpreters and uneven victim identification. Winrock interviewees said tools need audio and simple native-language wording. People cannot use a help number they cannot find or understand.
- **Who would use it:** Outreach teams, shelters, and, if they want it, labour inspectors. A worker-facing version can be a module inside idea 1 rather than a second app.
- **Why existing tools do not fully cover it:** National hotlines and nonprofit directories exist, but they are often online-only, language-limited, or quickly out of date. Polaris-style directories are strongest in the United States.
- **Build complexity (estimate):** Low, if a partner already maintains the referral list. The work is recording, translation, versioning, and a very simple offline Android or audio package. Optional light hardware is a rugged phone, headphones, and a printed card — not a custom circuit board.
- **What the work involves:** An Android app or even a static packaged site, plus careful content pipelines. This does not need low-level systems work.
- **Ethical and safety risks:** A stale emergency number is worse than none. Do not invent a new hotline you cannot staff. Do not record users. Get permission before using any organization’s number, and set a review calendar.
- **Partner type:** Shelter and outreach nonprofits, and whatever official or nonprofit body already updates local referral lists.

### 3. Adopt-a-tool maintenance for small Southeast Asian nonprofits

- **Problem it solves:** TAT and Winrock both document tools that worked in a pilot and then stalled. Small civil-society groups often cannot keep a vendor or a developer after the grant.
- **Who would use it:** A small number of existing nonprofit tools that already have users in the region — websites, server-side apps, Android builds, data pipelines.
- **Why existing tools do not fully cover it:** Commercial maintainers are expensive, and 84% of mapped tools are proprietary. This is unglamorous work, and it may help more, sooner, than a new brand.
- **Build complexity (estimate):** Low to start (an audit of one tool), but it is an ongoing duty, not a weekend project. Budget time for security updates, not just features.
- **What the work involves:** Web applications, scripting, Android, and systems engineering. A strong fit for a small technical team that can stay with a partner over time.
- **Ethical and safety risks:** You may be given access to case or victim data. Refuse that access unless there is a contract, data minimization, and a security review. Clarify intellectual property so the nonprofit is not trapped again. Do not “improve” a tool in ways that change how victims are identified without the partner and survivors agreeing.
- **Partner type:** One civil-society organization at a time, with a written agreement on intellectual property, data, and what happens if you stop.

### 4. Open frontline screening companion

- **Problem it solves:** Frontline responders still struggle to screen consistently and privately across many languages. Thailand has been consolidating official screening forms, but the 2025 TIP report says practice is uneven, especially for forced labour and forced criminality.
- **Who would use it:** Labour inspectors, nonprofit outreach staff, and multidisciplinary teams — on the responder’s device, with headphones, following the Apprise pattern. It is not a self-rescue app for people whose phones have been taken.
- **Why existing tools do not fully cover it:** The original Apprise research was deeply local and openly licensed; the maintained audit product is commercial. Official paper forms do not solve language or privacy-from-the-overseer problems by themselves.
- **Build complexity (estimate):** Medium. The software is straightforward; getting indicators, translations, legal review, and field protocol right is not.
- **What the work involves:** An offline Android app with careful synchronization and a restrained backend. Hardware, if any, is headphones and a cheap dedicated handset, not a custom radio.
- **Ethical and safety risks:** An overseer who can see answers, or a summary screen that is too revealing, can endanger a worker. Data on a responder phone can be lost, stolen, or demanded. A score can be treated as a legal finding. Do not deploy without a protocol for what happens when someone asks for help.
- **Partner type:** Labour-rights nonprofits first; government screening or inspector-training programs only if they ask. Check whether any open fork of Apprise is still in use before starting.

### 5. Worker-owned recruitment-cost and contract ledger

- **Problem it solves:** Debt bondage and murky wage deductions are repeatedly described in the Thailand TIP report, including in fishing and seafood processing, where workers may not have their bank cards or a clear account of fees.
- **Who would use it:** Workers who still control a phone (often pre-departure or in formal workplaces) and the legal-aid groups who help them later.
- **Why existing tools do not fully cover it:** Corporate worker-voice and payroll tools usually belong to the brand or employer. Early TAT mapping treated worker payment-security tools as a small category. Workers need a record they control.
- **Build complexity (estimate):** Medium, mostly because privacy, export for lawyers, and adversarial employers are hard. A paper-plus-photo first version may be wiser than a clever vault.
- **What the work involves:** An Android app, encryption, and a minimal programming interface. Do not overbuild.
- **Ethical and safety risks:** If an employer finds the app, the worker may be punished. A cloud copy can be breached or demanded by authorities. Promising that the ledger “will win a case” would be unethical, as Winrock warned about tools that promise remediation they cannot deliver.
- **Partner type:** Migrant legal-aid organizations and unions that already litigate or mediate wage and recruitment disputes.

### 6. Thai-language wrapper for public vessel data

- **Problem it solves:** Global Fishing Watch already models forced-labour risk from public tracking data, but local unions and nonprofits may still lack a simple, Thai-language way to prepare for a port visit or to understand public movement patterns. Global Fishing Watch itself has said labour inspections remain rare.
- **Who would use it:** Civil-society and union staff, and perhaps inspectors who already have a mandate — not private citizens hunting boats.
- **Why existing tools do not fully cover it:** The gap is less “no satellite model exists” than “the people who talk to crew at the dock may not have a usable, local interface and a labour checklist.” Confirm that gap with users before writing code. Global Fishing Watch has discussed adding a labour insights tab to Vessel Viewer.
- **Build complexity (estimate):** Medium, if it only wraps public programming interfaces and adds language, checklists, and caveats. High, and unjustified, if it tries to rebuild the model.
- **What the work involves:** A web front end, data pipelines, and careful documentation. No custom hardware.
- **Ethical and safety risks:** A “high risk” flag is not evidence and can smear a crew or a small operator. Public maps can feed harassment. Automatic identification systems can be switched off, spoofed, or missing, especially on smaller boats. This tool must say that plainly and must not support vigilante boarding or online pile-ons.
- **Partner type:** Fisher and migrant-labour groups already doing port outreach; contact Global Fishing Watch about collaboration or reuse so you do not fork their work badly.

### 7. Nonprofit-gated job-ad pattern monitor

- **Problem it solves:** Stimson notes that entry-point controls rarely catch fraudulent job ads. Academic work such as Stony Brook’s Anansi pipeline studies job scams, but frontline Southeast Asian nonprofits may lack a shared, restricted view of repeating patterns.
- **Who would use it:** A closed group of trusted nonprofits or researchers. Not the public, not journalists by default, and not a messaging “hunter” channel.
- **Why existing tools do not fully cover it:** Platform trust-and-safety teams work inside their own sites. Public reporting apps collect incidents, but do not necessarily give nonprofits a structured early-warning picture of recruitment ads in regional languages.
- **Build complexity (estimate):** Medium to high, and the legal work may dominate the software. Collect only public pages, respect terms of service and local law, and get a lawyer. Unauthorized access to accounts or systems is out of scope.
- **What the work involves:** Web collection and classification, with human review. This is easy to do badly.
- **Ethical and safety risks:** High. A public or leaky list can ruin a legitimate employer, teach traffickers how to evade flags, or invite vigilantism. False positives will happen. Do not automate takedowns. Do not scrape private groups or bypass access controls.
- **Partner type:** A research nonprofit or practitioner coalition, after legal review. Build this later, if at all, and only behind authentication.

### 8. Low-cost tracker for small Thai fishing vessels

- **Problem it solves:** Smaller commercial boats (SEAFDEC’s current study focuses on 10 to under 30 gross tonnes) often lack an affordable vessel-monitoring option. Labour abuse is harder to reach when a vessel stays out of port for a long time. This device would not detect trafficking; it could, at best, support fisheries management and, indirectly, more frequent contact with shore.
- **Who would use it:** Regulators and vessel operators who agree to fit it — only after SEAFDEC and the Department of Fisheries say there is still a gap. The 27 October 2026 SEAFDEC and Department of Fisheries meeting was already reviewing Triton Sphere, a solar terminal that logs positions every 30 seconds, uses ELTRES and LTE rather than a conventional satellite vessel-monitoring system, installs without wiring into the boat’s electrical system, has no user on/off switch, and can include a wearable man-overboard distress signal.
- **Why existing tools do not fully cover it:** Global systems based on automatic identification miss boats that have no transponder or that switch it off. Commercial vessel-monitoring systems can be costly. Whether a newly built board is actually cheaper, certifiable, and wanted is unknown until those agencies and vendors are asked.
- **Build complexity (estimate):** High. Radio, power, enclosure, type approval, and field reliability are real engineering, not a weekend circuit board. If it proceeds, use cheap parts that are still in active production, and treat end-of-life or not-recommended-for-new-design components as disqualifying.
- **What the work involves:** Electronics, firmware, and possibly drivers. Embedded and systems skills matter more here than ordinary web development.
- **Ethical and safety risks:** Tracking can be used to control workers rather than to protect them. A device can be removed, jammed, or left on a tied-up boat. A man-overboard wearable is a safety feature, not an anti-trafficking implant, and should never be hidden on a person. Labour nonprofits need a seat at the design table so this does not become only a policing tool.
- **Partner type:** SEAFDEC and the Department of Fisheries first. If they are already adopting a commercial system, do not compete. If they want an open, cheaper complement, involve labour nonprofits from the start.

## Risks

Park the following ideas unless a specialist nonprofit and a serious security review lead the work.

Do not build covert emergency beacons, hidden location tags, or offline mesh radios for people held in scam compounds or trapped at sea. IJM and other public sources describe confiscation of phones and documents, violence, and tight control of movement. A device that is found can mark someone as a resister. Radio emissions can be searched for. This is not a first hardware project, and it is not a place for unsupervised experimentation.

- Do not build a public “trafficker map,” a compound-hunting dashboard, or a tool that invites untrained people to investigate or intervene.
- Do not clone Spotlight, Traffik Analysis Hub, Golden Dreams, or a global satellite risk model.
- Do not stand up a new victim hotline unless you can staff it, securely, without interruption.
- Do not scrape private accounts, bypass access controls, or write exploit-like collection tools. If a monitor is ever built, it should use lawful public information and a partner’s legal advice.
- Do not add facial recognition or covert recording to a screening or outreach tool.

## Recommendation

Start with idea 1, the pre-departure scam-job red-flag Android app, and treat idea 2 — offline rights audio and verified referral numbers — as the first content module inside it. That pairing matches the strongest stated prevention gap (recruitment into scam compounds). It can work offline in Burmese, Khmer, Lao, Thai, Vietnamese, Chinese, and English, and it does not require putting hardware on a person in captivity. It can stay useful even if the “detector” is only a carefully written checklist.

Do not begin by training a model or designing a circuit board. Begin by asking whether a trusted local organization wants this, and whether it would collide with Golden Dreams, government awareness campaigns, or an existing messaging or social-media effort. Winrock’s interviewees put the test simply: make sure it is relevant, that nobody else is already doing it, that people will use it, and that you can show impact without overselling.

1. Search TAT’s 2026 tool map and ask two or three Southeast Asia migrant-worker or safe-migration organizations what they already give jobseekers. Stop or narrow the idea if the gap is already filled.
2. If a partner is interested, co-design the red-flag list and the wording with survivor-led and community groups. Pay people for their time. Agree who owns the content and the data before any code is written.
3. Write a short threat model: what happens if a trafficker studies the app, if an employer searches a phone, if a referral number is wrong, or if the app is too reassuring. Decide to store as little as possible — ideally nothing that identifies a user.
4. Get legal review of the language (no defamation, no fake “official” status, no automated accusations) and of how official hotlines and embassies are listed.
5. Build a small offline Android first version: language choice, audio, checklist or guided questions, uncertainty language, and partner-approved referrals. Leave screenshot intelligence and any nonprofit-only monitor (idea 7) for a later, separate decision.
6. Pilot with the partner, in a limited community, with a way to measure whether people understand the guidance and whether referrals work. Do not do a public launch from a personal account.
7. In parallel, offer idea 3 as quiet help: if a nonprofit has a useful tool that is about to go dark, fixing that tool may help more, sooner, than shipping a new brand.

Ending human trafficking is the goal. The first build should be a tool that a partner will actually maintain, that a jobseeker can use without creating an account, and that does not increase harm if it fails. If no partner wants it, do not ship it.
