# Debt Relief and Sovereign Debt Restructuring for Low-Income Countries: An Ethical Research Case

*A research note on how debt service competes with spending on food, health and education, how the G20 Common Framework and other restructuring routes have worked in practice, what debt swaps and climate-resilient debt clauses can and cannot do, who the creditors now are, and the strongest arguments against more generous relief. It is research and writing only. Figures come from the sources listed at the end. Each DOI was checked against the Crossref registry and each link was opened on 18 November 2026. Advocacy and creditor-side sources are labelled as such.*

---

## 1. How debt service crowds out food, health and education

**The scale (official sources).**

- **UNCTAD, *A World of Debt 2025*** (26 June 2025) [1]:
  - Global public debt reached a record $102 trillion in 2024. Developing countries owed $31 trillion of it, and their debt has grown twice as fast as developed economies' since 2010.
  - Developing countries paid $921 billion in net interest in 2024, 10% more than in 2023.
  - A record 61 developing countries spent 10% or more of government revenue on interest.
  - 46 developing countries spend more on interest than on either health or education. "3.4 billion people live in countries that spend more on interest payments than on either health or education".
  - Since 2020, developing regions have borrowed at rates two to four times those of the United States.
- **World Bank, *International Debt Report 2025*** (December 2025) [2]:
  - Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) paid out about US$741 billion more in principal and interest between 2022 and 2024 than they received in new financing. It was "the largest debt-related outflow in more than 50 years".
  - LMICs "paid a record US$415 billion in interest alone".
  - Among the 22 most highly indebted countries, "one out of every two people today cannot afford the minimum daily diet necessary for lasting health" [2].

**UN agency advocacy.** UNICEF's *State of the World's Children 2025* [3] says external debt repayments are "exceeding health and education spending combined in many low-income countries". It also reports that 45 developing countries pay more in interest than they spend on health, and 22 more than on education. UNCTAD's count of 46, above, uses a different test (health *or* education). UNICEF is an official UN agency, but this report is written to advocate for children and is labelled accordingly here.

**Campaign advocacy.** Debt Justice (February 2026) [4] is a campaign organisation, and its figures are advocacy estimates. It calculates that external debt payments by 56 low- and lower-middle-income countries averaged 19.2% of government revenue in 2025. That is the highest in any year since 1990, and the figure has more than trebled since 2010. The figures come from World Bank and IMF data. Debt Justice attributes the fall in payments in the 2000s partly to the HIPC and MDRI debt-relief initiatives [4].

**Peer-reviewed evidence on crowding out.** Fosu (2008) [5] uses five-year panel data for 35 sub-Saharan African countries (1975–94). He finds that "although actual debt servicing has little impact, a binding debt-servicing constraint that reflects the debt burden would shift expenditure away from health". He also finds that aid tends to shift spending towards health, but "the debt-burden effect is dominant" [5].

**What the evidence does and does not show.** Interest exceeding health spending is a comparison of two budget lines. It does not prove that each dollar of interest is a dollar taken from health. Fosu's distinction matters: the burden of debt, and the expectation of having to pay, distorts budgets more than the actual payments do. The headline counts [1][3] also mix low-income countries with large middle-income economies. The ethical point still holds. When a large share of revenue goes abroad to creditors, governments have less room to protect food, health and schooling, and the World Bank's own figure on unaffordable diets [2] shows who bears the cost.

## 2. The G20 Common Framework: Chad, Zambia, Ghana and Ethiopia, and why Sri Lanka was different

**What the Common Framework is.** The "Common Framework for Debt Treatments beyond the DSSI" was endorsed by the G20 and the Paris Club in 2020 [6]. Only countries that had been eligible for the G20 Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI) can request a treatment. That means International Development Association (IDA) countries and UN-defined least developed countries that are current with the IMF and World Bank [6].

How a case works:

- The IMF and World Bank debt sustainability analysis (DSA) and an upper-credit-tranche IMF programme set the "restructuring envelope", the total relief needed [6].
- An Official Creditor Committee (OCC), which brings Paris Club and non-Paris Club creditors such as China into one room, agrees terms in principle and then a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU).
- Each creditor then implements the MoU through its own bilateral agreement.
- The debtor must seek "comparability of treatment" (CoT) from its other creditors, meaning terms at least as favourable as the OCC's [6].

**Timelines from the official record.** The G20's June 2025 note [6] and the Paris Club term sheets [7] give the key dates.

| Milestone | Zambia | Ghana | Ethiopia |
|---|---|---|---|
| Request for treatment | 1 Feb 2021 | 13–20 Dec 2022 | 3 Feb 2021 |
| IMF staff-level agreement | 3 Dec 2021 | 12 Dec 2022 | published at programme approval |
| OCC formed | 16 Jun 2022 | 12 May 2023 | 16 Sep 2021 |
| IMF programme approved | 31 Aug 2022 | 17 May 2023 | 29 Jul 2024 |
| Agreement in principle | 22 Jun 2023 | 12 Jan 2024 | 21 Mar 2025 |
| MoU agreed | 13 Oct 2023 | 11 Jun 2024 | 2 Jul 2025 |
| MoU signatures complete | 2 Apr 2024 | 28 Jan 2025 | 31 Dec 2025 |

The G20 note itself gives the time from staff-level agreement to agreement on main terms: one year and six months for Zambia, one year and one month for Ghana, and seven months for Ethiopia [6]. Each case was faster than the one before, but every case was slow compared with the Debt Service Suspension Initiative's original pandemic-era goal of quick relief.

**Chad, the first completed case.** Chad was the first country to reach an agreement under the Common Framework. On 13 November 2022 the IMF Managing Director welcomed "the first Common Framework accord", reached with an official creditor committee comprising China, France, India and Saudi Arabia, "as well as private creditors" [31]. The IMF said the treatment was consistent with Chad's IMF-supported programme, "reduces the risk of debt distress" and "provides protection against downside risks, including lower oil prices" [31]. Contemporaneous reporting identified the principal private creditor as Glencore and described the treatment as a reprofiling of debt service, including obligations falling in 2024, rather than a reduction of the debt stock [32]. World Bank President David Malpass, as quoted in that reporting, said: "The agreement reached by the creditors provides no immediate debt reduction. As a result, the debt service burden of Chad remains heavy and is crowding out priority expenditures on food, health, education and climate" [32]. The official IMF statement and the World Bank president's criticism should be read together: the first Common Framework case delivered a rescheduling with contingency protection, not a haircut. That outcome is part of the Framework's record on delay and on the depth of relief.

**Zambia.**

- **Default and request.** Zambia missed a Eurobond coupon in November 2020, "Africa's first pandemic-era sovereign default", and applied under the Common Framework on 1 February 2021 [8].
- **Debt and IMF support.** External debt was US$20.9 billion at end-2022. Of this, US$6.8 billion was owed to private creditors, US$6.3 billion to official bilateral creditors and US$3.6 billion to multilaterals [7]. The IMF approved an SDR 978.2 million (about US$1.3 billion) Extended Credit Facility on 31 August 2022 [7].
- **Official creditors.** The OCC was co-chaired by China and France, with South Africa as vice-chair. It applied a stock treatment that reschedules debt to 2043. Creditors get an "upside treatment" if Zambia's debt-carrying capacity is upgraded [7].
- **Bondholders.** Late in 2023 the OCC rejected a deal Zambia had struck with bondholders. It found comparability would be met only in the upside scenario, not the base case [8]. A new agreement on key terms followed on 25 March 2024, and bondholders voted it through in the first week of June 2024. Under the deal, bondholders write off about US$840 million [8].
- **Implementation.** By the Paris Club's June 2026 update, 10 of the 16 OCC creditors had signed bilateral agreements. South Africa, Sweden, Switzerland, the United States, Russia and Denmark were still "ongoing" [7].
- **Unresolved.** A dispute over whether the regional lender Afreximbank deserves preferred-creditor status remains open [8].

**Ghana.**

- **Domestic debt first.** Ghana restructured its domestic debt first, beginning in December 2022 [9].
- **IMF support.** The IMF approved an SDR 2.242 billion (about US$3 billion) Extended Credit Facility on 17 May 2023 [7].
- **Official creditors.** The OCC, co-chaired by China and France, applied a "flow" treatment. It reschedules debt service falling due from January 2023 to December 2026 and extends the weighted average maturity from 2029 to 2038 [7].
- **Bondholders.** Bondholders gave "more than 98 percent consent" to exchange US$13 billion of Eurobonds. The exchange brought a 37% nominal haircut (about US$5 billion) and US$4.3 billion of debt-service relief during the IMF programme. It also cut the average interest rate on the bonds from over 8% to under 5% [9].
- **New bond clauses.** The new bonds include a loss-reinstatement clause, an information-sharing clause and a most-favoured-creditor clause [9].
- **Speed.** The World Bank notes that Ghana "completed its restructuring in half the time of previous processes" with help from the Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable [2].
- **Implementation.** By July 2026, 15 of the 25 OCC members had signed bilateral agreements. Japan, Canada, Saudi Arabia and seven others were still "ongoing" [7].

**Ethiopia.**

- **Debt and IMF support.** External debt was US$31 billion at end-June 2024: 50% multilateral, 38% official bilateral and 12% private [7]. The IMF approved an SDR 2.6 billion (about US$3.4 billion) Extended Credit Facility on 29 July 2024 [7].
- **Official creditors.** The OCC suspended debt service from 1 January 2023 and later applied a stock treatment rescheduling to FY2038/39 [7]. By September 2026, nine bilateral agreements were signed and three were ongoing. Japan and Korea needed no amendment because their existing terms were already more favourable [7].
- **Bondholders.** Ethiopia defaulted on its Eurobond (the "2024 Notes") in December 2023 [10]. A bondholder agreement reached in January 2026 was rejected by the OCC a month later on comparability grounds [10]. On 29 June 2026 a new agreement in principle provided for an US$880 million bond maturing in July 2029 plus a "New Money Warrant". The OCC co-chairs did not object, subject to approval by the wider OCC [10].
- **The creditors' view.** The bondholder committee, representing about 45% of the notes, is a creditor and its view should be read as such. It argued that Ethiopia "languished unnecessarily in default for over 2 ½ years" because the IMF's DSA was "based on wildly erroneous estimates", and that the Common Framework has "broad flaws" [10].
- **Not verified.** A later OCC decision that the June terms met comparability was reported in search results but not confirmed from a page opened for this note.

**Sri Lanka: outside the Common Framework.** Sri Lanka is a middle-income country and was "not eligible for the CF" [11], because it was not DSSI-eligible [6].

- **Moratorium.** It suspended payments on official bilateral and commercial external debt on 12 April 2022 [12].
- **Official creditors.** Japan describes the OCC it launched in April 2023 as "the first framework to coordinate between Paris Club countries and non-Paris Club countries on debt restructuring for middle-income countries". The OCC was co-chaired by India, Japan and France [12]. China was not a member: China, Saudi Arabia and Iran attended preparatory meetings only [12], and Sri Lanka settled separately with China Eximbank and China Development Bank [11]. The OCC agreed main parameters on 23 November 2023 and finalised the MoU on 26 June 2024 [12].
- **Bondholders.** In December 2024, holders of 97.86% of US$12.5 billion of international bonds agreed to modify or exchange them [12]. The government reports a baseline "NPV effort" of 40% at an 11% discount rate [12]. ODI, using exit yields, estimates the bondholder haircut at "less than 10%, even in the base case" [11]. The gap shows how much "relief" depends on the discount rate chosen.

**Critiques: delay, shallow relief and comparability of treatment.**

- **Shallow relief in the first case.** Chad's 2022 agreement, the Framework's first completed deal, provided no immediate reduction in the debt stock [31][32]. Official creditors and the IMF presented it as protection against downside risks, including lower oil prices [31]. That is a different outcome from later stock treatments such as Zambia's rescheduling to 2043 [7], and it bears on critiques that the Framework can be both slow and, in some cases, shallow.
- **Delay.** Zambia's process kept the economy "at a standstill for over 3.5 years" [8]. By my count from the official dates, Ethiopia waited more than five years from its February 2021 request to a bondholder agreement in principle (June 2026) [6][10].
- **A vague comparability test.** ODI calls comparability of treatment "a flimsy variable" and the outcomes "too little, too late and too complex" [11]. The CGD paper describes a "lack of clarity on both the definition and enforceability" of comparability, and an official creditor committee holding "effective veto power" over private deals [8]. It proposes a two-tier test, with separate yardsticks for official and private creditors, defined in advance [8].
- **Sequencing.** Official creditors negotiate first and private creditors after. Proposals to run the two in parallel are gaining ground; ODI says Ethiopia "appears to be adopting" this approach by opening official and bondholder talks in parallel [11].
- **State-contingent instruments.** Value-recovery instruments that pay creditors more when the economy does well can win bondholder support, but at the cost of relief [11]. ODI notes the IMF's concern that one-way triggers risk "a permanently higher debt burden" after temporary good news [11].
- **Optimistic forecasts.** The IMF-World Bank low-income-country DSA has been criticised for over-optimistic revenue forecasts [11]. In Ethiopia, bondholders made the opposite complaint, that it was too pessimistic [10]. Both critiques say the restructuring envelope rests on contestable forecasts.

## 3. Debt swaps: for nature, climate and food

**The large "debt-for-nature" conversions.**

| Country (year) | Debt retired or refinanced | New financing | Conservation funding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Belize (2021) | US$553m "Superbond" bought back at 45% discount | US$364m Blue Loan | ~US$180m over 20 years | TNC [13] |
| Barbados (2022) | Bond buyback (amount not given in source) | Blue Loan with US$150m guarantees (IDB US$100m, TNC US$50m) | ~US$50m over 15 years | IDB [13] |
| Ecuador, Galápagos (2023) | US$1.628bn of international bonds | US$656m loan | US$323m over 18.5 years; >US$450m with endowment | DFC [13] |
| Gabon (2023) | US$500m of debt refinanced | Blue bond with up to US$500m US risk insurance | ~US$163m over 15 years | TNC [13] |

- **Belize:** principal fell by US$189 million and debt by about 12% of GDP. The blue loan's internal rate of return was 6.1% including all insurance, fees and reserves [13].
- **Ecuador:** the deal was backed by US$656 million of US government political-risk insurance and an US$85 million IDB guarantee. DFC says it brings "more than $1.126 billion lifetime savings" in debt service [13].
- **Barbados (2024):** a further "debt-for-climate-resilience" operation, backed by US$300 million of IDB and EIB guarantees, generated about US$125 million in fiscal savings for water and sewage resilience. It is a sustainability-linked loan with penalties if water targets are missed [13].

**Debt-for-food and development swaps.** Egypt has used bilateral debt swaps with the World Food Programme [14]:

- **Italy (2003–2022):** about US$22 million, benefiting 93,000 students. Children received daily fortified date bars, and families received monthly food rations conditional on their children's regular school attendance.
- **Germany (from 2013):** US$83.3 million across five phases for school meals and rural development.

This account comes from a School Meals Coalition story with WFP material [14]. It reports outputs, not independently evaluated outcomes.

**Evidence and critiques.**

- **Scale.** Ecuador's swap, the largest, retired US$1.6 billion of bonds. Developing countries' net interest bill was US$921 billion in 2024 alone [1]. Swaps can help individual countries but are not a systemic answer.
- **Efficiency.** Chamon, Klok, Thakoor and Zettelmeyer (2024) [15] find that as fiscal support for climate, "debt swaps are generally dominated by conditional grants". As a way to reduce debt, they are "generally dominated" by grants combined with a proper restructuring. Swaps make sense "when they noticeably reduce debt risks while avoiding the costs of a full-blown debt restructuring", or pragmatically when grants are unavailable [15].
- **Design.** Buybacks at market prices let creditors who stay out free-ride on the price rise. Chamon et al. recommend negotiated exchanges backed by collective action clauses, with any buyback done by the donor [15].
- **Costs and complexity.** The deals depend on credit enhancement from DFC, the IDB, the EIB and TNC, private reinsurance and arranger fees [13].
- **Sovereignty and accountability.** Conservation funds governed partly by non-government actors, such as Belize's fund, whose nine-member board is appointed from government and non-government sectors [13], raise questions about who decides how the money is spent. No independent evaluation of conservation outcomes was opened for this note.

## 4. Climate-resilient debt clauses

**Grenada (first mover).** In November 2015 the Paris Club rescheduled Grenada's debt and introduced a "first-ever 'hurricane clause'" [16]. Grenada's 7% bonds due 2030, issued in its 2015 restructuring, allow it to defer payments after a tropical cyclone, linked to its parametric policy with the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility (CCRIF) [16][17].

Hurricane Beryl struck on 1 July 2024, and CCRIF paid Grenada US$44 million. Because modelled losses exceeded US$30 million, Grenada deferred two payment dates, 12 November 2024 and 12 May 2025. The deferred interest is added to principal [16]. This defers payments; it does not cancel any debt.

**Barbados (broadened).** In its 2018–19 restructuring, Barbados put a natural-disaster clause "across nearly their entire debt stock" [17]:

- Triggers include earthquakes and excess rainfall as well as cyclones.
- The loss threshold is US$5 million, so the clause triggers more easily.
- Payments can be deferred for two years.
- In the second-stage version, holders of 50% of a bond can block a deferral within 15 days.

The practitioners who wrote this account advised Barbados on the restructuring. They estimate the clause could free up to US$700 million, almost 15% of the economy [17].

**UK Export Finance (UKEF).** At COP27 (8 November 2022), UKEF became "the first export credit agency in the world" to offer climate-resilient debt clauses (CRDCs) [18]. Eligible low-income countries and small island developing states can suspend all principal and interest due to UKEF for 12 months. They repay over five years after a one-year grace period, and a deferral is not an event of default. A model term sheet for private bonds and loans was also launched [18].

**World Bank.** The World Bank announced CRDCs at the June 2023 Paris summit [19]. In November 2024 it extended them to cover "all natural disasters, including droughts, floods and health emergencies", with deferral of up to two years. At that point, 14 of the 45 eligible countries had included the clause, one (St Vincent and the Grenadines) had used it after Hurricane Beryl, and there is "no cost to borrowers" [19].

**The 2023 Paris summit.** The Chair's summary of the Summit for a New Global Financing Pact (22–23 June 2023) [20] records three things:

- A call by the UK, France, the US, Spain, Barbados, the World Bank Group and the IDB for "bilateral, multilateral and private sector creditors to offer climate-resilient debt clauses by the end of 2025".
- That the "100bn USD goal" for rechannelling SDRs had been reached.
- "A broad call for timely delivery and strengthened clarity on the process of the Common Framework". The summit acknowledged the Bridgetown 2.0 agenda [20].

This note could not verify how far the end-2025 target was met.

**Assessment.** These clauses add liquidity: they defer payments; they do not reduce the debt. Their value depends on wide adoption (14 of 45 eligible World Bank borrowers by late 2024 [19]), on triggers that match the hazards a country faces, and on not being offset by higher borrowing costs. No source opened for this note measured any pricing effect.

## 5. The creditor landscape

**China.**

- **Size and hidden debt.** Horn, Reinhart and Trebesch (2021) [21] build a database of 4,900 loans and grants to 146 countries. They find that by 2017 China "had become the world's largest official creditor, surpassing the World Bank and the IMF". Its loans "typically resemble commercial rather than official lending", and "50% of China's official lending to developing countries is not reported" in standard debt statistics [21].
- **Contract terms.** Gelpern et al. (2023) [22] study 100 contracts. They find confidentiality clauses, lender-controlled revenue accounts, "no Paris Club" clauses and cancellation clauses that "potentially allow the lenders to influence debtors' domestic and foreign policies" [22].
- **Rescue lending.** AidData and co-authors report 128 rescue-lending operations in 22 countries worth US$240 billion by end-2021 [23]. Their average interest rate was 5%, against 2% for a typical IMF rescue loan [23]. Low-income countries typically receive extensions rather than new money [23].
- **Cooperation in the Common Framework.** China co-chaired the official creditor committees for Zambia, Ghana and Ethiopia, and signed bilateral agreements implementing all three treatments [7]. It was also a member of the official creditor committee for Chad, the first Common Framework agreement, alongside France, India and Saudi Arabia [31]. The CGD paper argues several of China's interventions in Zambia were "substantively constructive" rather than obstructionist [8]. ODI notes China "is learning and evolving" but "remains unwilling to provide substantial haircuts" [11]. In this record China appears as one official bilateral creditor among others in a shared restructuring system, not as an external party to it.
- **The "debt-trap" claim and its critique.** A widespread public narrative holds that Chinese official lending is a deliberate strategy to trap borrowers and seize strategic assets. That claim should be separated from documented concerns about contract terms and data. The concerns that are documented in the sources already cited include confidentiality clauses, collateral and lender-controlled accounts, hidden debt, and the higher average cost of Chinese rescue loans relative to IMF programmes [21][22][23]. The further claim of a deliberate asset-seizure strategy is not established by those sources. Brautigam (2020), in a peer-reviewed article titled "A critical look at Chinese 'debt-trap diplomacy': the rise of a meme", examines that narrative critically [33]. The full text of the article was not re-opened in this revision, so no further claims about its case studies are made here. The article is used as a critical review of the intent claim, not as a finding that Chinese lending is costless or risk-free.

**Gulf lenders.** Lons and Alhasan (2024) [24] estimate that Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, the UAE and Qatar disbursed about US$363 billion (constant 2020 dollars) in bailout aid to 22 countries between 1963 and 2022. The aid included direct budget support, central-bank deposits, oil and gas assistance or credit, debt write-offs, credit swaps and sovereign bond purchases [24]. Saudi Arabia sits on the IMF–World Bank Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable [24]. It is also on the official creditor committees for Chad, Zambia, Ghana and Ethiopia, and was among the first to sign a Zambia bilateral agreement (December 2024) [7][31]. The authors also document a newer "transactional approach", such as preferential access to privatised assets in Egypt [24].

**Multilaterals and the preferred-creditor debate.**

- **Role.** Multilateral lenders now provide "nearly half of long-term net financing" to LMICs. The World Bank's net flows reached a record of about US$36 billion in 2024 [2]. Multilaterals hold large shares of debt in these cases: 50% of Ethiopia's external debt and 17% of Zambia's [7].
- **Outside the restructuring.** By convention, claims of the established multilateral lenders sit outside restructurings.
- **The debate.** Supporters argue this preferred status keeps cheap, new lending flowing in a crisis. Critics argue it shifts the burden to other creditors. The Zambia dispute over whether Afreximbank, a regional lender, deserves the same status shows the line is contested [8]. ODI notes "insufficient clarity on how preferred creditor status is applied to plurilaterals" [11].
- **The African Development Bank.** It held about US$0.9 billion of Zambia's debt at end-2022, roughly 2.7% of public debt [8]. Afreximbank's argument is that it is a regional multilateral "analogous to" the African Development Bank. CGD notes that comparable regional-bank claims were included in Ghana's restructuring perimeter [8]. This note did not find a separate primary source on the African Development Bank's own position.

**The Paris Club and bondholders.**

- **The Paris Club.** It was formed in 1956 as an informal group of official creditors [12]. It now works alongside non-members through official creditor committees rather than alone. India, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Türkiye, Brazil and China all sit on recent committees [7][12].
- **Bondholders.** They held 51% of Ghana's external debt as private creditors [7] and US$12.5 billion of Sri Lanka's [12]. Bondholder groups have accepted large NPV haircuts, estimated at about 50% for Ghana and Zambia [11]. They also argue their interests are subordinated to official creditors' politics [10].
- **Overall shift.** Government-to-government lending to LMICs fell to its lowest level since the global financial crisis in 2024, about US$4.5 billion [2].

## 6. Fair counterarguments

**Moral hazard.** The concern is that generous relief rewards over-borrowing and lax lending. The evidence cuts in two directions.

- Cruces and Trebesch (2013) [25] study 180 restructurings from 1970 to 2010. They find that higher haircuts are "associated with significantly higher subsequent bond yield spreads and longer periods of capital market exclusion", so markets do not simply "forgive and forget". Relief has a real cost to the debtor, which limits the hazard.
- Reinhart and Trebesch (2016) [26] find that debt relief averaged 16% of GDP in recent decades. Debtors' economies improve "only if these involve debt write-offs", while "softer forms of debt relief, such as maturity extensions and interest rate reductions, are not generally followed by higher economic growth" [26].

That is an argument for relief that is decisive when it is given, not for avoiding relief. Most Common Framework treatments for official creditors are reschedulings and interest cuts [7], the "softer" kind.

**Creditor coordination and holdouts.**

- **NML v. Argentina.** Buchheit and Gulati (2017) [27] explain how the case "upset this strategic balance". US courts let holdout creditors obtain "an injunction preventing the borrower from making payments to its other equally-ranking creditors without making a 'ratable' payment to the holdouts", based on the bonds' pari passu clause [27].
- **Collective action clauses.** Fang, Schumacher and Trebesch (2021) [28] study 23 bond restructurings. Higher haircuts predict higher holdout rates. CACs reduce the problem, but "only the strongest form of CACs, with single-limb aggregate voting, minimizes the holdout problem" [28].
- **In practice.** Sri Lanka's 2024 exchange used aggregated CAC bonds [12], and Ghana's consent exceeded its 65% threshold [9].

The lesson is that contract design matters, and that holdouts remain a risk for bonds without those clauses.

**Market access.** Debtors and creditors alike worry that deep relief closes markets. Cruces and Trebesch support the concern [25]. But the World Bank reports that international bond issuance is "gradually returning" at more sustainable prices [2], and the Ethiopian bondholders' complaint was that relief was too deep, not too shallow [10]. Fast, credible restructurings may matter more for re-entry than shallow ones.

**The HIPC and MDRI track record.** The two initiatives relieved 37 countries, 31 of them in Africa, of "more than $100 billion in debt" [29]. Cassimon et al. (2015) [30] find that debt relief, especially the enhanced HIPC initiative, "has had a positive impact on recipient country total domestic revenue and public investment". MDRI raised current primary spending and domestic revenue ratios [30].

There is a sobering point: Zambia, Ghana and Ethiopia all received HIPC relief [29], and all three were back in restructuring within about two decades. Zambia's own figures show that the rebuilt debt was not mainly the product of one official bilateral creditor. Of US$20.9 billion of external debt at end-2022, US$6.8 billion was owed to private creditors and US$6.3 billion to all official bilateral creditors together, with US$3.6 billion owed to multilaterals [7]. The immediate trigger of the crisis was a missed Eurobond coupon in November 2020 [8]. Chinese state banks were part of the post-2005 borrowing, as the CGD paper discusses [8], but they sat alongside commercial bondholders and other official lenders. Relief alone, without changes in lending practices by official and private creditors, transparency and revenue, does not prevent a repeat.

## 7. Proposals, limitations and open questions

**Proposals that follow from the evidence.**

1. **Make the Common Framework predictable.**
   - Publish timelines and the comparability methodology in advance, for example CGD's two-tier test [8].
   - Negotiate with official and private creditors in parallel, not in sequence [11].
   - Protect debtors from litigation during negotiations [11].
2. **Extend an equivalent process to vulnerable middle-income countries.** Sri Lanka's committee [12] shows such a process is possible outside the Common Framework.
3. **Favour write-offs over reschedulings where the DSA shows insolvency.** The evidence on growth after relief points this way [26]. Price state-contingent instruments symmetrically, with downside protection as well as upside [11].
4. **Use swaps narrowly.** Use them where they clearly reduce debt risk or where grants are unavailable [15]. Prefer negotiated exchanges with CACs to market buybacks, and require independent evaluation of conservation and nutrition outcomes [14][15].
5. **Make climate-resilient clauses the default.** Include them in new official and private lending to climate-vulnerable countries, with broad triggers, as in the World Bank's 2024 extension [19]. Track whether they raise borrowing costs.
6. **Improve debt transparency across all creditors.** This should cover confidentiality clauses, collateral and hidden debts [21][22], rescue lending and rollovers [23], and Gulf central-bank deposits [24].
7. **Protect social spending during programmes.** Where the World Bank finds half the population of the most indebted countries cannot afford a healthy diet [2], IMF-supported programmes should include protected floors for food, health and education spending. This note did not evaluate existing programme floors.

**Ethical tensions.** Relief helps the people of the debtor country now, but weakens creditors' willingness to lend. Bondholders often include pension funds, and the most-favoured-creditor clauses used to protect them [9] can slow future deals. Earmarked swaps direct money to global public goods, such as marine biodiversity, that the debtor's own citizens might rank below health or schooling. Preferred-creditor status protects new lending, but places the cost of relief on bilateral and private creditors.

**Limitations.**

- **Primary data.** Timelines and terms come from official term sheets, ministries of finance and creditor statements. Some figures, such as bondholder haircuts and Sri Lanka's "NPV effort", depend on the discount rate the author chooses [11][12].
- **Advocacy and interested parties.** UNICEF and Debt Justice figures are labelled advocacy [3][4]. The Ethiopian bondholder statement is a creditor's view [10]. The Barbados clause estimate comes from the country's own advisers [17], and the Egypt swap results from a programme story [14].
- **Unverified.** Three things: the OCC's August 2026 comparability decision on Ethiopia's bond terms, whether the end-2025 CRDC target was met, and the African Development Bank's own position on preferred status. Sources also disagree on when Sri Lanka's OCC was formed: Japan says April 2023, while a Paris Club note says 9 May 2022. This note uses Japan's date.
- **No independent evaluations** of the swaps' conservation or nutrition outcomes were found.

**Open questions.**

- Will Ethiopia's bond exchange close, and how will the warrant be treated for comparability?
- Will the remaining bilateral agreements for Zambia and Ghana be signed, and what happens to Afreximbank's claim?
- Do CRDCs change borrowing costs?
- Can a middle-income counterpart to the Common Framework be formalised?
- Will the next generation of loans from all creditors, Chinese, Gulf, multilateral and bond markets, carry the transparency and contingent clauses that might prevent a fourth debt cycle?
