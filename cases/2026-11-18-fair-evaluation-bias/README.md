# Making evaluation track merit: what the evidence shows about blind auditions, anonymised CVs, structured interviews and performance reviews

*Ethics case-study source brief 13. Anonymous author. Prepared November 2026. Policy level only: this brief deals with procedures, experiments, evaluations and published research, not individual cases. Every factual claim is tied to a numbered source in the SOURCES section. Items marked **UNVERIFIED** could not be confirmed against a text read live for this brief. The brief does not recommend any tool; it reports when each one has helped women, helped men, done nothing, or backfired.*

**Source labels used throughout:**
- **[PR]** peer-reviewed research
- **[GOV]** government report, government trial, or government-funded guidance
- **[WP]** working paper (not peer-reviewed)
- **[REV]** evidence review written for policymakers (IZA World of Labor)
- **[COM]** expert commentary or blog post (not peer-reviewed)

---

## Read this first: four things that shape every finding below

1. **A tool can only remove a bias that is there.** Removing identity from an evaluation removes *whatever* the evaluator was doing with that identity. Where evaluators were penalising a group, blinding helps that group. Where they were favouring it, blinding takes that advantage away. Where they were neutral, blinding changes nothing. Reviews of the anonymous-application trials say this outright [5][8].
2. **"Bias" in this literature runs in both directions.** Several high-quality studies find evaluators favouring women [5][7][16][22], several find them favouring men [13][14][15][18][19][20], and some find each happening in different fields [14][18][22]. A fair reading has to report both.
3. **Many headline results come from one organisation, one occupation or one hypothetical exercise.** Field data from a single retail chain [13], one university [19], one technology company [15], one foundation [9], or a framed exercise that participants knew was not real [7] may not carry over to other settings.
4. **"Merit" has to be measured somehow, and the measure can itself carry bias.** Several studies show bias entering through how criteria are defined [14], how the rating scale is built [19], which words are rewarded [9][15], or how ratings are turned into rewards [17][20][21]. Hiding names does not touch any of these channels.

## KEY FINDINGS

### 1. Blind auditions: a famous claim whose statistics are disputed

**What the original paper claimed [1] [PR].**
- Using an individual fixed-effects design, the authors "find that the screen increases the probability a woman will be advanced and hired."
- **Estimates as reported:**
  - In preliminary rounds with no semifinal, the screen raised a woman's chance of advancing by about 11 percentage points.
  - In finals, it raised a woman's chance of winning by about 33 percentage points.
  - In semifinals, the effect "remains strongly negative", meaning women did *relatively worse* behind the screen.
  - The completely blind hiring estimate was positive (about 5 percentage points) but "not statistically significant".
- **The authors' own caveat:** "some of our estimates have large standard errors and there is one persistent effect in the opposite direction". Even so, "the weight of the evidence suggests" impartiality improved.
- **The figures most widely repeated:** the screen raised women's advancement "by 50 percent" in certain preliminary rounds, and blind auditions "can explain 30 percent" (elsewhere "about one-third") of the increase in the share of women among new hires [1].

**The statistical critique [2][3] [COM].**
- Pallesen notes that the paper's simplest comparison (Table 4) shows men doing *relatively better* in blind auditions [2]. He accepts that this table is observational and could be confounded.
- He argues that the within-person comparisons draw on very few orchestras (one to three per category) and that no difference in them is statistically significant [2].
- On the regression table, he finds one marginally significant estimate, which he says rises to p ≈ 0.33 once corrected for multiple testing [2].
- His conclusion is that the paper "presents no statistically significant evidence that blind auditions increase the chances of female applicants" [2].
- Gelman, a statistician, reviewed the paper in response. He wrote that "the punchline seems to be that the data are too noisy to form any strong conclusions". On the authors' remark that the point estimates are "economically significant", he wrote: "That's just what you get when you have a small sample and noisy data" [3].

**Fair reading.**
- Both sides agree on the core facts:
  - the paper itself reports large standard errors, one estimate in the opposite direction, and a non-significant result for the final hiring stage [1];
  - the popular "50 percent" figure is a point estimate from particular rounds, not a robust overall effect [1][2].
- The critique does *not* show that screens harmed women or had no effect. It shows that this dataset cannot establish the effect with confidence.
- Neither critique [2][3] is peer-reviewed. No published peer-reviewed re-analysis was located for this brief.

### 2. Anonymised CVs: helps when evaluators penalise a group, backfires when they favour it

- **Sweden, Gothenburg (field, difference-in-differences) [6] [PR]: helped women.** Anonymous applications "increased the chances of both women and individuals of non-Western origin of advancing to the interview stage". Women "experienced an increased probability of being offered a job". Ethnic-minority applicants gained interviews but not job offers.
- **France (randomised, 2010–2011) [4] [PR]: backfired for its main intended beneficiaries.** That group was ethnic-minority and deprived-neighbourhood applicants, not women.
  - Participating firms became "less likely to interview and hire minority candidates" with anonymous CVs.
  - The interview gap widened from 2.4 to 13 percentage points, and the hiring gap widened by 3.7 points [4] (J-PAL summary).
  - **Proposed mechanisms:**
    - *Self-selection*: firms that volunteered were already more favourable to minority candidates.
    - *Lost context*: anonymisation "prevents the attenuation of negative signals". Firms could no longer read weaker-looking records, such as interrupted work histories, in light of the adverse labour-market conditions those applicants face [4].
  - **Effect on women:** a summary of the earlier working-paper results reports that women gained callbacks with anonymous CVs "at least if they compete with male applicants". Roughly half the vacancies drew applicants of only one sex [5].
- **Australia (BETA, randomised framed exercise, 2016–17) [7] [GOV]: backfired for women and minorities, helped men.** With names visible, participants were "2.9% more likely to shortlist female candidates and 3.2% less likely to shortlist male applicants" than with de-identified CVs.
  - Gains from visible identity also applied to minority women (8.6%), minority men (5.8%) and, most strongly, to Indigenous women (22.2%).
  - De-identification would therefore be expected to *lower* women's and minorities' shortlisting chances and *raise* men's.
  - The report warns of "subject reactivity or scrutiny bias" because participants knew the exercise was hypothetical [7].
- **Germany (eight organisations, 2010–2011) [5] [PR]: mostly neutral, with one reversal for women.**
  - Under anonymous applications, "all applicant groups have the expected equal chances" of an interview in nearly all processes.
  - In one process studied in detail, women were favoured under standard applications. Anonymisation lowered their interview chance by about 9 percentage points relative to men, though the overall effect for women relative to the previous year was not significant. Migrants gained.
  - **The authors' conclusion:** anonymisation "can reduce discrimination—if discrimination is present beforehand". It has no effect where none exists, and "can even prevent the employer from applying measures such as affirmative action" [5].
- **Economist hiring (small randomised trial, summarised in [5]): backfired for women.** Women had a higher invitation probability than men with standard applications, and the difference vanished with anonymity. "The underrepresented gender is thus hurt by anonymous job applications" [5].
- **Blinding does not remove proxies [9] [WP].**
  - In blinded review of grant proposals, women still received "significantly lower scores", which reviewer characteristics, topics and prior quality measures could not explain.
  - The gap disappeared after controlling for how titles and descriptions were worded (use of "broad" versus "narrow" words).
  - Those wording features did not predict later innovative performance, while women produced more follow-on output after funding [9].
  - So a style-based signal that blinding leaves in place can disadvantage the better-performing group.
- **Overall assessments.**
  - The IZA review: anonymous applications "have the potential to reduce discrimination only when discrimination is high", "may simply postpone discrimination to later in the hiring process", and "may foil other positive measures" [8] [REV].
  - UK government-funded guidance rates CV anonymisation only "promising", not "effective". It advises trying it only where a group's share falls sharply between application and the CV sift [10] [GOV].

### 3. Structured interviews: the strongest validity evidence, with thinner direct evidence on gender

- **Validity [11][12] [PR].**
  - The classic meta-analysis found that "structured interviews were found to have higher validity than unstructured interviews", and that validity depends on the interview's content [11].
  - A 2022 re-analysis argues that earlier meta-analyses over-corrected for range restriction, lowering most validity estimates by .10–.20. Even so, "structured interviews emerged as the top-ranked selection procedure" [12].
- **Bias reduction.**
  - UK government-funded guidance rates structured interviews (the same questions in the same order, scored against criteria set in advance) as an "effective" action. It states that unstructured interviews "are more likely to allow unfair bias to creep in" [10] [GOV].
  - Experimental evidence on *how* structure might reduce bias is consistent with this:
    - fixing criteria before seeing who the candidate is eliminated gender discrimination in hiring judgements [14] [PR];
    - clear evidence of a candidate's high competence reduced gender-role bias [18] [PR].
- **Limits.**
  - The validity evidence [11][12] concerns predicting job performance. It is not a direct test of gender-specific bias, and no meta-analysis of gender gaps in structured versus unstructured interviews was verified for this brief (**UNVERIFIED** evidence gap).
  - Structure standardises the questions but cannot fix criteria that are themselves skewed [14][15].
  - Validity estimates are lower than once believed [12].

### 4. Performance and promotion reviews: the gaps sit in subjective "potential" and in turning ratings into rewards

- **Women rated higher on performance, lower on potential [13] [PR].**
  - At a large retailer, women "receive substantially lower potential ratings despite receiving higher performance ratings".
  - Potential ratings explain "approximately half of the gender promotion gap".
  - The lower potential ratings proved inaccurate: "women subsequently outperform male colleagues, both on average and on the margin of promotion".
  - The authors identify two mechanisms: strategic retention and stereotyping.
- **The pattern recurs across studies [16] [PR].** Across field studies, "females generally scored slightly higher than males" on job-performance ratings (mean d = −0.11), "although … ratings of promotion potential were higher for males".
- **Rewards diverge from ratings [17] [PR].** Across 93 studies of evaluations and 97 of rewards, sex differences in rewards (d = .56) were "14 times larger" than differences in performance evaluations (d = .04). Evaluation differences "did not explain reward differences". In highly prestigious occupations, women "performed equally, but were rewarded significantly lower".
- **Vague criteria let bias in, in both directions [14] [PR].**
  - Evaluators redefined what the job required to suit the credentials of whichever candidate matched the stereotype:
    - for police chief, criteria shifted to favour the male candidate;
    - for a women's studies professorship, they shifted to favour the female candidate.
  - "Commitment to hiring criteria prior to disclosure of the applicant's gender eliminated discrimination."
- **Written reviews [15] [PR].**
  - Men and women were "equally likely to be described as having technical ability".
  - Women were more often seen as "too aggressive", and men as "too soft".
  - "Taking charge" was linked to the highest ratings for men but not for women.
- **Design of the rating scale [19] [PR].**
  - In the most male-dominated fields at one university, the switch from a 10-point to a 6-point teaching-rating scale changed the size of the gender gap.
  - According to the authors' institution's summary, men had received perfect 10s 31.4% of the time and women 19.5%. On the 6-point scale, the shares receiving a perfect score were 41.2% for men and 41.7% for women.
  - In fields that were not male-dominated, ratings were about the same under both scales.
  - A survey experiment replicated the effect and linked it to stereotypes of "brilliance".
- **Accountability and transparency [21] [PR].** At one company, women, ethnic minorities and non-US-born employees initially received smaller rewards than comparable US-born white men with the same performance scores. That gap shrank after accountability and transparency procedures were introduced into the reward process.
- **"Meritocracy" branding can backfire [20] [PR].** In three experiments, when a company was "explicitly presented as meritocratic", managers awarded a male employee a larger bonus than an equally qualified female employee.
- **Self-assessments.** UK guidance places performance self-assessments among actions with "mixed results". It advises that self-scores not be shown to the manager until after the manager's own rating, because self-scores can anchor appraisal scores [10] [GOV].
- **Calibration committees.** No rigorous study of calibration panels as such was verified for this brief (**UNVERIFIED** evidence gap). The closest verified evidence is the accountability-and-transparency field study [21].

### 5. Evidence of bias favouring women or against men

- With names visible, Australian senior public servants favoured women and minority candidates, so blinding helped men [7].
- In the German trial and the economist-hiring trial, women were favoured under standard applications [5].
- In French teacher-accreditation exams, non-blind oral exams favoured women by about 10 percentile ranks in maths, physics and philosophy. They favoured men by 3–5 percentile ranks in literature and foreign languages. In both cases the bias favoured the sex under-represented in the field [22].
- Performance ratings in field studies slightly favour women on average [16].
- Laboratory meta-analysis found men preferred for male-dominated jobs, but "no strong preference for either gender" for female-dominated or integrated jobs [18].

## CONTESTED / UNCERTAIN POINTS

1. **Whether blind auditions raised women's hiring at all.** The original authors read the weight of noisy evidence as positive [1]. The critics read the same tables as showing no statistically significant effect [2][3]. Neither side has peer-reviewed new data. The best-supported statement is that the effect is *not established* by this study, not that it was absent.
2. **Whether pro-women findings in hypothetical exercises reflect real hiring.** The Australian result comes from a hypothetical exercise that participants knew was a study [7]. The economist trial was small [5]. The French oral-exam result is real but limited to one public examination system [22].
3. **Why anonymised CVs backfired in France.** The authors' explanation (self-selected, already-favourable firms that lost the ability to contextualise weaker records) is a strong inference from the data. Other firms might respond differently [4][8].
4. **What "potential" ratings measure.** One large study shows these ratings under-predict women's later performance [13]. It comes from one retail employer, and the two proposed mechanisms (strategic retention, stereotyping) may weigh differently elsewhere.
5. **Whether gaps reflect evaluator bias or real differences in the work.** Meta-analyses of field ratings cannot fully separate rater bias from genuine performance differences [16][17]. The experiments isolate bias [14][18][20] but use simulated decisions.
6. **Whether blinding can succeed when style differs by group.** Grant-review evidence suggests that wording and communication style carry gendered signals through anonymity [9]. This is a single working paper and is not peer-reviewed.
7. **How well results travel across countries and stages.** Anonymisation affects only the first screening stage. Its effect on offers is often unmeasured or null [5][6][8].

## WHEN IT HELPS WHOM / WHEN IT BACKFIRES

| Tool | Helped women | Helped men | Null or backfired | Why (best-supported mechanism) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blind auditions | Original estimates positive in some rounds [1] | Semifinal estimate favoured men behind the screen [1] | Overall effect not statistically established [1][2][3] | Removes sight of the candidate; data too noisy to size the effect |
| Anonymised CVs | Sweden: interviews and offers [6]; France: callbacks when competing with men [5] | Australia: +3.2% shortlisting for men when blinded [7]; economist trial [5] | France: minorities hurt [4]; Germany: mostly neutral [5] | Helps where evaluators penalised a group; backfires where they favoured it or used context to read records generously [4][5][8] |
| Blinding with style cues left in | n/a | Men scored higher in blinded grant review [9] | Backfired on a group that performed better afterwards [9] | Word choice carries gendered signals that blinding leaves in place [9] |
| Structured interviews and criteria fixed in advance | Removed pro-male criterion shifting in a male-typed job [14] | Removed pro-female criterion shifting in a female-typed job [14] | No verified backfire; direct gender evidence thin | Same questions and pre-set scoring limit after-the-fact rationalisation [10][11][12][14] |
| Rating-scale redesign | Closed the top-rating gap in male-dominated fields [19] | n/a | No effect where no gap existed [19] | Fewer scale points reduce the room for "brilliance" stereotypes in top scores [19] |
| Accountability and transparency in rewards | Shrank the reward gap at equal ratings [21] | n/a | Single-firm evidence [21] | Managers must justify reward decisions [21] |
| Declaring a culture "meritocratic" | n/a | Favoured men in bonuses [20] | Backfired on women [20] | Feeling objective may license bias [20] |

**The pattern, in plain terms:**
- Tools that **hide identity** are neutral in direction: they remove whichever bias exists. That can mean helping women, helping men, or removing a deliberate preference for an under-represented group [4][5][7][8].
- Tools that **fix and standardise criteria** in advance have the most consistent support for making judgements track merit for both sexes [10][11][12][14][19]. Direct evidence on gender gaps in structured interviews is still limited.

## FAIR COUNTERARGUMENTS

- **"Blinding is fair by definition, whatever the outcome."** If the aim is equal treatment, a tool that removes a preference for women [7] or for under-represented candidates [5] is working as designed. On this view, those results are a success for merit, not a backfire. The opposing view treats some identity-aware judgement as legitimate context [4][5]. Which framing is right is a value question the data cannot settle.
- **"Structure costs validity."** Standardised procedures may stop evaluators from using job-relevant judgement or recognising unusual talent. The authors of the German trial note that standardised forms leave "creative individuals" fewer chances to show their credentials [5]. The validity evidence, however, favours structure on average [11][12].
- **"Gaps in ratings may reflect real performance."** Field meta-analyses cannot rule this out [16][17]. The "potential" study, though, directly tested later outcomes and found women's lower potential ratings inaccurate [13].
- **"The orchestra critique is itself not peer-reviewed."** True [2][3]. Its central points restate standard errors and caveats printed in the original paper [1].
- **"Hypothetical studies overstate pro-women bias."** Participants who know they are observed may act in a socially desirable way, as the Australian report concedes [7]. The French exam data, however, come from real high-stakes evaluations and show the same "favour the under-represented sex" pattern [22].
- **"Diversity tools can undermine perceived legitimacy."** Evidence on how hidden or declared procedures affect perceived fairness was not verified for this brief (**UNVERIFIED**). The meritocracy-framing experiments show that labels alone do not guarantee fair outcomes [20].
