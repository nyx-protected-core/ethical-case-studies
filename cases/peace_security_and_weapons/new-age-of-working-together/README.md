# Could a new age of working together exist?

Date: 2026-11-19

## Summary

This research asks whether a world that stops fighting over religion and trade, spreads wealth to every region, and brings automation to Africa and poorer nations is possible. Each of the five areas comes out as possible, but only with major change, and none of it happens by default.

## The short answer

**Yes, it could exist, but not on the road the world is on now.** The evidence shows real progress. Poorer countries have been slowly catching up since about 2000. Fast internet and mobile money have created jobs and lifted households out of poverty in Africa. Global deals like the Montreal Protocol and the eradication of smallpox worked. The same evidence also shows that military spending is at a record high, aid just had its biggest fall on record, and the default economics of automation pull wealth toward the countries that already have it.

Past cooperation has worked on narrow, well-defined problems, with shared science, money for the weakest members, and enforceable promises. No precedent exists for a general global age that ends conflict over trade and belief and spreads wealth evenly. That would have to be built, one agreement at a time.

## Wealth: is it spreading?

Share of global personal wealth in 2025:

| Group | Share |
|---|---|
| Top 10% of adults | 75% |
| Top 1% | 37% |
| Bottom 50% | 2% |

The table shows each group's share of global personal wealth in 2025. These are modelled estimates from the World Inequality Report 2026, and the method is contested.

Gaps between countries are closing, but slowly. Peer-reviewed studies (Patel, Sandefur and Subramanian 2021; Kremer, Willis and You 2022) find that poorer countries have been catching up since the mid-1990s, and especially since 2000. At that pace, though, the average developing country would take about 170 years to close half the gap. In 1950, US income per head was 17 times India's. In 1990 it was nearly 30 times, and in 2017 about 9 times.

Where you are born matters most. Lakner and Milanovic estimate that equalising average incomes between countries would cut global inequality by about 77%. Equalising incomes within every country would cut it by only 23%.

Extreme poverty fell from about 2.3 billion people in 1990 to about 831 million in 2025, mainly because of growth in East and South Asia. This is a World Bank figure. Poverty counts depend on survey coverage and on where the poverty line is drawn, and governments and lenders both have reasons to show progress.

The tools that help are shrinking. Aid has a modest but real positive effect on growth (Clemens et al. 2012; Galiani et al. 2017). In 2025, though, donor aid fell 23.1% to $174.3 billion, the largest drop on record, according to OECD figures that donors themselves report. Remittances to poorer countries, about $685 billion in 2024, are now larger than foreign investment and aid combined.

## Automation and Africa

| Finding | Detail | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Fast internet created jobs | When undersea internet cables reached 12 African countries, employment, new firms and incomes rose, including for less-educated workers (Hjort and Poulsen 2019). | Works |
| Mobile money lifted households | Access to Kenya's M-PESA mobile money lifted about 194,000 households, roughly 2% of Kenyan households, out of extreme poverty (Suri and Jack 2016). | Works |
| The factory ladder is shorter | Countries now run out of industrialisation opportunities sooner and at lower incomes than early industrialisers did (Rodrik 2016). | Barrier |
| Rich-country robots cost jobs elsewhere | US robot use cut employment in Mexico by reducing Mexico's exports to the US (Faber 2020). | Barrier |
| Power comes first | About 600 million people in sub-Saharan Africa had no electricity in 2023, an estimate from the International Energy Agency. Unreliable power keeps firms small over the long run (Fried and Lagakos 2023). | Barrier |
| Learning lags behind enrolment | Across 164 countries, more children went to school but learning gains were limited (Angrist et al. 2021). | Barrier |

Light, networked technology is already spreading, and it measurably helps. Heavy automation is different. A peer-reviewed model (Alonso et al. 2022) shows that robots replacing labour pull capital and returns toward countries that are already rich, unless rules change. For automation to spread wealth to Africa, three things would have to change: industrial power would need to be reliable, schools would need to produce real learning, and new rules would need to decide who owns and taxes the machines. That last change is largely untested.

## War, religion and the peace dividend

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| World military spending, 2025 | $2,887 billion |
| Military spending as a share of world GDP | 2.5%, highest since 2009 |
| Donor aid, 2025 | $174.3 billion, down 23.1% |
| Military spending compared with aid | about 16.6 times |
| State-based conflicts, 2024 | 61 |
| Organized-violence deaths, 2024 | about 159,800 |

The spending figures come from SIPRI and the OECD, and the conflict counts from the Uppsala Conflict Data Program. Government-reported spending can be understated, and it is flagged as such.

Religion as a cause of conflict has grown. In one peer-reviewed dataset (RELAC), the share of conflicts involving religious issues rose from 3% in 1975 to 55% in 2015. How violent a conflict becomes depends on the kind of claim being made, not on the religious tradition itself.

Some things are proven to reduce war. After civil wars, peacekeeping cut the risk of war returning by more than 55% between 1947 and 1999, and by 84% after the Cold War (Fortna). A conflict with 2,500 battle deaths raises undernourishment, lowers life expectancy by about a year, and raises infant mortality by about 10% (Gates et al.). The money spent on war is money lost for development.

## Trade wars versus cooperation

Trade wars mostly hurt the country that starts them. In the 2018–19 US–China trade war, tariffs were passed almost entirely to US buyers. US consumers and firms that buy imports lost about $51 billion, and manufacturing jobs in the most exposed industries fell rather than rose, because input costs and retaliation outweighed the protection.

In the 1930s, the Smoot-Hawley tariff and the retaliation that followed were costly. US exports to countries that retaliated fell 28–32%. Even so, tariffs explain only part of that era's trade collapse.

Binding agreements do raise trade, but unevenly. Rich members gained more than poor ones. Some tools for poorer countries work in narrow ways. Aid for Trade spent on infrastructure helps, and compulsory licences have lowered drug prices. The institutions are stalled, though. The WTO's appeals body has been unable to rule since November 2020. Gains from a US trade-preference programme for Africa faded, and the poorest countries have never used compulsory licensing.

The main obstacle is political, not technical. The gains from cooperation are spread thinly, while the losses fall on concentrated places that vote.

## What past cooperation teaches

| Precedent | Detail | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Montreal Protocol (1987) | Every country has ratified it. Developing countries were given different timetables and money to cover their costs. Without it, ozone-depleting emissions in 2010 would have been 11–13 times the baseline. | Worked |
| Smallpox eradication | Smallpox was eradicated through shared science and campaigns that reached the last villages, with independent certification at the end. | Worked |
| Green Revolution | Over 8,000 modern crop varieties came from more than 400 public breeding programmes in over 100 countries. | Worked |
| European integration | On average it raised incomes, but one member in the study was made worse off. | Mixed |
| Marshall Plan | Its effect is still debated, and it covered only Western Europe, in a Cold War setting. It was never a universal system. | Limited |
| Targets-only climate treaties | They underperformed because their promises were short-lived and did not bind the largest future emitters. | Failed |

Cooperation works when the problem is technically solvable, the science is shared and checked, money covers the weakest members, and promises can be enforced. The Enlightenment is often cited here, but the historical record shows a growth of useful knowledge in the West, not a worldwide peace over religion or trade.

## What it would take

| Change | Why the evidence points to it |
|---|---|
| Move part of military spending into development | Military spending is now about 16.6 times aid. Peacekeeping is proven to stop wars from coming back. |
| Reliable industrial power for Africa | Machines need steady power before they need technology. |
| Schools that produce real learning | Technology spreads only as fast as people's skills. |
| New rules on who owns and taxes automation | Without them, the returns flow back to countries that are already rich. |
| Working trade rules and fair preferences | Binding rules raise trade, and poorer members need them to be enforced. |
| Global minimum taxes and debt relief | These tools work, but they are being diluted. |
| Narrow treaties with money and enforcement | This is the pattern behind every success in the record. |

## A 2050 scenario

**This is a scenario, not a forecast.** If the changes above were made, 2050 would not have one world government. It would have many narrow agreements, each with money and enforcement. Africa would have reliable power grids and better schools. Machines would be locally owned or taxed, so that their returns stayed in poorer nations. Peace would be held by peacekeeping, with part of military budgets moved to development. Gaps between countries would close much faster than the current 170-year pace. If those changes are not made, the record suggests islands of success, like a disease wiped out or a regional union formed, while wealth and technology stay concentrated.

## How this was researched

Five research notes, about 650 lines in total, cover wealth, automation, conflict, trade and precedents. Each conclusion rests on peer-reviewed studies. Official statistics from the World Bank, OECD, SIPRI and the International Energy Agency are used only as data, each with a note on how governments' reporting incentives could bias it. Think-tank and news sources are labelled secondary and never carry a conclusion. Following the rule for global topics, no Canadian data, research, or Canadian-affiliated authors are used, and several studies were dropped for that reason.

Known gaps: no peer-reviewed estimates of tariff rounds after 2024 were used, the current legal status of the US trade-preference programme for Africa was not checked, and direct evidence that rich-country robots cost jobs in African countries does not yet exist. The full notes and their reference lists are kept with the project files, and any claim here can be checked against them.
