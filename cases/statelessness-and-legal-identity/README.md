# Statelessness and legal identity: millions with no nationality, and how countries are ending it

*Case study, 2026-11-18*

## The ethical question

A stateless person is someone no country recognises as a national. Without nationality, people often cannot go to school past a certain age, work legally, open a bank account, travel, marry legally or get justice when they are wronged. This makes them easy targets for exploitation, including trafficking. Every state has the right to decide who its nationals are. But do states also have a duty to make sure no one is left with no nationality at all? And how can that duty be met fairly in every region?

## Scale, with real figures

- **At least 4.4 million people.** UNHCR estimated that 4.4 million people were stateless or of undetermined nationality at the end of 2024. That figure comes from data from only 101 countries; data were missing for just under half of all countries, so the true number is likely much higher (UNHCR, 2025, A/80/12).
- **Progress is real but slow.** In 2024, 47,200 people acquired or confirmed a nationality, about 1% of the known stateless population (UNHCR, 2025; UNHCR Global Report 2024). Over the 10-year #IBelong campaign (2014–2024), 613,100 stateless people and people of undetermined nationality gained a nationality (UNHCR, #IBelong campaign page).
- **The wider identity gap.** The World Bank estimates that just under 850 million people lacked official proof of identity in 2021, most of them in lower-income countries in Africa and South Asia (World Bank ID4D, 2022). Not all of them are stateless, but missing papers are often the first step toward statelessness.
- **Sex discrimination in law.** As of March 2025, UNHCR and UN Women found that 24 countries, across nearly every region, still deny women the same right as men to pass their nationality to their children (UNHCR and UN Women, 2025). The UN Special Rapporteur on violence against women has called sex-based discrimination in nationality laws one of the major causes of statelessness (OHCHR, 2023).

## Root causes

1. **Gaps and conflicts between nationality laws**, for example when a child born in one country cannot get the nationality of either that country or a parent's country (Weissbrodt and Collins, 2006).
2. **Discrimination by ethnicity, religion or sex** in who is counted as a national (Weissbrodt and Collins, 2006; UNHCR and UN Women, 2025).
3. **State succession and redrawn borders**, such as the break-up of the Soviet Union, which left many people with undetermined nationality (UNHCR #IBelong report, 2024).
4. **Weak civil registration.** Births that are never registered leave people unable to prove where they were born or who their parents are (World Bank ID4D, 2022).
5. **Paper citizenship without real rights.** Kingston (2014) argues that statelessness should also be understood as a lack of *functioning* citizenship: some people hold a nationality on paper but cannot use the rights it should bring.

## What has worked

- **Whole-country solutions in Central Asia.** Kyrgyzstan became the first country to resolve all known cases of statelessness, and Turkmenistan later did the same (UNHCR, 2024 and 2025).
- **Group recognition in Africa.** Kenya granted nationality to members of the Makonde, Shona and Pemba minorities (UNHCR #IBelong report, 2024). A joint UNHCR and World Bank study found that nationality for the Shona community was followed by less discrimination, better access to finance, more job security and better health care (UNHCR Global Report 2024).
- **The largest single step yet, in Southeast Asia.** On 29 October 2024 Thailand's cabinet approved, in principle, a faster route to permanent residence for about 335,000 long-term residents and members of recognised ethnic minorities, and to Thai nationality for more than 142,000 of their children born in Thailand. That covers most of the nearly 600,000 people registered as stateless in the country (UNHCR Asia Pacific, 2024). The government said that reviewing these cases one by one could have taken up to 44 years (Royal Thai Government Public Relations Department, 2024).
- **Legal reform elsewhere.** Malaysia adopted a constitutional amendment so that women can pass nationality to children born abroad on the same basis as men, and reforms were reported in Kazakhstan, Montenegro, Japan, Georgia, Colombia and Mali (UNHCR Global Report 2024).
- **Treaties and coalitions.** Since 2010 there have been 77 new accessions to the 1954 and 1961 UN statelessness conventions. In October 2024, UNHCR launched the Global Alliance to End Statelessness, which has more than 140 members from governments, intergovernmental bodies, UN partners and stateless-led organisations (UNHCR, 2024 and 2025).

## The link to trafficking

The claim that statelessness raises the risk of trafficking is widely made but has rarely been tested. A study by Tilburg University researchers and the Institute on Statelessness and Inclusion surveyed and interviewed hill tribe communities in northern Thailand (Rijken, van Waas, Gramatikov and Brennan, 2015). It found that the consequences of statelessness overlap closely with known trafficking risk factors: poverty, fewer job options, poor access to education and health care, discrimination and corruption. Respondents most often named fewer job opportunities (70%) and poverty (59%) as consequences of having no nationality. Stateless people also had fewer ways to cope with a crisis, which can push them toward risky migration. This is the main reason ending statelessness belongs in any serious anti-trafficking plan.

## Ethical tensions

- **Sovereignty versus the right to a nationality.** States decide who belongs, yet the Universal Declaration of Human Rights says everyone has the right to a nationality. Solutions have to respect both.
- **Security screening versus speed.** Thailand's approach requires good conduct and no links to another nationality. Checks build public trust, but heavy paperwork can shut out the poorest and least documented.
- **Permanent residence first, or nationality at once?** A staged route is easier to agree politically but leaves adults waiting years for full rights.
- **Data collection risks.** Registering stateless people is the first step to helping them, but registers can be misused for deportation or control without strong data protection (see this collection's case on digital surveillance and privacy).
- **Burden sharing.** Some of the largest stateless populations are in middle-income countries with limited budgets; richer states that do little to help still benefit from a more stable world.

## Original proposals (this study's own)

These are proposals of this study, not established policy.

1. **A "no one born stateless" birth guarantee.** Every country adopts a simple safeguard: a child born on its territory who would otherwise be stateless gets its nationality automatically, checked at birth registration rather than years later. Pair this with free, mobile birth registration in remote areas, like Thailand's mobile registration days for the Moken people.
2. **A results-based statelessness fund.** Donors from every region, including Gulf states, China, India, Brazil, Türkiye and the EU, pay into a pool that gives middle- and low-income countries a fixed grant for each person who is confirmed as a national and receives working ID. Payments are made only after an independent check, so money follows real results.
3. **Equal nationality reform pact.** The 24 countries that still treat women and men differently when passing on nationality are offered technical legal help and peer support from countries in their own region that have already reformed. Malaysia's recent amendment is one example. Reform then comes from peers rather than outside pressure.
4. **Anti-trafficking screening at registration.** When stateless people register, trained staff also offer confidential screening for trafficking and labour exploitation and refer people to help, without any link to immigration enforcement.
5. **Status-to-work bridge.** People granted residence or nationality get fast access to work permits, bank accounts and recognised skills certificates within weeks, so the new status quickly opens legal work.

## Risks

- Registration without real data protection can expose people to harm.
- Results-based payments can create incentives to count people who already had papers; audits must guard against this.
- Political backlash in host countries may stall reforms, especially when statelessness is tied to ethnic or border disputes.
- Staged status can become permanent second-class status if the second step never comes.

## Recommendation

Make ending statelessness a shared global goal with local ownership. Every state should guarantee that no child is born stateless, end sex discrimination in nationality laws, and make birth registration free and universal. Countries with large stateless populations should follow tested models, such as group recognition in Kenya, whole-country resolution in Kyrgyzstan and Turkmenistan, and Thailand's mass fast-track route. The international community, with real contributions from every region, should fund this on a results basis. Because statelessness and trafficking risk overlap so closely, nationality work should be built into anti-trafficking strategies.
