# Recruitment Fees and Debt Bondage: The Employer Pays Principle, National Rules, Corporate Repayment and How to Verify It

*Revisions (18 November 2026).* Revised after an independent review. The changes correct facts, fill gaps and tighten wording. The review is not named here.

## 1. Introduction: how a fee becomes a debt, and a debt becomes a trap

By the Colombo Process's estimate, over 2.5 million Asian workers leave their countries every year under contract to work abroad [27]. Many find their jobs through recruiters. In many migration corridors, the worker, not the employer, pays for that recruitment. The bill covers the recruiter's service charge, and often also the visa, the air ticket, medical tests, training, and "facilitation" payments that never appear on a receipt.

Few low-wage workers have that money in cash. So they borrow it from relatives, from moneylenders, or from the recruiter, or they sell or pledge land, livestock or jewellery. An IHRB study of migrant workers in Malaysia and Qatar found that 10 of the 24 workers interviewed had borrowed from informal lenders at annual interest rates of 36 to 72 per cent, while those who reached a bank paid 12 to 24 per cent [31].

That debt changes the worker's position at the destination. A worker who owes several months' wages cannot afford to quit a bad job, complain about unpaid overtime, or go home early. If the employer or recruiter also holds the worker's passport, controls the visa, or adds new charges, the debt becomes a tool for keeping the worker in place. This is **debt bondage**: work demanded to repay a debt, or a debt manipulated to force work or conditions the person would otherwise refuse [1].

This case looks at:

1. how big the problem is;
2. the international principles that say workers should not pay;
3. national rules in origin and destination countries, and how well they are enforced;
4. what companies have repaid to workers, and how complete those repayments are;
5. practical methods to find fees and verify repayment, with pseudocode;
6. the strongest counterarguments; and
7. recommendations ranked by the strength of evidence.

A note on framing. The main Asia–Gulf corridors are shaped by decisions in both origin and destination countries, and by the regional and multilateral bodies they have built together. Most of the reforms described here were designed and carried out by those governments themselves, often with technical support from the ILO and IOM. The case treats them that way.

### 1.1 Scale: debt bondage within forced labour

The most recent global estimates come from the ILO, Walk Free and IOM. They refer to 2021 and were published in 2022 [1].

| Measure | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| People in forced labour on any given day, 2021 | **27.6 million** | [1] |
| Share of adults in what the report calls "forced labour exploitation" who are in debt bondage | **20.9%**, about one in five (19.7% of men, 22.0% of women) | [1], Figure 13 |
| Debt bondage share by sector | Mining and quarrying **43.1%**, agriculture **31.0%**, construction **27.4%**, services excluding domestic work 19.4%, domestic work 19.3%, manufacturing 14.0% | [1], Figure 13 |
| Forced labour prevalence, adult migrant workers vs. non-migrant workers | Nearly **14 per 1,000** migrant workers vs **4.1 per 1,000** non-migrants, more than three times higher | [1] |

The report links these figures directly to recruitment. It says that while some debt bondage relates to feudal forms, "many are linked to extortionate fees and related costs paid by workers – particularly migrant workers – during recruitment" [1].

### 1.2 Scale: what workers pay, measured in months of wages

The UN's Sustainable Development Goals include an indicator on exactly this. **SDG indicator 10.7.1** is the "recruitment cost borne by employee as a proportion of monthly income earned in country of destination". The ILO and the World Bank are its joint custodians. It is expressed in **months of earnings**: how many months of destination pay it takes the worker to earn back what they paid to get the job. Costs and earnings must come from the same worker, for the first job in the most recent destination country, within roughly the previous three years [2][3].

Surveys run by the ILO and the World Bank's Global Knowledge Partnership on Migration and Development (KNOMAD) in 2014–15 produced the first comparable corridor figures. Amounts below are in US dollars, from surveys in 2014–15; the cited slide does not state a price-year basis [4]. The table is a selection of 11 of that slide's 15 corridor rows.

| Origin → destination | Average total recruitment cost (US$) | Months of destination earnings |
|---|---|---|
| Pakistan → Saudi Arabia | 4,395 | **10.6** |
| Bangladesh → Kuwait | 3,136 | **9.0** |
| Pakistan → United Arab Emirates | 2,351 | 7.2 |
| Ethiopia → Saudi Arabia | 991 | 3.6 |
| Nepal → Qatar | 1,054 | 3.3 |
| India → Kuwait | 1,248 | 2.5 |
| India → Qatar | 1,149 | 2.0 |
| Viet Nam → Republic of Korea | 1,582 | 1.3 |
| Philippines → Qatar | 480 | 1.1 |
| Sri Lanka → Kuwait | 319 | 0.9 |
| Bulgaria → Spain | 201 | 0.2 |

Two points stand out. First, the cost of the *same job market* varies hugely by origin. The ILO's global study notes that average costs to take up work in Saudi Arabia ranged from US$309 for workers from the Philippines to US$4,460 for workers from Pakistan [9]. This case's interpretation is that the spread may reflect bargaining power and regulation in the corridor, not only the real cost of matching a worker to a job. The same study offers a competing explanation: costs workers are willing to incur also depend on what they expect to earn, and on that ratio Pakistan was 10.5 months, India 4.5 and the Philippines 0.7 [9]. Second, low-cost corridors include Philippines → Qatar (1.1 months) and the Republic of Korea's Employment Permit System (about 1.2–1.3 months), both of which are heavily regulated, and also Sri Lanka → Kuwait (0.9 months) and the Spain corridors (Bulgaria 0.2, Morocco 0.3 and Ecuador 0.8 months) [4]. Regulation is one pattern, not the only one.

More recent national data show that the problem has not gone away. Bangladesh's government Cost of Migration Survey 2020, which sampled 8,000 migrant households, found that workers paid on average **417,000 taka (about US$5,000)**, roughly **17 months** of average net earnings at the destination. Men paid more than four times what women paid (US$5,664 vs US$1,201), and workers going to Saudi Arabia paid US$5,236 on average [5]. The definition of earnings differs from the 2014–15 surveys (see [5]), so the figures should not be compared directly, but the scale is clear.

## 2. The principles: who should pay

International standards are consistent on the core point: **workers should not pay for their own recruitment**.

### 2.1 ILO Private Employment Agencies Convention, 1997 (No. 181), Article 7

> "1. Private employment agencies shall not charge directly or indirectly, in whole or in part, any fees or costs to workers." [6]

Article 7(2) lets a government authorize exceptions for certain categories of workers and specified services, "in the interest of the workers concerned" and after consulting employers' and workers' organizations. Under Article 7(3), a government that does so must report the exceptions and its reasons to the ILO [6]. The convention therefore sets a clear default, no fees, but leaves room that many governments have used.

### 2.2 Protocol of 2014 to the Forced Labour Convention (P029) and Recommendation No. 203

The Protocol was adopted in Geneva on 11 June 2014. Article 2(d) requires measures for "protecting persons, particularly migrant workers, from possible abusive and fraudulent practices during the recruitment and placement process" [7]. The accompanying Recommendation No. 203 is more specific. It calls for coordinated efforts by governments "with those of other States" to "regulate, license and monitor labour recruiters and employment agencies and eliminate the charging of recruitment fees to workers to prevent debt bondage" (para. 4(i)). It lists "eliminating the charging of recruitment fees to workers" first among measures against abusive recruiters (para. 8(a)) [7]. The Recommendation's own wording frames this as cooperation between states.

### 2.3 ILO General Principles and Operational Guidelines for Fair Recruitment, and the Definition of Recruitment Fees and Related Costs (published together, 2019)

The General Principles were adopted by a Meeting of Experts on Fair Recruitment in Geneva in September 2016 [8]. General Principle 7 reads:

> "No recruitment fees or related costs should be charged to, or otherwise borne by, workers or jobseekers." [8]

The 2019 definition closes common loopholes [8]:

- Fees and costs count "regardless of the manner, timing or location of their imposition or collection" (para. 6).
- They "should not be collected directly or indirectly, such as through deductions from wages and benefits" (para. 7).
- **Recruitment fees** include payments to recruiters, payments for recruitment to work for a third party, payments in direct recruitment by the employer, and "payments required to recover recruitment fees from workers" (para. 9).
- **Related costs** include medical tests, insurance (including enrolment in migrant welfare funds), skills tests, training and orientation, equipment, travel and lodging, and administrative costs such as visas, passports, background checks, exit clearances and work permits (para. 12). The list is "not exhaustive" (para. 13).

### 2.4 The Dhaka Principles and the Employer Pays Principle

The Dhaka Principles for Migration with Dignity are a set of principles for the responsible recruitment and employment of migrant workers, coordinated by the Institute for Human Rights and Business (IHRB). Principle 1 is "No fees are charged to migrant workers – The Employer Pays Principle": "The employer should bear the full costs of recruitment and placement. Migrant workers are not charged any fees for recruitment or placement" [10].

IHRB describes the **Employer Pays Principle** as "an employer commitment to pay the full costs associated with recruitment". It is endorsed by the Leadership Group for Responsible Recruitment, a group of companies and expert organizations. In IHRB's words, it is "a commitment to ensure that no worker should pay for a job" [11].

### 2.5 IOM's IRIS Standard

IOM's International Recruitment Integrity System (IRIS) Standard sets out what ethical recruitment means for labour recruiters and how they can show compliance. It has two overarching principles and five specific ones. The first specific principle is **"Prohibition of Recruitment Fees to Jobseekers"**. The others cover freedom of movement, transparency of terms, confidentiality and data protection, and access to remedy [12].

### 2.6 What the principles leave open

The principles agree on *who should pay*. They say less about three practical questions that decide whether workers are actually protected:

- **Detection:** how to find out what a worker really paid when receipts are missing or false.
- **Remediation:** how much to repay, including interest on loans, and how fast.
- **Allocation:** who bears the cost when a fee was taken by an informal sub-agent in a village, far from the employer.

Sections 4 and 5 deal with these.

## 3. National rules and how well they are enforced

The ILO's 2020 global comparative study reviewed 90 countries' laws and policies on recruitment fees [9]. It found three ways of regulating: a general statement allowing fees, a **cap** on what a worker may be charged, or an itemized list of which costs fall on the employer and which on the worker. Origin countries in Asia have mostly chosen caps or itemized lists. Destination countries in the Arab States mostly prohibit charging workers, but say little about costs charged in the origin country [9].

### 3.1 Origin countries

**Philippines.** Under the 2016 Revised Rules and Regulations governing land-based overseas workers, a licensed agency may charge a placement fee of up to **one month's basic salary** stated in the approved contract. No fee may be charged to **domestic workers**, to seafarers, or to workers going to countries where collecting recruitment fees is prohibited [9]. The study counts at least 16 itemized cost categories in Philippine rules, more than any other country it reviewed [9].

*Enforcement evidence.* The 2014–15 KNOMAD/ILO surveys found the Philippines → Qatar corridor at **1.1 months** of earnings, among the lowest of the Gulf corridors surveyed; only Sri Lanka → Kuwait, at 0.9 months, was lower [4]. That is consistent with tighter regulation, but it is not zero. The surveys did not isolate the effect of the rules from other factors, so this is association, not proof.

**Nepal: "free visa, free ticket" (2015).** In July 2015, Nepal's government adopted a ministerial policy for seven destination countries (Malaysia and six Gulf states). Employers must pay for the visa and the air ticket, and recruitment agencies may charge workers at most **NPR 10,000** (about US$100) as a service charge, and only if the employer has not paid the agency's service fee [9][13]. Nepal has also written employer-pays terms into bilateral agreements. According to the ILO, the agreements and memorandums with Malaysia, Jordan, Mauritius and the UAE state that the employer covers recruitment costs, and the 2005 agreement with Qatar puts travel costs on the employer [13].

*Enforcement evidence.* The ILO's 2021 country profile says that reports by Nepal's National Human Rights Commission, the Ministry of Labour, Employment and Social Security and a 2017 government Gulf visit all found "shortcomings in the implementation of this policy". Problems included difficulty monitoring payments between worker and agency, failure to keep receipts, and "forged receipts with the authorized charge of NPR 10,000". The profile concludes that "migrant workers continue to pay high fees" [13]. It also notes that in 2019 Nepal's courts ordered the government to enforce the policy effectively, and that compliance varies by corridor: an ILO/Tufts study found the Nepal → Jordan corridor had applied the no-fee principle "fairly effectively", apart from some related costs such as passports [13].

Peer-reviewed work from Nepal supports this picture. A 2022 study citing a Nepal Rastra Bank survey reports that more than three quarters of migrants knew about the policy, yet only about a third paid no more than the permitted amount and about a quarter paid more than NPR 50,000 [14]. A 2024 qualitative study of 55 workers who had gone to the Gulf found that, although the policy reduced direct charges, "intermediaries and other indirect costs have offset this reduction", and that longer processing times after 2015 added new costs [15].

**Indonesia: zero placement cost for listed occupations.** Indonesia's Law No. 18 of 2017 on the Protection of Indonesian Migrant Workers provides, in Article 30, that migrant workers cannot be charged placement costs, apart from costs such as their national identity card, passport, medical check and competency certificate [9]. In July 2020, the national migrant worker protection agency (BP2MI) issued Regulation No. 9 of 2020 to put this into effect [16]. It:

- lists 14 cost items that the worker may not be charged, including tickets, visa, contract legalization, training, agency fees, police certificate, social security, medical checks, local transport and accommodation (Art. 3(2));
- applies this to ten occupations, mainly household and care work, cooks, family drivers, gardeners, cleaners, plantation workers and migrant fishing crew (Art. 3(3));
- assigns most items to the employer, and training and competency certification to regional governments (Art. 3(4)–(5));
- bans loans imposed unilaterally on workers or their families as a way of paying placement costs where these lead to wage deductions abroad (Art. 4) [16].

*Enforcement evidence.* We found no official evaluation of how fully this regulation is applied. A 2026 peer-reviewed qualitative study of recruitment agents, regulators and NGOs in Singapore and Indonesia describes "fly now, pay later" systems in which migrant domestic workers build up debt from their home villages onwards and repay it through salary deductions at the destination [17]. That study does not measure compliance with the 2020 regulation, so it shows the risk that remains, not the size of any gap.

**Bangladesh.** We could not verify the current text of Bangladesh's fee rules from a primary or official source in time for this case, so we report only outcomes. Bangladesh has some of the highest measured costs: 9.0 months of earnings for Bangladesh → Kuwait in 2014–15 [4], and about 17 months in the government's 2020 survey [5]. Bangladesh is also the origin side of the best-evaluated fair recruitment pilot (Section 3.2).

### 3.2 Destination countries

**Qatar.** Qatar's Labour Law (No. 14 of 2004) prohibits charging workers recruitment fees, expenses or any other costs. For domestic workers, covered by Law No. 15 of 2017, employers are also prohibited from deducting recruitment fees from pay. Entry, exit and residence are governed by Law No. 21 of 2015 [18]. After a complaint to the ILO Governing Body in 2014 and a high-level tripartite mission in 2016, the Governing Body endorsed a programme of cooperation with Qatar in 2017, and the ILO opened an office in Doha in April 2018 [36]. Since then, according to the ILO's November 2022 progress report [19]:

- **Exit permits:** since Law No. 13 of 2018 and a 2019 ministerial decision, most migrant workers can leave the country without their employer's approval.
- **Changing jobs:** legislation adopted in September 2020 removed the employer's permission requirement for changing jobs. Between November 2020 and August 2022, 348,455 of 532,290 applications to change jobs were approved.
- **Minimum wage:** when the minimum wage law came into force in March 2021, more than 280,000 workers (about 13% of the private sector workforce) got a pay rise to the new minimum.
- **Wage Protection System:** 67,128 companies were registered by August 2022, and 913 showed violations.
- **Workers' Support and Insurance Fund:** it had paid out **QAR 1,165,316,181 (over US$320 million)** in unpaid wages and benefits by 30 September 2022.
- **Recruiter licensing:** the Ministry revoked 54 recruitment agency licences in the first nine months of 2022.
- **Qatar Visa Centres:** these were set up in origin countries (Bangladesh, India, Nepal, Pakistan, the Philippines and Sri Lanka) to handle biometric enrolment, medical checks and the signing of an electronic employment contract before departure, free of charge to workers. This is a joint origin–destination arrangement meant to prevent contract substitution [9][19].

*Enforcement evidence.* The ILO is candid that gaps remain. Fair recruitment tools are "still a challenge" to bring to scale. Some employers retaliate against workers who want to change jobs, and non-payment of wages remains the main cause of complaints [1][19]. The best causal evidence on fees comes from a fair recruitment pilot in Qatar's construction sector, launched in 2018 and evaluated over 2017–2020, run by a Qatari contractor and its recruitment partners with Qatar's labour ministry and the ILO. Workers were recruited from Bangladesh. According to the ILO's summary of the Tufts University impact assessment [20]:

| | Recruited before the no-fee policy (adopted January 2018) | Recruited after the no-fee policy, before the pilot | Recruited after the pilot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average fees and related costs paid by the worker | **US$3,408** (about 11.7 months of wages) | US$669 (about 2.7 months) | **US$300** (about 1 month) |
| Had no migration-related debt | 45% | 90% | 93% |
| Contract changed during migration | 35% | 17% | 8% |

The peer-reviewed evaluation (n = 598) found that workers recruited under the intervention paid less in fees, felt less obligation to migrate, worked fewer hours, reported better relations with supervisors and better mental health, and were more interested in migrating again. Interestingly, job satisfaction and trust in the employer were *higher* among conventionally recruited workers. The authors read this as post-decision dissonance: people who have paid a lot tend to justify the choice to themselves [21].

**Qatar's Universal Reimbursement Scheme.** The Supreme Committee for Delivery & Legacy, the Qatari body responsible for the 2022 World Cup projects, created a scheme that moved the burden of proof from the worker to the employer, because most workers cannot prove what they paid. By December 2021, **266 contractors had agreed to reimburse about QAR 103.95 million to 49,286 workers over 36 months, and QAR 82.35 million had been paid**. Eleven contractors extended the scheme to 18,066 workers outside the Committee's projects [22]. At the fixed exchange rate of QAR 3.64 per US dollar, that is roughly US$28.6 million pledged and US$22.6 million paid (our conversion).

**United Arab Emirates.** Under Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021 on labour relations, an employer may not charge a worker, or collect from them, recruitment and employment costs, "either directly or indirectly". Recruiters of domestic workers are also prohibited from taking any commission from the worker. Employers may not keep workers' passports. Wages must be paid through the **Wage Protection System (WPS)** or another system approved by the labour ministry [23].

*Enforcement evidence.* We found no official evaluation of the fee ban itself. There is strong peer-reviewed evidence on an earlier mobility reform, though. In January 2011, the UAE allowed migrant workers to move to a new employer at the end of their contract without the old employer's approval. Using monthly earnings data, a study in the *Journal of Political Economy* found that real earnings after contract expiry rose by more than 10%. Exits from the country fell. Before the reform, the authors estimate, firms paid incumbent workers about 51% of their marginal product; afterwards this rose to as much as 72%. However, firms then hired fewer new migrants and paid them lower starting wages [24]. Section 6 returns to this trade-off.

**Saudi Arabia.** On 4 November 2020, the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development announced a **Labor Reform Initiative**, which came into effect on 14 March 2021. It lets expatriate private-sector workers change employers when their contract ends without the employer's consent, and during the contract subject to notice and conditions. It also lets them apply for exit and re-entry visas and final exit visas themselves, with the employer notified electronically. The ministry describes it as complementing the Wage Protection System and digital documentation of contracts [25]. We found no official evaluation of its effect on recruitment fees.

### 3.3 Regional and multilateral cooperation

- **Colombo Process.** This is a regional consultative process on overseas employment and contract labour run by Asian countries of origin. Its members are Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Cambodia, China, India, Indonesia, Nepal, Pakistan, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Viet Nam. It has a thematic working group on "Fostering Ethical Recruitment Practices", which Bangladesh convened in 2021 [27].
- **Abu Dhabi Dialogue.** In January 2008, ministers from 20 countries of origin and destination adopted the Abu Dhabi Declaration: Afghanistan, Bahrain, Bangladesh, China, India, Indonesia, Kuwait, Malaysia, Nepal, Oman, Pakistan, the Philippines, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Thailand, the UAE, Viet Nam and Yemen. The declaration "recognizes the joint responsibility of countries of origin and destination to work to prevent illegal recruitment practices and to promote transparent recruitment and employment policies" [26].
- **Bilateral agreements** such as Nepal's with Qatar, Malaysia, Jordan, Mauritius and the UAE now commonly include employer-pays clauses [13]. Joint infrastructure such as Qatar's visa centres in origin countries puts parts of the recruitment process under shared control [9][19].
- **ILO and IOM programmes** provide technical support to both sides: the ILO–Qatar programme [19], the fair recruitment pilots [20], and IRIS [12].

### 3.4 What the enforcement evidence says overall

1. **Rules on paper are now widespread; compliance is not.** Nepal is the clearest documented case of a cap that workers routinely pay above, with false receipts used to hide it [13][14][15].
2. **The clearest reductions came where someone took active responsibility.** In the Bangladesh → Qatar pilot and Qatar's reimbursement scheme, an employer or programme checked what workers had actually paid instead of relying on the rule alone [20][22].
3. **Destination-side mobility reforms raise wages for workers already in the country**, but may shift costs onto new arrivals [24].
4. **Rigorous evaluations are rare.** For most of the reforms above, including Indonesia's 2020 regulation, the UAE's 2021 fee ban and Saudi Arabia's 2021 initiative, we found no published official or peer-reviewed evaluation of their effect on fees.

## 4. Corporate fee-refund remediation

### 4.1 Commitments

**Responsible Business Alliance (RBA).** The RBA Code of Conduct, version 8.0, took effect on 1 January 2024. Members must require their next-tier suppliers to acknowledge and implement it [32]. A separate RBA "Definition of Fees" took effect on 1 January 2021 [28]. The RBA says its Code "includes a requirement that no worker should pay recruitment fees or costs for employment". It dates its "no-fees" policy to 2015 in one statement [29] and to 2016 in another [30]. The difference may be adoption versus entry into force, but we could not confirm this. An ILO brief describes the requirement as in place "since 2015" [32].

**AAFA/FLA Apparel & Footwear Industry Commitment to Responsible Recruitment.** On 22 October 2018, 123 apparel and footwear companies signed this commitment, developed by the American Apparel & Footwear Association and the Fair Labor Association. Signatories agreed to put it into their social compliance standards by 31 December 2019 and to report on implementation [33]. The commitment was relaunched on 28 March 2023. It commits signatories to work with suppliers so that "no workers pay for their job", workers "receive a timely refund of fees and costs paid", workers keep control of their travel documents, and workers are told their basic employment terms, in a language they understand, before leaving home [33].

### 4.2 Reported repayments (as stated by the bodies themselves)

| Body | Figure as reported | Period | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| RBA member companies and their suppliers | About **US$50.5 million** to close to **54,000 workers** | Since 2009, as of January 2021 | ILO brief describing the RBA [32] |
| RBA | Helped drive reimbursement of "over **$60 million**" | "In the last few years alone" (stated 2026) | RBA event notice [30] |
| One electronics supplier in Thailand (RBA case) | About **US$4.99 million** to **10,570** current workers; a further **US$1.75 million** set aside for **3,714** workers who resigned between 2016 and 2019 | Announced December 2019 | RBA [29] |
| One technology company's suppliers | More than **US$34.5 million** to more than **37,700** supplier employees | Since 2008 | Company report [34] |
| One retailer's Malaysian supplier | About **AUD 734,000** to **230** workers (98% of those identified) | Identified in 2021 | IOM/ILO, citing the company's 2023 statement [35] |
| Qatar Universal Reimbursement Scheme | **QAR 82.35 million** paid of about QAR 103.95 million pledged, for **49,286** workers | To December 2021 | Supreme Committee [22] |

**Caution on adding these up.** The figures overlap. A company's supplier repayments may also be counted in an industry body's totals if the company or its suppliers belong to that body, and the sources do not say whether they do. The RBA's "$60 million" figure gives no start date. None of these figures has been independently audited in a public document we could find. They are best read as the bodies' own statements of scale.

### 4.3 How complete are repayments?

The most detailed review we found is a 2026 paper by IOM and the ILO on repayment practice in Asia since 2019 [35]. Its main findings:

- **Repayments are often averages, not individual amounts.** Many companies repay each group of workers (for example, by nationality and recruitment year) the group's average fee. This is quicker and helps workers who cannot remember or prove what they paid, but it under-repays those who paid more than average. Some civil society groups report that third parties sometimes advise workers to accept less than they paid [35].
- **Inflation and lost opportunity are rarely included, and interest is not always clear.** The most detailed public guidance, from the consultancy Impactt, sets as a *minimum* that repayment include interest on loans and exchange-rate effects. As *good practice* it adds inflation and about 2% for lost opportunity cost. The review says it is "not clear" how far companies account for exchange rates and inflation, and that companies "largely see" the opportunity-cost addition "as aspirational" [35]. The RBA's own guidance is mostly restricted to members [35]. We found no public RBA or IHRB formula for interest, so the pseudocode in Section 5 treats interest as a parameter.
- **Former, subcontracted, transfer and "walk-in" workers are often missed.** Eligibility is commonly tied to who was on site when the problem was found. Former workers who have gone home are hard to trace, and subcontracted workers are "the exception rather than the norm" [35]. In the Thai electronics case, 3,714 former workers had not been repaid at the time of the announcement [29].
- **Single complaints rarely trigger a wider check.** A worker who complains may be repaid individually, with "no further action" and no review of whether the practice was systemic [35].
- **Instalments can extend the bondage.** Workers waiting for later instalments may feel unable to leave. Workers who expected a larger or faster repayment sometimes take on new loans [35].
- **Workers may not know what the money was for.** In one IOM validation, workers thought a repayment was a bonus, so a later audit could again record unrepaid fees [35].

IHRB's 2017 review of company remediation pointed to the root cause: companies and suppliers benefit indirectly when workers pay for their own recruitment, and "an absence of recruitment fee receipts, the threat of reprisals and the ineffectiveness of company mechanisms" mean that debt often goes undetected [31].

## 5. Verification methods

Repayment is only as good as the evidence of what was paid. No single source is reliable. Receipts are often missing or false [13]. Worker memory fades and is affected by fear. Company documents describe policy, not practice. The IOM/ILO review puts it plainly: worker testimony should be treated as the primary evidence, and "the burden of proof is on the employer" to show that it paid the costs [35]. The methods below are meant to be used together.

### 5.1 Worker surveys

- **Sample properly.** Use a representative sample stratified by nationality, gender, recruitment year, recruiter and origin location. Impactt's guidance sets a minimum of 20% of each group; as best practice it interviews everyone in groups of 40 or fewer (and in workforces of 100 or fewer) and at least 40 workers in larger groups. CGF/AIM-Progress guidance sets a minimum of 40–45% [35]. For corridor-level statistics, the ILO/World Bank SDG 10.7.1 guidelines prefer household-based surveys, oversampling the villages that send the most migrants. Establishment surveys, administrative records, or traveller surveys at departure or return can complement them or replace them where no suitable household survey exists [3].
- **Ask at both ends.** Costs paid in the home village, such as sub-agent fees, passport "help", travel to the capital and waiting time in hostels, are often invisible at the destination [5][13]. Survey at origin (before departure and after return) and at destination.
- **Make it safe and understandable.** Interview off-site or through trusted third parties, in the worker's own language, without supervisors present, with informed consent. Collect no names in the analysis dataset.
- **Use the SDG 10.7.1 cost list** (recruiter charges, visa, passport, medical tests, insurance, training, travel, welfare fund fees, and deductions from wages) so that results are comparable across sites and with national statistics [2][3][4].

### 5.2 Payment tracing

- **Match documents:** recruitment agency service agreements, invoices from the employer to the agency, and the agency's receipts to workers. IHRB recommends that suppliers be required to prove they paid recruitment costs, using service agreements, purchase orders, electronic transfers and itemized receipts [31].
- **Follow the money:** where workers consent, compare loan records, bank or mobile-money transfers made before departure, and remittance patterns afterwards.
- **Check wage slips** for unexplained deductions, which can be a way of recovering fees. The ILO definition counts these as fees [8]. Wage protection systems in the Gulf create a digital payroll trail that can be compared with contracts [19][23].
- **Look for warning signs:** receipts for exactly the legal cap (the Nepal pattern [13]), very similar receipts across many workers, and an employer that cannot show it paid the recruiter at all. An employer that paid nothing to a recruiter who delivered workers is strong evidence that the workers paid instead.

### 5.3 Document checks

Check who holds passports and residence permits. Retaining identity documents is prohibited in Qatar and the UAE [18][23] and in the Dhaka Principles [10], and the ILO identifies it as a common form of coercion [5]. Check contracts signed at origin against contracts at destination for substitution. In the Qatar pilot, contract changes fell from 35% to 8% [20].

### 5.4 Grievance channels

Grievance channels are necessary but they are weak at detecting fees, for three reasons:

- **Fear.** Workers without secure status, or without their documents, are "discouraged from filing complaints" [5].
- **Scale.** Complaints about fees often come from "single, courageous workers" and rarely trigger a wider investigation [35].
- **Throughput is not the same as resolution.** Qatar's labour ministry received 34,425 complaints between October 2021 and October 2022, and its online platform received 227 anonymous complaints through a whistleblower channel in the same period. The ministry settled 66.5% and sent 30.7% to dispute committees, which ruled for the worker in 84% of cases [19]. That shows the channel is used and often favours workers. But the main complaints were about wages, not recruitment fees, which are usually paid before arrival.

Good practice is to offer several channels (hotline, app, worker committees, trade unions), allow anonymous reporting, and treat any single report of fees as a reason to survey the whole group [35].

### 5.5 Pseudocode: fee detection and remediation workflow (illustration only, not run)

```text
# ---------- INPUTS ----------
# survey[w]  : anonymous survey record for worker w (stratum, recruiter, origin district,
#              recruitment year, itemized costs paid, loan principal, loan interest rate,
#              loan months, currency, date paid)
# docs[w]    : documents linked to w by a pseudonymous ID (agency receipts, wage slips,
#              employer-to-agency invoices, passport-holding status)
# policy     : remediation parameters set in advance with worker representatives
#              (include_interest, opportunity_rate, inflation_index, fx_table,
#               min_cell_size, deadline_days)

# ---------- STEP 1: estimate fees paid per worker ----------
for each worker w:
    reported   = sum(survey[w].itemized_costs converted with fx_table at date paid)
    documented = sum(docs[w].receipts) + sum(fee-like deductions in docs[w].wage_slips)
    # Worker testimony is the primary evidence; documents can raise an estimate, never lower it.
    fee[w] = max(reported, documented)
    if docs[w].receipt_amount == legal_cap and reported > legal_cap:
        flag(w, "receipt equals legal cap; likely under-receipted")
    if docs[w].passport_held_by_employer:
        flag(w, "document retention")

# ---------- STEP 2: flag recruiters and corridors ----------
for each group g in group_by(recruiter, origin district, recruitment year):
    if size(g) < policy.min_cell_size:
        merge g into its nearest larger group  # avoid identifying individuals
    share_paid[g]  = fraction of workers in g with fee > 0
    median_fee[g]  = median(fee[w] for w in g)
    employer_paid_recruiter[g] = exists(invoice from employer to recruiter for g)
    if share_paid[g] > 0 or not employer_paid_recruiter[g]:
        flag(g, "fees present; review recruiter")

# ---------- STEP 3: calculate refund owed ----------
for each worker w:
    group_avg = mean(fee[v] for v in group(w))
    group_med = median(fee[v] for v in group(w))
    # Use whichever method is most favourable to the worker.
    base = max(fee[w], group_avg, group_med)
    interest = 0
    if policy.include_interest and survey[w].loan_principal > 0:
        interest = actual interest paid or owed on the recruitment loan
    adjust = inflation_and_fx_adjustment(base, date paid, today)
    opportunity = base * policy.opportunity_rate   # e.g. 0.02 in Impactt good practice
    owed[w] = base + interest + adjust + opportunity
    # Eligibility includes former, subcontracted and transfer workers who paid.

# ---------- STEP 4: pay and verify receipt ----------
for each worker w:
    pay owed[w] as one lump sum where possible, to an account the worker controls
    record bank or mobile-money confirmation
verification_sample = random sample stratified as in Step 2
for each w in verification_sample:
    independent interviewer confirms (off-site, own language):
        amount received == owed[w]
        no kickback, deduction or "service charge" taken back
        worker understands the payment is a refund of recruitment fees
        no threats or reprisals since payment
    if any check fails: reopen case for the group, not just the individual

# ---------- STEP 5: follow-up ----------
after 3 to 6 months:
    repeat a short survey of the same groups and of new hires
    alert if new hires report fees > 0, if share_paid has not fallen,
          or if workers who were repaid report new debt
publish aggregate results only (groups at or above min_cell_size)
```

### 5.6 Notes on the method

- **Small samples.** With a few dozen workers per group, averages are noisy. Report medians and ranges, merge small groups before publishing, and never publish a figure for a group so small that individuals could be identified. Where a group is small, interview everyone, as the guidance recommends [35].
- **Retaliation safety.** Keep survey data separate from employer HR systems. Use pseudonymous IDs held by the independent party. Do not reveal which workers reported fees. Watch for warning signs after payment, such as sudden transfers, dismissals, or new "charges". Verification interviews exist partly to check for retaliation [35].
- **Bias in self-reports.** Workers may under-report out of fear or because the recruiter told them to, or over-report if they think it will increase repayment. Matching survey answers with payment records, and looking at group patterns rather than single answers, reduces both risks.
- **Prevention beats repayment.** Every step above is expensive after the fact. The IOM/ILO review and the Qatar pilot both point to direct or closely managed recruitment, with the employer paying the recruiter openly, as the most effective way to stop fees in the first place [20][35].

## 6. Counterarguments

### 6.1 "Costs will just be passed back to workers through lower wages or deductions"

*The argument.* If employers must pay recruitment costs, they will recover them through lower wages, more deductions, or fewer hires.

*Evidence.* This is partly supported. The UAE study found that when workers gained bargaining power, firms hired fewer new migrants and lowered their starting pay [24]. In Indonesia → Singapore recruitment, debts are recovered through salary deductions at the destination [17]. That is why the ILO definition counts deductions to recover fees as fees [8]. But the same UAE study found that firms had been paying incumbent workers only about half of their marginal product, so there was real room to pay more without losing money [24]. In the Qatar pilot, worker-paid costs fell by about 91% (from US$3,408 to US$300), and the ILO reported business benefits rather than losses [20].

*Assessment.* Pass-through is a real risk, which is why wage slips and deductions must be monitored. It is not a reason to keep worker-paid fees. The UAE evidence suggests that, in labour markets this uncompetitive, employers have room to absorb at least part of the cost.

### 6.2 "Informal sub-agents in origin villages are out of reach"

*The argument.* Much of the money is taken by unlicensed brokers in home villages, so destination employers and even origin regulators cannot control it.

*Evidence.* This is supported. In Nepal, indirect costs and intermediaries offset the reduction in direct fees [15]. The ILO notes that passport, language and travel costs are often "uncovered, hidden and not reported" [13]. Fees and related costs are now defined to include payments made "regardless of the manner, timing or location" [8], which puts responsibility on the employer and its recruiters even when the cash went to a sub-agent.

*Assessment.* This is the strongest practical objection. The best response is to cut the chain: direct hiring, licensed recruiters paid openly by the employer, and origin-side surveys that capture village-level payments [20][35].

### 6.3 "Enforcement gaps and corruption make rules meaningless"

*Evidence.* The Nepal evidence of false receipts at the legal cap is clear [13][14]. On the other hand, Qatar revoked 54 agency licences in nine months [19], and Nepal's courts ordered the government to enforce its policy [13].

*Assessment.* Rules without checking are weak. The evidence supports pairing rules with verification and with joint origin–destination tools such as visa centres and bilateral agreements.

### 6.4 "Bans push recruitment underground"

*Evidence.* There is some evidence for this. After Nepal's cap, fees moved off the books, with receipts for NPR 10,000 issued whatever was really paid [13][14]. We found no rigorous study showing that a fee ban *increased* total fees or irregular migration. Corridors with strong rules, such as Philippines → Qatar, show some of the lowest costs [4].

*Assessment.* A cap that is not enforced can hide fees rather than end them. That argues for verification and transparent employer-to-recruiter payment, not for allowing fees.

### 6.5 "Employers bear new costs and lose competitiveness"

*Evidence.* The employer's cost of recruiting is usually far below what workers pay. IHRB notes that workers typically pay more for recruitment than employers would have been charged for the same service [31], and the ILO's study shows the same Saudi job costing from US$309 to US$4,460 depending on the origin country [9]. The Qatar pilot reported business benefits, including better selection of workers and fewer disputes [20]. The IOM/ILO review notes that buyers sometimes share repayment costs with suppliers, and that repayment is harder where national law still allows fees [35].

*Assessment.* There is a real cost, especially for small suppliers. It is smaller than the burden now carried by workers, and it can be shared along the supply chain.

### 6.6 "Workers want to pay; it is their choice"

*Evidence.* Workers do accept fees as the price of a job. The ILO's global study notes that, with many more jobseekers than jobs, recruiters "price their services according to what the market will bear" [9]. But the Bangladesh → Qatar evaluation found that fee-free recruits felt *less* obligation to migrate and reported better mental health [21]. That suggests the "choice" to pay is shaped by the debt, not freely made.

## 7. Recommendations, ranked by strength of evidence

**Strong evidence** (causal studies or consistent official findings across several countries)

1. **Recruit directly, or through a small number of vetted recruiters, and have the employer pay the recruiter openly.** The Bangladesh → Qatar pilot cut worker-paid costs from about 11.7 months of wages to about 1 month, with less debt and fewer contract changes, in a peer-reviewed evaluation [20][21]. The IOM/ILO review names direct hiring as one of the most effective ways to prevent fees [35].
2. **Let workers change employers and leave the country without employer permission.** The UAE's 2011 reform raised incumbent workers' earnings by more than 10% [24]. Qatar's 2018–2020 reforms are now in operation at scale [19]. Pair these reforms with monitoring of starting wages and hiring of new arrivals [24].
3. **Do not rely on caps or receipts alone.** In Nepal, a cap with weak monitoring left many workers paying far above it, with false receipts hiding the difference [13][14][15].

**Moderate evidence** (good descriptive or administrative data; no full causal test)

4. **Measure fees regularly with SDG 10.7.1 surveys at both ends of each major corridor**, as a joint task of origin and destination statistical offices, so progress can be tracked [2][3][5].
5. **Shift the burden of proof to the employer in remediation.** Qatar's reimbursement scheme reached 49,286 workers without requiring receipts [22]. The IOM/ILO review recommends treating worker testimony as primary evidence [35].
6. **Repay in full, quickly, and to everyone who paid**, including former and subcontracted workers. Use the most favourable of individual, average and median amounts, and include loan interest, with independent verification of receipt [35].
7. **Use wage protection systems to detect deductions** that recover fees, and extend them to domestic workers [19][23].

**Emerging or limited evidence** (plausible, but not yet evaluated)

8. **Joint origin–destination infrastructure**, such as visa centres in origin countries, standard contracts signed before departure, and employer-pays clauses in bilateral agreements [9][13][19]. These are promising, but we found no published evaluation of their effect on fees.
9. **Regional dialogues as channels for shared standards.** The Colombo Process working group on ethical recruitment and the Abu Dhabi Dialogue's commitment to joint responsibility [26][27] are natural forums for agreeing common definitions (such as the ILO 2019 definition) and shared fee surveys.
10. **Industry commitments with public, audited repayment data.** RBA and AAFA/FLA commitments exist [28][33]. Public reporting that separates amounts, workers, time periods and verification results would make these totals comparable and credible.
