# How a trafficked worker's testimony is weighed

An ethical case study. Research only: nothing here is operational guidance, legal advice, or a proposal to build a tool.

## 0. Summary

A trafficking case often comes down to whether one frightened person is believed. That is an ethical problem as well as an evidential one. The person's account may be the only evidence the court has [S2]. Trauma, fear, delayed disclosure and the conditions of the interview can make that account incomplete or inconsistent without it being false [S2] [S3] [S5] [S6]. If the case rests on that account alone, a credibility attack, a recantation, a missing interpreter, or a decision not to testify can collapse the prosecution [S2] [S4] [S7] [S10]. The ethical response is not to discount the person's word. It is to interview in a way that does not cause further harm, to interpret accurately and independently, to assess credibility in light of what is known about trauma and memory, and to build corroboration so that justice does not depend on one witness remaining brave, consistent and available [S1] [S2] [S4] [S9].

Four findings structure this study.

1. **Trauma-informed interviewing.** Trauma commonly affects memory, concentration, sense of time and the ability to give a fluent, chronological narrative [S3] [S5] [S12]. Discrepancies between repeated accounts are common, especially on details the speaker rates as peripheral, and they increase with delay among people with high post-traumatic stress [S5]. Treating inconsistency itself as proof of fabrication is not supported by that evidence [S5] [S6]. WHO's ten principles for interviewing trafficked women start with "do no harm" and require that questions not be designed to provoke distress [S1].

2. **Interpreters.** An interpreter can enable a fair account or destroy it. Accuracy means not adding, omitting or reshaping what was said [S11]. Neutrality means no advice, no side conversations and no conflict of interest [S11] [S16]. The gravest risk is an interpreter tied to the traffickers. WHO tells interviewers to screen for any involvement with trafficking agents, not to accept unknown volunteers, and records a case in which authorities unknowingly used the trafficker, who said he was the women's uncle, as the interpreter [S1]. UNODC likewise warns that volunteer interpreters "may be connected to the traffickers" and that traffickers have used interpreters to infiltrate investigations [S11]. Language and culture gaps can also distort meaning, increase shame, or turn a woman's words into "local gossip" [S1] [S3] [S11].

3. **Why a single-witness case collapses.** UNODC, after reviewing 135 cases from 31 jurisdictions, found that victim testimony is often the central or only evidence, and that trafficking cases "often stand or fall on the availability/quality of victim testimony" [S2]. Uncorroborated testimony, even if consistent, may be inadequate to prove the offence beyond reasonable doubt; some legal systems will not convict on the word of one witness [S2]. In United States research, prosecutors repeatedly said they would not proceed without a cooperating victim, and often not without more than one [S7]. In a later national survey, 70 per cent of responding prosecutors said victim testimony was their primary source of evidence; the most common obstacles to that testimony were intimidation, unstable housing, and the person not wanting to endure the trauma of a trial [S10]. Lack of cooperation is widely cited as the biggest barrier, yet in a study of state-level cases, victim cooperation did not independently predict charging. Physical and corroborating evidence strongly did. Almost one-third of those cases had no such evidence [S9].

4. **Corroboration that can take the weight off that witness.** Strategic investigation can support, and sometimes partly substitute for, the person's testimony: financial and business records, contracts, messages, medical records, other witnesses, physical evidence and digital records [S2] [S4] [S10] [S15]. This includes documents the worker kept. In one case digested by UNODC, notebooks in which victims recorded dates they were taken out, cash advances and other personal matters were admitted even though the victims themselves did not testify [S2]. Employer ledgers have been used to show a disparity in pay [S2]. A worker-held wage and hours log can play a similar corroborative role. It is not self-proving. It can show that entries were made contemporaneously and have not been altered; it cannot, by itself, prove that the entries are true. If an exploiter finds it, it can put the worker at risk. Financial investigation should start at the beginning of the case, so that a prosecution which will rely on the worker as a witness is not left with that witness alone [S15].

This study does not claim that documentary records make testimony unnecessary, or that a self-kept log should be treated as conclusive. It claims that a system which weighs one traumatised account in isolation, through an unvetted interpreter, and then treats inconsistency as deceit, is neither safe nor fair.

## 1. Why so much weight falls on one person's word

The international definition of trafficking in persons, in the UN Trafficking in Persons Protocol, has three elements: an act (recruitment, transportation, transfer, harbouring or receipt of persons); a means (threat or use of force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power or of a position of vulnerability, or giving or receiving payments or benefits to achieve the consent of a person having control over another person); and a purpose of exploitation [S2]. Where the person is a child, the means need not be proved [S2]. Those elements often leave little physical trace. The crime is covert. People may be moved, isolated, or still under threat. They may not see themselves as victims, may fear the police, or may have been told that they themselves have committed an offence [S7].

In that setting, the person's own account becomes the main way to explain what happened, how control was exercised, and what the exploitation was. UNODC's 2017 case digest, based on 135 cases from 31 jurisdictions, states that testimonial evidence was critical in most of the cases reviewed, that victim testimony "seems to be the most relied upon source of evidence", and that "often this may be the only, or the main, piece of evidence available". Even when other evidence exists, the person's testimony is often needed to explain it: a medical report may show bruises, but often only the person can say how they were inflicted [S2]. A Dutch analysis cited in the same digest, of 77 investigations in 2012, likewise found that criminal investigations rely to a large extent on the statements of victims [S2].

A later US survey of local prosecutors found the same pattern: 70 per cent of respondents said victim testimony was their primary source of evidence used to prosecute trafficking cases [S10]. Law enforcement officers in an earlier multi-site US study reported that victim statements were the most important element of a trafficking investigation, even when other corroborating evidence was available [S7].

This is a fragile place to put a case. The person may be frightened, indebted, ashamed, still in contact with the exploiter, or facing removal from the country [S4] [S7]. The 2024 US *Trafficking in Persons Report* introduction, and a factsheet drawn from it, set out a framework for balancing prosecution and protection. That framework describes the bind clearly [S4] [S8]: cases often move slowly; people are anxious about the outcome, fearful of retaliation, re-traumatised by having to recount events, and subject to "the distrust of authorities that traffickers often instill and manipulate to compel victims' silence" [S4]. They face "conflicting pressures from authorities encouraging them to cooperate, traffickers seeking to silence them, and their own efforts to put traumatic events behind them" [S4]. For some, speaking and being believed can be vindicating. For many, the process is harrowing if support is weak [S4].

The ethical problem is not that courts listen to the person. They should. The problem is that the whole case may be made to rest on whether that one account survives cross-examination, delay, translation and fear.

## 2. Trauma-informed interviewing

### 2.1 How trauma affects memory and the ability to give an account

People who have been trafficked commonly live with serious psychological after-effects. A 2012 systematic review of 19 studies, all of which concerned women and girls, mostly trafficked for sexual exploitation, found a high prevalence of violence and of mental distress. Across studies that screened for these symptoms, reported rates of anxiety ranged from 48.0 per cent to 97.7 per cent, depression from 54.9 per cent to 100 per cent, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) from 19.5 per cent to 77.0 per cent [S12]. Memory problems were among the physical health symptoms commonly reported [S12]. The evidence on men, and on people trafficked for labour, was thin in that review, and the authors were careful about generalisation [S12]. The direction of the finding is still relevant to labour cases: a later OSCE/ODIHR guidance document, citing subsequent health studies that did include men, women and children in post-trafficking services, lists poor memory and concentration, dissociative symptoms, shame, self-blame, panic, and emotional withdrawal among common consequences of trafficking-related trauma [S3].

OSCE/ODIHR describes trauma-informed practice as recognising trauma indicators, understanding their impact, allowing rest breaks, protecting confidentiality and consent, taking a culturally congruent approach, and supporting people to give a full account of what happened to them [S3]. The point is not only kindness. Trauma can block or distort recollection and the sense of time, and can change how a person trusts an interviewer and what they are able to disclose [S3].

The scientific literature on autobiographical memory after trauma, developed largely in the asylum context but directly applicable to credibility assessment, reaches the same conclusion. Memory of a past event is a reconstruction, not a replay [S6]. Emotionally charged central features of an event tend to be retained better than peripheral details, but even central details decline over time [S6]. PTSD, described in that literature as a "disorder of memory", is associated with less specific, less detailed recollection and with inconsistencies [S6]. Avoidance, a core symptom cluster of PTSD, can mean that an account given later contains material that was not produced at the first interview [S6]. It is unreasonable to expect precise recall of some details that a lay assessor might assume would be unforgettable, such as the exact appearance of a person who inflicted serious harm [S6].

### 2.2 Why accounts can be inconsistent without being false

A repeated-interview study of 39 refugees (27 Kosovan and 12 Bosnian), already recognised as a group and with no incentive to fabricate, found discrepancies between the two accounts for every participant [S5]. That study is of refugees, not of trafficked workers. It is cited here because it is a controlled test of how trauma and delay affect consistency in repeated interviews, which is the mechanism also invoked in trafficking cases, not because its sample was trafficked. The mean discrepancy rate was 0.32. For participants with high levels of post-traumatic stress, the number of discrepancies increased with the time between interviews (correlation 0.70, P<0.01). That association was not found in the group with low post-traumatic stress. Discrepancies were significantly more common in details the interviewee rated as peripheral than in details rated as central [S5]. The authors' conclusion was that "the assumption that inconsistency of recall means that accounts have poor credibility is questionable", and that such inconsistencies "should not be relied on as indicating a lack of credibility" [S5].

UNODC's case digest records the same pattern in criminal trafficking trials. "In cases of trafficking in persons, statements by victims are often inconsistent or even include outright falsehoods." Consistency matters in ordinary credibility assessment, but in trafficking cases "inconsistencies may arise from a range of reasons other than lack of credibility", including "lapses in memory, confusion about the chain of events or traumatic reactions", as well as fear that traffickers will retaliate [S2]. The digest quotes courts that accepted this. In one Canadian case, the court found "consistency on the substantive aspects of the testimony" despite omissions, memory lapses, exaggerations, contradictions and hesitation, and said that, given the effects of traumatisation, "it would have been worrisome had the victim been able to testify in detail and in chronological order" [S2]. The digest advises judges to ask, among other things, whether there is an external explanation for contradictions (trauma, family relations, fear, threats, lapse of time, indoctrination) and whether an understanding of the traumatic process yields a more nuanced reading of the evidence [S2].

The peer-reviewed experimental evidence on this point is still narrow. The discrepancy rates come from that one 39-person refugee study [S5]. A related review in the asylum and testimony literature describes memory as a reconstruction, and says emotionally charged central features tend to be retained better than peripheral details [S6]. A broader review of memory disturbance in post-traumatic stress disorder finds that avoidance or suppression of memories, overgeneral memory, and impaired retrieval of voluntary trauma memories can all occur in PTSD, which is one reason an account can be incomplete or change between interviews [S17]. The full text of that review was not re-opened for this revision; those points are taken from its published abstract and are otherwise **UNVERIFIED**. No source opened here is a study of trafficked workers' own memory consistency.

The main United Nations standard on how to read inconsistencies in a traumatised person's account is the *Istanbul Protocol* (2022 revision), the OHCHR manual on the effective investigation and documentation of torture and other cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment [S18]. It is a manual for clinical and legal documentation of torture, not a trafficking-specific instrument, but it is the principal UN text on this evidential problem. Paragraph 347 states: "Inconsistencies between a person's allegations of abuse and the findings of the evaluation may arise from any or all of the aforementioned factors and should not be assumed to indicate untruthfulness." It also notes that a person may be unable to give dates, times, places, frequencies or the exact identities of perpetrators, and that "a broad outline of the alleged traumatic events will emerge and stand up over time" [S18].

None of this means that every inconsistent account is true, or that courts should abandon scrutiny. It means that inconsistency, especially on peripheral dates, numbers, sequences or descriptions, is a poor standalone test of whether a trafficked person is lying.

### 2.3 How credibility is assessed, and how it should be

In practice, credibility is often assessed in ways that cut against a traumatised witness. The 2024 US trafficking framework states that everyone who gives a statement or testimony in a trafficking case "is inevitably subjected to credibility challenges", whether by judges in inquisitorial systems or by the defence in adversarial ones. Those challenges commonly rest on "delays in reporting their victimization, trauma-related inconsistencies in recalling and recounting certain details, or involvement in unlawful acts related to their victimization" [S4]. US prosecutors interviewed in 2012 said that a victim's background and personal circumstances influenced whether a case was taken, and whether it was reduced to a lesser charge [S7]. A later survey found that "believability in front of a jury was still important, even with what we know today about the impact of trauma on victims and victims' statements" [S10]. Some cases were declined for reasons that included "the victim's trauma, background, or case" [S10].

Trauma-informed interviewing is the ethical and practical counterweight. WHO's 2003 recommendations, written by Cathy Zimmerman and Charlotte Watts, set ten principles for interviewing trafficked women. They were written mainly for researchers, journalists and service providers, but UNODC has presented them as applicable also to other trafficked people [S1] [S14]. The principles include: do no harm, and do not undertake an interview that will make the person's situation worse; know the risks of the case before starting; prepare referral information and do not make promises that cannot be kept; select and prepare interpreters carefully; protect anonymity and confidentiality; obtain informed consent, including the right not to answer and the right to stop; respect the person's own assessment of risk; do not re-traumatise; be prepared for emergency intervention; and put the information to good use [S1]. On re-traumatisation, WHO is specific: questions should never be designed to provoke a strong emotional reaction; questions that force unnecessary traumatic detail should be excluded; judgmental questions should not be posed; the person should direct the pace; and a distressed person should never be pushed to continue [S1]. Anticipation of an interview can itself be severely stressful [S1].

OSCE minimum standards for a first interview, as reproduced in UNODC's toolkit, add that the person should be told, clearly and in their own language, about the procedure and its consequences; that experienced interpreters should be present; that questions about intimate life should be avoided; that conclusive identification may take time because of the complexity of the crime and the effects of post-traumatic stress; and that a reflection period, during which the person receives support without having to make an immediate statement, is a particularly effective way to allow self-identification [S13]. Other evidence, besides the person's statement, should be collected [S13].

The US framework draws the evidential implication: trauma-informed, culturally appropriate interviewing, often with professional interpreters, can "elicit more accurate statements, minimizing discrepancies that could later be used to attack the victim's credibility, and enabling the victim to provide more detailed information that could lead to other sources of evidence" [S4]. Expert testimony may be used, where the law allows, to explain the effects of trauma on memory and recall [S4]. Stabilisation and support should come before any expectation of "meaningful participation" [S4]. Protection and services should not be conditioned on a charge or a conviction [S4].

## 3. Interpreters

### 3.1 Accuracy

If the interview is not in a language the person and the interviewer both speak well, the record of the testimony is only as good as the interpretation. UNODC's module for criminal justice practitioners on interpreters is unequivocal. The interpreter's role in a law enforcement interview is "to accurately interpret questions" and answers [S11]. "Accurate interpretation of the language used" is essential. Interpreters must not change the meaning of words they find difficult or offensive, must not add anything to what was said, must not recast questions into the third person, and must not turn an open question into a closed one [S11]. They should not hold a conversation in a language that one of the people present does not understand; everything said should be interpreted [S11]. They should flag cultural differences that affect communication, for example that nodding means "yes" in some cultures and "no" in others, and should tell the interviewer why they are intervening [S11].

A distorted interpretation does not only produce a poor transcript. It can create apparent inconsistencies between interviews, or between what the person said and what a later official record claims they said. Those discrepancies are then used, as section 2 describes, to attack credibility [S4] [S5].

### 3.2 Neutrality

Neutrality is a separate requirement from linguistic skill. UNODC requires a conflict-of-interest check before an interpreter is used, and again if a conflict becomes apparent during the interview [S11]. Common conflicts include knowing the person or members of their family [S11]. In some communities the pool of interpreters is small, so the chance that the interpreter knows the person, or knows of their family in the country of origin, is high [S11]. The interpreter should not be personally or emotionally involved in the case [S11]. If anyone outside the interview approaches them, they should report it [S11].

Practice guidance used by investigators makes the same points in plainer terms: the interpreter is not a social worker, friend, ally, investigator or prosecutor; should not offer personal opinions or advice; and should remain "neutral and without judgment" [S16]. Certified or court-accredited interpreters are preferred because they are more likely to know the legal setting and the ethical standards expected of them [S16]. WHO similarly warns against interpreters who may be judgmental, shocked or offended by what a woman reveals, and says they should be briefed on trafficking and on the range of abuse they may hear [S1].

### 3.3 The risk that an interpreter is tied to the traffickers

This is not a theoretical risk. WHO's first instruction when selecting interpreters is to make certain they have "no involvement with any trafficking agents" [S1]. Its recommendations are blunt: "Do not accept unknown volunteer interpreters, as they may be involved in the trafficking situation, or may have something to gain by participating in the interview." If the only available interpreter cannot be trusted, "it may be better not to conduct an interview" [S1]. In a boxed example in the same document, authorities in the United States "unknowingly used the trafficker as the interpreter to interview several trafficked women about an alleged suicide of a young girl. The man told the authorities that he was their uncle" [S1].

UNODC gives the same warning. Volunteer interpreters "should be avoided in trafficking cases. They may be connected to the traffickers, or at least know the victim and his or her family." If their use is unavoidable, investigators should assess possible links (using people from the same vehicle is singled out as particularly risky), watch the interviewee's reactions, keep the use of that interpreter to a minimum, and check the interpretation later with an official interpreter [S11]. "Traffickers have frequently shown they will attempt to infiltrate investigations and using the interpreter is a good way of doing that. Be very careful not to use anyone with links to the traffickers or their associates" [S11]. Interpreters who start out independent may later be intimidated, threatened or corrupted [S11]. Ideally, separate interpreters should be used for each victim and for the suspect, both to avoid contamination of accounts and to reduce attempts by suspects to use an interpreter to intimidate [S11]. At a minimum, the same interpreter should not be used for victims and for suspects [S11].

Independent practice guidance likewise tells investigators to be "particularly wary of any non-accredited interpreters who come forward to volunteer their services on the case as they may have ties to the defendant(s) and be actively seeking information about the case" [S16].

An interpreter who is tied to the traffickers can do more than mistranslate. They can report back what was asked and answered, intimidate the speaker into silence or a false account, and taint every later use of that testimony.

### 3.4 Language and cultural gaps

Even an honest, skilled interpreter does not automatically close the gap between the interviewer and the speaker. WHO notes that a shared language can build trust, but can also have the opposite effect. A woman may not trust, or may feel ashamed speaking in front of, someone from her own community. She may be less willing to talk about stigmatised subjects, such as sexual abuse or sex work, in front of a person "instilled with the same moral codes". Less discreet interpreters "may treat a woman's responses as good local gossip", and "local gossip can travel fast and furiously". Unless the interpreter's background and circle of contacts can be assessed, "there is no assurance that information will not be leaked to the wrong places" [S1]. WHO therefore recommends working with someone familiar with violence against women and, unless the respondent indicates otherwise, someone who is not from her home community; asking her privately whether she has a trusted person she prefers; and considering cultural mediators who can bridge the gap between interviewer and respondent [S1]. Interviews in translation also take longer, which prolongs both risk and the time spent re-experiencing distressing events [S1]. A male interpreter or interviewer can raise further difficulties in cases involving sexual violence [S1].

OSCE/ODIHR likewise treats professional interpreters and cultural mediators as part of a trauma-informed system, and stresses culturally congruent communication [S3]. UNODC's interview standards say that cultural and language barriers should be thought about in advance, and that cultural mediators and language interpreters should be involved where possible [S13]. UNODC also notes practical language problems: street terms or trade terms may be unfamiliar even to a fluent interpreter, who needs enough notice to prepare [S11].

The ethical implication is that "we had an interpreter" is not enough. The interpreter must be screened, briefed, accurate, neutral, and acceptable to the person speaking. If those conditions cannot be met, going ahead can be more dangerous than waiting [S1].

## 4. Why prosecutions collapse when the only evidence is one frightened witness

UNODC puts the structural problem in one sentence: "Trafficking cases often stand or fall on the availability/quality of victim testimony" [S2]. The digest then explains why that is so often fatal.

First, the testimony itself is frequently imperfect in the ways already described: inconsistent, partial, delayed, or, in some cases, containing falsehoods told out of fear, loyalty, shame or instruction by the trafficker [S2]. If there is "no other credible evidence to corroborate testimony", those weaknesses have nowhere to be absorbed [S2]. People may not come forward at all, or may give only limited testimony [S2]. They may be too fearful to testify in court; some civil-law systems have developed ways to use written statements or to have a judge question a fearful witness, but those tools are not always available and do not replace investigation [S2].

Second, even a consistent and credible account may not be enough. In some cases "the only evidence available to the court is the victim's testimony and the defendant's denial". Depending on the circumstances, "even when consistent and credible victim testimony is presented to the court, such victim testimony, in itself, may be inadequate to support a conviction for trafficking in persons if there are no other pieces of evidence, as the prosecution may not be deemed to have shouldered its burden of proof 'beyond reasonable doubt'" [S2]. This is a particular risk in common-law systems, where the court is not an active evidence-gatherer and a failure to adduce further evidence that exists may be treated as a weakness in the case [S2]. In addition, "in certain legal systems, general evidential rules may prohibit conviction on the basis of the testimony of one witness" [S2]. The digest gives examples of appeals and acquittals that turned on the dearth of other evidence: a Kenyan conviction reversed for failure to call a potential witness and to produce medical and age evidence; a Belgian labour case acquitted on appeal where surrounding circumstances were not proved [S2].

Third, prosecutors may never bring the case. In interviews across 12 US counties, "prosecutors in all jurisdictions overwhelmingly described victim reluctance to testify or lack of cooperation as the biggest challenge they faced prosecuting human trafficking cases. Often prosecutors reported that without a cooperating victim or, ideally, multiple cooperating victims, they would not proceed with a prosecution" [S7]. Officers were often unprepared for the degree of trauma, and sometimes resorted to tactics normally used on suspects, including arrest, to secure cooperation [S7]. The later Anti-Trafficking Review analysis of state-level cases confirmed the culture of that barrier: in nearly every study site, prosecutors and police cited lack of victim cooperation as the biggest obstacle [S9]. But the same study found that victim cooperation did not independently predict the decision to charge. "It is possible that victim cooperation alone was insufficient to bringing human trafficking cases in the absence of" other evidence [S9]. What strongly predicted prosecution was "the existence of physical and corroborating evidence". Almost one-third of the cases had no such evidence. Without it, prosecutors "may be forced to abandon prosecution or agree to a plea to a lower-level offence" [S9]. Only 22 per cent of the state-level cases with identified perpetrators were charged as human trafficking; prosecutors often used lesser, more familiar offences [S9]. In the earlier 12-county study, state prosecutors overwhelmingly felt that labour trafficking cases should be handled at the federal level; suspected labour trafficking, especially where immigration status was in question, was more often than not sent straight to federal immigration authorities and never reached a state or local prosecutor [S7].

A 2021 study of local US prosecutors found some shift toward corroboration, but not an escape from dependence on the witness. Seventy per cent still said victim testimony was their primary evidence. The most commonly reported obstacles to that testimony were witness intimidation and unstable housing, "followed closely by the victim not wanting to endure the trauma of a trial" [S10]. The most common reasons for declining a case included the person's trauma or background [S10]. "Believability in front of a jury was still important" even as more offices tried to rely less on testimony by gathering digital and other corroboration [S10].

Fourth, the person may be gone, recant, or be unsafe. The US framework notes that foreign victims may be repatriated, and that prosecutions should still proceed where the law allows, including by remote testimony or funded return [S4]. Intimidation and contact by the trafficker can explain reluctance or recantation and should themselves be investigated and, where appropriate, charged [S4]. But if the case was built only on that one person's continued willingness to speak, any of those events ends it.

The ethical cost of this model is borne by the person who was exploited. They are asked to carry the case. If they cannot, or if their trauma-affected account is disbelieved, the exploiter is not held to account and the person may be left less safe than before. WHO's "do no harm" principle is engaged at that point as much as at the interview itself [S1].

## 5. What corroboration can make the case not rest on that witness alone

### 5.1 A mosaic, not a single pillar

UNODC's digest is organised around a "mosaic of evidence": testimonial evidence from the person, from other witnesses, from experts and from defendants; out-of-court statements; documentary evidence; and "real" evidence such as photographs, equipment, biological traces and demeanour [S2]. Documentary evidence "can include financial and business records, contracts, e-mails, text messages or invoices" [S2]. The point of the mosaic is that each piece can support another, so that weaknesses in any one piece, including the person's testimony, do not decide the case.

The 2024 US framework calls this "Developing Evidence to Decrease Reliance on Victim Testimony". "In human trafficking prosecutions, every piece of evidence counts because each piece of corroborating evidence is important to reduce reliance on victim testimony, preventing undue credibility attacks, and to increase the likelihood of conviction" [S4]. Investigators and prosecutors should look early and continuously for "electronic records, physical evidence, and other potential witnesses" [S4]. Even "limited circumstantial corroboration of one small aspect of a trafficker's conduct can, when combined with other evidence, provide significant substantiation of a survivor's account" [S4]. In some cases, other admissible evidence "may minimize the need for victim testimony and can become essential to enabling a prosecution to proceed even if the victim is not able to participate in the trial" [S4]. The person should be told clearly that they are not "responsible" for a successful prosecution, and that services do not depend on the outcome of the case [S4].

Where the trafficking charge itself cannot be proved without the person's testimony, prosecutors can still, where the facts and the law allow, focus on more readily provable conduct such as assault, threats, financial crimes or witness tampering, or resolve a case by a plea that does not require the person to testify at trial [S4]. Those are second-best routes to accountability. They are better than a collapse that leaves the exploiter untouched. They are not a reason to skip investigation of the trafficking itself.

### 5.2 Financial, digital, medical and other witness evidence

A 2017 ASEAN–ILO workshop on investigating and prosecuting trafficking for labour exploitation recommended that investigators "instigate parallel financial investigations in relation to each case and initiate the financial investigation techniques from the commencement of the criminal investigation itself" [S15]. Investigators and prosecutors should work together "to maximise the potential of securing the essential corroborative evidence that is crucial in prosecutions for offences of trafficking for the purposes of labour exploitation, most especially in respect of those prosecutions that will rely on the testimony of the victim as a witness" [S15]. The same recommendations treat withholding or refusing to pay wages as a core labour-trafficking issue, and treat restitution of unpaid wages as part of the remedy [S15].

UNODC's digest shows how financial and other records have worked in real cases. In the Australian slavery case *Wei Tang*, business records showed a disparity between payments to the exploited women and payments to other women in the same establishment; the records appeared in a different part of the ledger and used a different percentage of the fees, which supported the prosecution case [S2]. Western Union transcripts in German cases showed where money was channelled and helped to prove exploitation [S2]. Other documentary examples in the digest include text messages that supported an otherwise uncorroborated account; recorded telephone conversations; unofficial marriage contracts and receipts; hotel, bank and ticket records of an organised recruitment network; and immigration-related letters [S2]. The digest is also honest that documents can cut the other way: in *R. v. Ladha*, text messages, a recorded conversation and a return air ticket were used by the defence to undermine the complainant's account, and the defendant was acquitted [S2]. Corroboration is not a one-way tool. That is a reason to gather it carefully, not a reason to leave the case on uncorroborated speech.

Digital evidence is now a routine form of this mosaic in some systems. In the 2021 US prosecutor survey, the corroboration reported most commonly was jail mail and calls, cell-phone and other digital evidence, and evidence of physical harm [S10]. Of those who answered the relevant question, 96 per cent reported using cell-phone evidence to support or corroborate victim testimony, and 91 per cent reported collecting and presenting other digital evidence for the same purpose [S10]. The US framework lists, as admissible corroboration in appropriate cases, medical records, testimony from first responders or other witnesses, certain statements by the accused, electronic messages, physical evidence from relevant locations, and video recordings [S4]. UNODC similarly treats other witnesses (neighbours, customers, family, law enforcement, NGO workers) and expert evidence as part of the mosaic [S2].

### 5.3 Documentary records the worker holds, including wage and hours logs

The mosaic should include records that belong to the worker, not only records that belong to the employer or the state. Employer files can be false, incomplete, or designed to show a lawful payment that was later taken back. A record the worker made at the time, and kept, can be independent of that file.

UNODC already documents this kind of evidence. In *Martin* (Philippines), "notebooks or journals written by the 34 victims were submitted. These recorded the dates they were taken out of the brothel for prostitution purposes, the nationality of the customers, the places to which the victims went, cash advances and other personal matters." The victims themselves did not testify. The court admitted the notebooks as "entries in the course of business", a recognised common-law exception to the rule against hearsay, "which may be submitted even if the persons who made the entries do not testify." The defendant was convicted [S2]. Those notebooks are not wage logs in a factory or on a boat, but they are the same class of document: a contemporaneous, worker-held record of time, movement and money.

A worker-held wage and hours log is a labour-trafficking counterpart of that record. It can corroborate, among other things, days and hours worked, amounts actually received, deductions, debts and "fees", and the gap between what a contract or an employer ledger shows and what the worker was paid. Used with bank records, contracts, inspectorate files, messages and other testimony, it can support the means and purpose elements of trafficking, especially withholding of wages, debt bondage and deception about pay [S2] [S15]. It can also support a claim for unpaid wages even if a trafficking charge is not proved [S15].

It has strict limits, which should be stated plainly.

- **It is not self-proving.** A log can show that an entry was made, roughly when, and whether it was later altered. It cannot, by itself, show that the entry is true. Credibility still depends on contemporaneous making, the integrity of the record, how the maker is identified, and corroboration by other facts [S2]. UNODC notes that common-law systems usually require a witness to attest to the circumstances under which a document was drafted, and limit admissibility in various ways [S2]. The *Martin* notebooks were admitted under a specific hearsay exception; that exception will not exist in every jurisdiction, and where the maker can testify, the log is more likely to be used to support that testimony than to replace it [S2].

- **It can be used against the worker.** As *Ladha* shows, documents and messages are available to the defence as well as to the prosecution [S2]. Gaps, later additions, or entries that do not match other evidence can be turned into a credibility attack, which is the very harm corroboration is supposed to reduce.

- **It can be unsafe to carry.** WHO warns that referral information found in a woman's possession can put her in danger from pimps, agents or employers [S1]. The same is true of a wage log that records underpayment or abuse. A design or a practice that ignores that risk fails the "do no harm" test [S1].

- **I found no multi-jurisdiction case-law review devoted specifically to worker-held electronic or paper wage logs in trafficking trials** (an **UNVERIFIED** gap in the literature I was able to open this round). The argument above is by analogy from the documentary and financial evidence that UNODC and the ILO workshop do record, not from a dedicated body of wage-log judgments.

These limits are reasons to treat a worker-held log as one tile in the mosaic, collected and weighed with care, not as a substitute for investigation or as a device that makes the worker responsible for proving their own exploitation.

## 6. Ethical implications

Several implications follow, for research, for criminal justice practice, and for any later design of tools that help a worker keep records.

- **The person's testimony has value and must be heard.** Trauma-informed interviewing exists so that the account is fuller, more accurate and less harmful to obtain, not so that the person can be set aside [S1] [S3] [S4].

- **Credibility assessment must be informed by the evidence on trauma and memory.** Inconsistency, delay, flat or emotional demeanour, and involvement in unlawful acts connected to the exploitation are weak reasons, on their own, to disbelieve a trafficked witness [S2] [S4] [S5] [S6]. Central, stable features of the account matter more than volatile peripheral details [S5] [S6].

- **Interpreters are a safety and integrity issue, not a logistical afterthought.** An unvetted, volunteer, or community interpreter can leak information, intimidate, or be the trafficker [S1] [S11]. Accuracy and neutrality have to be designed into the interview [S11].

- **A case that can be destroyed by one person's fear is not a fair case.** Corroboration is an ethical duty to the witness as well as a prosecutorial tactic. It reduces credibility attacks, reduces the need to testify, and can allow a case to continue if the person is repatriated, intimidated, or unable to speak [S2] [S4] [S9] [S15].

- **Worker-held wage and hours records belong in that corroborative set**, with the limits in section 5.3. They should never be the only plan, and they should never be kept in a way that increases the risk to the worker [S1] [S2].

- **Services must not be a reward for being a good witness.** WHO, OSCE and the US framework all separate assistance from the fate of the criminal case [S1] [S3] [S4] [S13]. Using arrest or the withholding of support to compel testimony repeats the harm [S7] [S9].

## 7. What this study does not claim

- It does not claim that every inconsistent account is true, or that courts should convict on an uncorroborated statement where the law requires more.
- It does not offer a script for interviewing, interpreting or prosecuting a live case.
- It does not assess any particular country's current conviction rate, and it does not use figures from the 2025 US *Trafficking in Persons Report*, which I could not open this round (**UNVERIFIED** as to 2025 wording).
- It does not claim that a worker-held wage log has been widely tested in trafficking trials, or that it is self-authenticating.
- It is research only. No tool, device, protocol or training programme is proposed for deployment here.
