# Global Tax Cooperation and Illicit Financial Flows: A Research Case

*A research note on how much tax revenue is lost to profit shifting and offshore evasion, what the 15% global minimum tax and the United Nations tax convention process are doing about it, how those losses compare with aid and food-security needs, and how the proceeds of human trafficking are laundered and traced. It is research and writing only. Figures and findings come from the sources listed at the end. Each DOI was checked against the Crossref registry and each link was opened on 18 November 2026. Where a figure is an estimate, the source and method are named. Advocacy estimates are labelled as such.*

---

## 1. Profit shifting: how big is it?

"Profit shifting" means multinational enterprises (MNEs) booking profits in low-tax jurisdictions rather than where the economic activity takes place. Much of it is legal tax avoidance rather than crime. That matters for the rest of this note: a lot of the money described as "illicit financial flows" in political debate is not illicit in the legal sense.

**Academic estimates.**

- **Tørsløv, Wier and Zucman (2023)** [1] use the national accounts of tax havens. They estimate that 36% of multinational profits are shifted to tax havens. US multinationals shift about twice as much as other multinationals, relative to their foreign earnings. If the shifted profits were reallocated, domestic corporate profits would rise by about 20% in high-tax EU countries, 10% in the United States and 5% in developing countries, and would fall by 55% in the havens [1].
- **Crivelli, de Mooij and Keen (2016)** [2] (first an IMF working paper in 2015) use panel data for 173 countries over 33 years. They find that cross-border tax "spillovers" are "if anything a greater concern for developing countries than for advanced – and a significant one" [2]. The EU Tax Observatory summarises their revenue-loss estimates as about 1% of GDP for OECD countries and 1.3% of GDP for non-OECD countries, and notes that the authors stress how tentative the estimates are [2].
- **Cobham and Janský (2018)** [3] re-estimate the IMF model with better revenue data from the ICTD–WIDER Government Revenue Database. They arrive at "a somewhat lower estimate of global revenue losses of around US$500 billion annually". They find the heaviest losses, relative to the economy, in low-income and lower-middle-income countries and across sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, and South Asia [3].

**Offshore wealth (individuals, not companies).** Alstadsæter, Johannesen and Zucman (2019) [4] match leaked offshore customer lists to Scandinavian wealth records. They find that the richest 0.01% of households evade about 25% of their taxes. Random tax audits detect evasion of less than 5% across the whole distribution [4]. The EU Tax Observatory's *Global Tax Evasion Report 2024* reports that offshore evasion has fallen by a factor of about three in ten years. It also finds that billionaires' effective personal tax rates equal 0% to 0.5% of their wealth [9].

**Official estimates.** The OECD's 2015 BEPS Action 11 report estimated global corporate income tax losses from base erosion and profit shifting at USD 100–240 billion a year at 2014 levels [5]. This is the range behind the BEPS project.

**Advocacy estimate.** The Tax Justice Network's *State of Tax Justice 2025* [6] is advocacy research, not peer-reviewed. It reports US$1.7 trillion lost to global corporate tax abuse over the six years 2016–2021. US multinationals account for US$495 billion (29%) of that, and the United States itself lost US$271 billion to its own multinationals. The report says public country-by-country reporting could have prevented US$475 billion of the losses [6]. Its methods build on country-by-country data and are contested. It is included here because it is widely cited, not because it is the most reliable figure.

**What the range means.** Credible estimates of the annual corporate revenue loss run from about USD 100–240 billion (OECD, 2014 levels) to about US$500 billion (Cobham and Janský). The figures differ in base year, data and method, and in whether they count only profit shifting or also tax competition and rate cuts. They should not be added together or averaged as if they measured the same thing.

## 2. Pillar Two: the 15% global minimum tax

**What it is.** In October 2021 more than 135 members of the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework agreed a two-pillar solution. Pillar Two, the Global Anti-Base Erosion (GloBE) rules, aims to ensure that MNE groups with revenue above EUR 750 million pay an effective tax rate of at least 15% in every jurisdiction where they operate [8][10]. Collection mainly works through an income inclusion rule (IIR), an undertaxed profits rule (UTPR) and, increasingly, qualified domestic minimum top-up taxes (QDMTTs), which let the country where the profit is booked collect the top-up first. The rules began to take effect in 2024. By January 2024 about 55 jurisdictions were taking steps to implement them [8].

**Revenue estimates.**

- **OECD (January 2024)** [8]: global gains of USD 155–192 billion a year, or 6.5–8.1% of global corporate income tax revenue. About a third comes from less profit shifting rather than from top-up tax. The share of low-taxed profit would fall from 36% to about 7% in the long run, once the substance carve-out has phased down (about 11% in year one). Shifted profit would roughly halve, and investment hubs would lose about 30% of their tax base. The analysis uses 2017–2020 data, before most countries had legislated [8].
- **EU Tax Observatory (October 2023)** [9]: the minimum tax was "initially expected to increase global corporate tax revenues by close to 10%", but loopholes cut the expected revenue by a factor of two. Relative to a 20% minimum rate with no carve-outs, the gap is a factor of three. The Observatory estimates that a loophole-free minimum tax would raise about $250 billion more [9].

**Theory.** Johannesen (2022) [7] models the minimum tax in a world of competing countries. Its welfare effect for non-havens is "generally ambiguous". It raises their revenue by curbing profit shifting, but it also pushes havens to raise their own rates, which transfers resources from non-haven firms to haven governments. The effect is "unambiguously positive when the global minimum rate is so high that profit shifting ends" [7]. In other words, a low rate with carve-outs may help non-havens less than its headline suggests.

**Status in 2026: the "side-by-side" package.** On 5 January 2026 the 147-member Inclusive Framework agreed a "side-by-side" package [10] with five parts:

1. A permanent Simplified Effective Tax Rate safe harbour, available from 2027 (2026 in some cases), plus a one-year extension of the transitional country-by-country reporting safe harbour.
2. A substance-based tax incentive safe harbour. Qualified incentives are capped at the greater of 5.5% of payroll costs or of the depreciation of tangible assets in the jurisdiction.
3. A "Side-by-Side" safe harbour and an "Ultimate Parent Entity" (UPE) safe harbour for groups headquartered in jurisdictions with eligible domestic and worldwide minimum-tax regimes. Groups that elect the SbS safe harbour are exempt from the IIR and UTPR.
4. An evidence-based stocktake, to be concluded by 2029, covering competitive imbalances and profit shifting.
5. A reaffirmation that every group stays subject to QDMTTs wherever they apply [10].

The US Treasury described the same agreement as one under which "U.S.-headquartered companies remain subject to only U.S. global minimum taxes while exempting them from Pillar Two" [11]. The OECD text does not name the United States. It sets the eligibility test and an assessment of existing regimes by the end of the first half of 2026 [10][11].

**Critiques.**

- **Carve-outs.** The substance-based income exclusion lets profits tied to payroll and tangible assets fall below 15%. The EU Tax Observatory attributes the halving of expected revenue to loopholes of this kind [9]. The 2026 incentive safe harbour widens the room for low effective rates where real substance exists [10].
- **Small gains for low-income countries.** The OECD's own figures are global. Most of the revenue goes to the jurisdictions where large MNEs are headquartered and to investment hubs that adopt QDMTTs, not to low-income countries where the profits may originate [8]. Tørsløv, Wier and Zucman's figure of a 5% rise in developing countries' domestic profits, if shifting were reversed, gives a sense of scale [1]. No source opened for this note gave a verified Pillar Two revenue figure for low-income countries in particular.
- **The 2026 exemption.** Exempting groups headquartered in the largest economy from the IIR and UTPR weakens the "common approach". The 2029 stocktake is the only built-in check on whether it reopens profit shifting [10][11].

## 3. The UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation

**Timeline (verified).**

- **22 December 2023: UNGA resolution 78/230.** Adopted by 111 votes to 46, with 10 abstentions [12]. It "decides to establish a Member State-led, open-ended ad hoc intergovernmental committee for the purpose of drafting terms of reference for a United Nations framework convention on international tax cooperation" [12].
- **16 August 2024: terms of reference adopted.** The Ad Hoc Committee adopted the draft terms of reference by a recorded vote of 110 to 8, with 44 abstentions [13][14]. The eight votes against were Australia, Canada, Israel, Japan, New Zealand, the Republic of Korea, the United Kingdom and the United States [14]. The terms of reference (Annex I of A/79/333) list commitments that include:
  - fair allocation of taxing rights, including equitable taxation of MNEs;
  - addressing tax evasion and avoidance by high-net-worth individuals;
  - mutual administrative assistance and exchange of information;
  - "addressing tax-related illicit financial flows, tax avoidance, tax evasion and harmful tax practices";
  - prevention and resolution of tax disputes [13].

  One early protocol must cover "taxation of income derived from the provision of cross-border services in an increasingly digitalized and globalized economy". The second was to be chosen from four priority areas, one of which was "measures against tax-related illicit financial flows" [13].
- **24 December 2024: UNGA resolution 79/235.** It adopted the terms of reference and set up an intergovernmental negotiating committee (INC) to draft the convention and two early protocols at the same time. The INC is to meet in 2025, 2026 and 2027 for at least three sessions a year, in New York and elsewhere, including Nairobi. It is to submit the final texts "in the first quarter of [the Assembly's] eighty-second session", which means late 2027 [15].
- **3 February 2025.** At the INC's organisational session, the United States said the convention's goals "are inconsistent with U.S. priorities" and that it did not plan to take further part. It also said it "intends to reject the outcomes" [16]. The subject chosen for the second protocol was the prevention and resolution of tax disputes, so illicit financial flows did not get their own early protocol [17].
- **2025–2026: negotiations.** UN DESA (3 August 2026) reports that the INC planned nine sessions over three years, aiming to deliver texts by 2027. The fourth session ended in New York on 13 February 2026. The fifth session, in New York from 3 to 13 August 2026, was the first to engage with draft text. There are three workstreams: the framework convention, a protocol on taxing cross-border services, and a protocol on tax disputes [17].
- **Latest verifiable status (10 November 2026).** In a written parliamentary answer, the Irish Government said five sessions had been held and the sixth would take place in Nairobi from 30 November to 10 December 2026. It said a General Assembly vote is expected in the latter half of 2027 [18].

**What could not be confirmed.**

- The current wording of the draft convention or of either protocol. No consolidated draft text was opened for this note.
- Whether the sixth session will finalise any part of the text.
- Which states will sign or ratify. A General Assembly vote adopts a text; it does not bind states.
- How far commitments on tax-related illicit financial flows will survive in the framework convention now that they have no dedicated early protocol.
- Whether the other seven states that voted against the terms of reference in 2024 are taking part in the negotiations. Only the US withdrawal was confirmed from a primary source [16].

**Why it matters.** The UN process gives every UN member an equal formal say in drafting binding tax rules, under the General Assembly rather than an OECD-hosted body. The trade-off is that an inclusive convention may not include the home states of many large MNEs, which limits what it can enforce in practice.

## 4. Revenue losses compared with aid and food-security needs

**The numbers, each in its own terms.**

| Quantity | Figure | What it measures | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corporate tax lost to profit shifting | USD 100–240 bn/yr (2014 levels) | Modelled global revenue loss, all countries | OECD [5] |
| Corporate tax lost to profit shifting | ~US$500 bn/yr | Modelled global revenue loss, re-estimated IMF method | Cobham & Janský [3] |
| Corporate tax lost, non-OECD countries | ~1.3% of GDP | Modelled long-run loss, "tentative" | Crivelli et al. [2] |
| Pillar Two revenue gain | USD 155–192 bn/yr | Projected global gain, mostly not in low-income countries | OECD [8] |
| Net ODA from DAC members, 2025 | USD 174.3 bn (0.26% of GNI) | Measured flow (preliminary), down 23.1% in real terms from USD 215.1 bn in 2024 | OECD [19] |
| Extra donor money to end hunger by 2030 | ~USD 14 bn/yr | Modelled additional donor cost, alongside more spending by poor countries | Ceres2030 [21] |
| People facing hunger, 2025 | 645 million (7.8%) | Measured estimate | FAO SOFI 2026 [20] |

**Context.** OECD figures show DAC ODA fell to USD 174.3 billion in 2025, the largest drop on record. US ODA fell 56.9%, and a further fall of 5.8% is projected for 2026. These are preliminary figures; final 2025 data is due in December 2026 [19]. FAO's *SOFI 2026* reports 645 million people facing hunger in 2025, about 14 million fewer than in 2024 and 43 million below the 2022 peak. About 2.1 billion people faced moderate or severe food insecurity, Africa has about 309 million undernourished people, and about 520 million could still be hungry in 2030 [20]. Ceres2030 (2020) estimated that donors would need to add about USD 14 billion a year to 2030, roughly doubling food-security aid, alongside more spending by poor countries. In the FAO–ZEF model the total extra public cost is about USD 39–50 billion a year, of which donors would provide USD 11–14 billion [21].

**Comparing like with like.**

- **Stocks, flows and models.** ODA is a measured flow of transfers. Revenue-loss estimates are models of a counterfactual tax base. Ceres2030 models the cost of an additional programme. A ratio between them, such as "losses are three times aid", compares a measured amount with modelled ones and should carry the full uncertainty of the larger range.
- **Who loses and who gains.** Most of the modelled revenue loss falls on high-income countries in absolute terms [1][2]. Ending profit shifting would raise domestic profits by about 5% in developing countries, against 20% in high-tax EU countries [1]. So recovered revenue would not mostly land in the countries where hunger is concentrated.
- **Fungibility.** Recovered tax revenue is not earmarked. There is no evidence that it would go to food security rather than other priorities or debt service.
- **A defensible statement.** Even the low end of the global profit-shifting range (USD 100 billion a year at 2014 levels [5]) is several times the extra donor money Ceres2030 estimated would end hunger by 2030 (about USD 14 billion a year [21]). Reaching that donor figure does not depend on recovering shifted profits. It is a question of aid budgets, which fell sharply in 2025 [19].
- **Uncertainty.** Every revenue figure here has a wide range, depends on method and is several years old (2014 to 2021 base years). Ceres2030's costings date from 2020, before price shocks and the 2025 aid cuts.

## 5. Laundering the proceeds of human trafficking

**Scale.** The ILO's *Profits and Poverty* (2024) [22] estimates US$236 billion a year in illegal profits from forced labour, up 37% (US$64 billion) since 2014. Forced commercial sexual exploitation accounts for 73% of the total, and profits come to close to US$10,000 per victim. About 27.6 million people were in forced labour on any given day in 2021 [22]. The FATF–APG report *Financial Flows from Human Trafficking* (2018) [23] used the ILO's earlier estimate of US$150.2 billion a year, of which US$99 billion came from forced sexual exploitation [23]. Neither figure is a laundering estimate. Both are modelled profits.

**Typologies.** The FATF–APG report [23] and Canada's FINTRAC indicators [26] describe recurring patterns:

- **Funnel accounts.** Cash is deposited in many places and withdrawn in one. One US case in the FATF report used "funnel accounts to launder and route cash from cities across the United States" [23]. FINTRAC lists accounts that appear "to function as a funnel account" [26].
- **Victims' own accounts.** Victims are made to open accounts that the traffickers control. FINTRAC notes that victims "can also be used to facilitate money laundering by acting as personal/business account holders" [26].
- **Cash-intensive and front businesses.** FINTRAC flags card payments at massage parlours, strip clubs, beauty salons and modelling agencies after normal business hours. It also flags rent paid for addresses linked to prostitution, and small, repeated payments for online advertising [26].
- **Trade-based and corporate laundering.** In one FATF labour-exploitation case, profits went to shell companies "made to look like logistics companies through a false invoicing scheme", with victims shown as the apparent owners [23].
- **Bulk cash, money mules and hawala.** These moved proceeds abroad in the same US sex-trafficking case [23].

**The FATF standard and beneficial ownership.** The FATF Recommendations, adopted in 2012 and last updated in June 2026, are the global AML/CFT standard. FATF assesses countries against them through mutual evaluations [24]. They cover beneficial ownership, the identity of the people who ultimately own or control companies and legal arrangements. FATF published guidance on beneficial ownership of legal persons in March 2023 and of legal arrangements in March 2024 [24].

**The 2022 CJEU ruling (WM and Sovim).** On 22 November 2022, in Joined Cases C-37/20 and C-601/20, the Court of Justice of the EU (Grand Chamber) held invalid the provision of the EU anti-money-laundering directive that made beneficial-ownership information "accessible in all cases to any member of the general public" [25]. The Court accepted that fighting money laundering is a general-interest objective that can justify even serious interference with privacy. But it held that unrestricted public access was "neither limited to what is strictly necessary nor proportionate" under Articles 7 and 8 of the EU Charter, compared with the earlier regime of access for those showing a "legitimate interest" [25]. This note did not verify how individual EU registers or later EU legislation responded.

**Public–private partnerships.**

- **Project Protect (Canada).** Launched in 2016 as a public–private partnership led by a major bank with FINTRAC. Since it began, FINTRAC reports a 750% increase in suspicious transaction reporting linked to sex trafficking and 979 disclosures of financial intelligence to police (figures to July 2021). FINTRAC's analysis found the victims were nearly all female, and 60% were under 25 [26].
- **JMLIT (UK).** The Joint Money Laundering Intelligence Taskforce, now "JMLIT+", has more than 200 members from law enforcement, banks, technology firms, NGOs and others. The National Crime Agency reports that from 2015 to 31 December 2024 it identified more than 10,700 accounts not previously known to law enforcement, closed over 8,100 accounts, contributed to more than 391 arrests and put over £248 million under restraint [27].

**Effectiveness evidence and critiques.** These figures are outputs (reports, disclosures, accounts closed, arrests), not measured reductions in trafficking or its profits. The partnerships report no counterfactual or independent evaluation. Pol (2020) [29] goes further. He finds that the AML regime as a whole "has less than 0.1 percent impact on criminal finances" and that "compliance costs exceed recovered criminal funds more than a hundred times over". He adds that "banks, taxpayers and ordinary citizens are penalized more than criminal enterprises". He notes that the data are poorly validated, so the findings "cannot be definitive" [29]. Pol argues that the problem may lie in the design of the policy itself, not in banks' implementation, and calls for closer links with public-policy evaluation and outcome measurement [29]. Set against the ILO's US$236 billion a year, the JMLIT figure of about £248 million restrained over nearly ten years shows the gap. These figures cover different crimes and countries, so the comparison shows only the order of magnitude.

**Ethical risks.**

- **De-risking.** FATF's own 2021 stocktake defines de-risking as institutions ending or restricting relationships "to avoid, rather than manage, risk". It found that de-risking has cut correspondent banking in some countries and narrowed access for money-transfer operators and non-profits. Smaller and more isolated economies and conflict zones are hit harder, and some transactions may move outside the regulated system [28].
- **Financial exclusion of migrants and sex workers.** Indicators such as small, repeated transfers, hotel stays in several cities, or after-hours payments at massage parlours [26] also describe many migrants and consenting adult sex workers. Blunt and Wolf (2020) [30] report a sex-worker-led survey after the closure of Backpage and the adoption of FOSTA. It found that the financial situation of most participants had deteriorated, as had their ability to reach community and screen clients. The authors argue that anti-trafficking policy is being used "to police already marginalised people". The same logic of closing off platforms and payment channels applies when banks exit whole groups of customers. Exclusion pushes people towards cash and informal channels, which can make exploitation harder to detect, not easier.
- **Privacy.** The CJEU ruling [25] and FATF's stocktake, which covers due process and procedural rights [28], both recognise that transparency and monitoring carry rights costs. Bulk data-sharing between banks and police in public–private partnerships raises the same questions of necessity and proportionality.
- **Misplaced success metrics.** Counting accounts closed or reports filed can reward exiting customers rather than helping victims [29].

## 6. Proposals, limitations and open questions

**Proposals that follow from the evidence.**

1. **Publish Pillar Two revenue by country income group.** The 2029 stocktake [10] should report revenue by country income group and by collection rule (QDMTT, IIR or UTPR). The OECD's 2024 projections [8] cannot show whether low-income countries gain.
2. **Revisit the carve-out.** Phasing down the substance-based exclusion faster, or raising the rate, would recover much of the revenue the EU Tax Observatory says was lost to loopholes [9]. Johannesen's model suggests the benefit to non-havens is clearest at a rate high enough to end profit shifting [7].
3. **Keep illicit financial flows in the UN convention.** IFFs lost out in the choice of second protocol [17]. The framework convention's commitment on "tax-related illicit financial flows" [13] should therefore carry concrete obligations, such as exchange of information and beneficial-ownership access for tax authorities, rather than being deferred to a later protocol.
4. **Separate the aid debate from the tax debate.** Closing the hunger financing gap estimated by Ceres2030 [21] depends mainly on restoring aid [19], not on recovering shifted profits. Advocacy that ties the two together risks delaying both.
5. **Make AML outcome-based.** Partnerships like Project Protect and JMLIT should publish victim-centred outcomes (victims identified and supported, confiscations from traffickers) with independent evaluation, in line with Pol's call for outcome-based evaluation [29].
6. **Protect access to banking.** Pair trafficking indicators with explicit guidance against blanket exits of migrants, sex workers and money-transfer operators, in line with FATF's risk-based approach [28]. Track the account closures that monitoring causes.
7. **Beneficial ownership after WM/Sovim.** Give competent authorities, obliged entities and parties with a documented legitimate interest, such as journalists and NGOs, tiered access to beneficial-ownership registers. This is the proportionality line the CJEU drew [25].

**Trade-offs.** Higher minimum rates and fewer carve-outs raise revenue but reduce countries' room to use incentives. A universal UN convention gains legitimacy but may lose the largest capital-exporting states [16]. More financial transparency helps investigators but costs privacy. Tighter AML controls can increase exclusion.

**Limitations.**

- **Estimates.** All revenue-loss figures are modelled. They depend on base years from 2014 to 2021 and are not directly comparable [1][2][3][5][6].
- **Pillar Two.** Figures are projections made before implementation and before the 2026 side-by-side package. No updated OECD revenue estimate after the package was opened for this note.
- **UN convention.** Draft texts were not opened. The status is current only to the 10 November 2026 answer and the August 2026 DESA update [17][18].
- **ODA.** The 2025 figures are preliminary [19].
- **Trafficking.** Profit estimates are not laundering estimates. Partnership results are self-reported outputs [26][27].
- **Sources.** The Tax Justice Network figures are advocacy research [6]. One summary of Crivelli et al.'s revenue figures comes from the EU Tax Observatory's research digest rather than the paper's full text [2].

**Open questions.**

- How much Pillar Two revenue low-income countries actually collect through QDMTTs once the 2026 safe harbours apply.
- Whether the 2029 stocktake will find renewed profit shifting by groups exempted under the side-by-side regime.
- What the UN framework convention will finally say on illicit financial flows, and how many states will ratify it after a 2027 vote.
- Whether public–private AML partnerships measurably reduce trafficking, and what they cost in financial exclusion.
- How the EU has balanced register access and privacy after WM/Sovim.
