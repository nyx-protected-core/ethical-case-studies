# Ethical case study

## Low-cost well and handpump upgrades for fresh water access in sub-Saharan Africa

Status date: 18 November 2026. Every figure below carries a source tag `[S#]` that points to SOURCES.md. Items marked **(unverified)** could not be confirmed from a text opened for this study. Items marked **(our arithmetic)** are simple calculations from cited figures. A companion case in this collection covers the wider financing and rights questions of safe drinking water and sanitation. This one is about cheap, practical upgrades to wells and handpumps that already exist, and about the maintenance systems that decide whether those upgrades last.

## 1. Why this is an ethics question

Drilling a new borehole is visible, photogenic and easy to count as "access". Keeping an existing well clean, a handpump working and a spare-parts store stocked is none of those things. Yet a large share of the rural water investment already made in sub-Saharan Africa sits idle at any moment [S8][S9], and the people who pay for that failure in time, money and illness are usually the poorest users of the point, most often women and girls [S1][S4][S5][S6].

The ethical questions are therefore practical as well as distributive:

- Who is responsible for keeping a water point working after the drilling crew leaves?
- When should a donor or government rehabilitate or upgrade what is already in the ground, rather than drill again?
- How should the time that women and girls spend fetching water be treated: as a private household chore, or as a barrier to equal participation in school, paid work and public life?
- Which upgrades actually last, and which create a new form of aid dependence (a pump that only works while an outside agency refills, repairs or unlocks it)?

This case study reviews the evidence on cheap, easy-to-implement upgrades, their rough costs and maintenance needs, and the arguments for and against the main ways of paying for upkeep.

## 2. The access problem

The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) household report published in August 2025, with estimates through 2024, remains the current household report as of this status date [S1][S2][S3].

In 2024, 2.1 billion people (one in four) still lacked safely managed drinking water. Of the 287 million people who used only a "limited" service (an improved source more than a 30-minute round trip away), 167 million lived in sub-Saharan Africa, more than a third of them in Ethiopia (37 million) and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (26 million) [S1][S2]. A further 106 million people worldwide still drank surface water [S1].

Rural coverage of safely managed drinking water rose from 50% to 60% between 2015 and 2024, while urban coverage stayed at 83%. The urban–rural gap in 2024 was 35 percentage points in sub-Saharan Africa for safely managed services, and 34 percentage points for at least basic services [S1]. Annex estimates for the region in 2024 put at least basic coverage at 68% overall (53% rural, 86% urban). Of the regional population of about 1.24 billion, 13% used a limited service, 13% an unimproved source and 6% surface water [S1].

Water on premises, the JMP's test of accessibility, is still unusual in the region: only 33% of people in sub-Saharan Africa had an improved source in the dwelling, yard or plot in 2024, including only 16% of the rural population and 55% of the urban population [S1].

Handpumps on wells and boreholes remain a main rural technology. A 2015 institutional analysis put the number of users of locally managed handpumps in rural Africa at around 200 million [S17]. That is a basic or limited service in JMP terms, not a safely managed one, but it is the service that cheap upgrades can protect.

### 2.1 Who fetches the water, and what that costs in time

When water is not on the premises, the work of collecting it is unevenly shared. The JMP's 2023 report, which had a special focus on gender and used data up to 2022, estimated that 1.8 billion people collected drinking water from off-premises sources. In seven out of ten of those households, women and girls were primarily responsible [S4][S5]. In the detailed breakdown, women were the main collectors in 63% of those households and men in 26%; girls under 15 collected water in 7% of households and boys in 4% [S4]. In sub-Saharan Africa, 45% of the population still relied on water collection, and women were four times as likely as men to be the collectors [S4].

Time use is also unequal. In Malawi, women and girls spent 52 minutes a day collecting water, against three minutes for men and boys. In Chad, girls spent eight minutes a day against 1.7 minutes for boys [S4][S5]. The 2025 household report, looking at later surveys, again finds women and girls more likely than men and boys to be responsible for off-premises collection, and more likely to spend over 30 minutes a day on it, with the heaviest burdens concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and Central and Southern Asia [S1].

An earlier analysis of Demographic and Health Survey and Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey data from 24 sub-Saharan African countries (survey years 2005–2012) found the same pattern among households whose collection trip took more than 30 minutes: adult women were the primary collectors in every country, ranging from 46% of such households in Liberia to 90% in Côte d'Ivoire, and when children collected the water, girls did so more often than boys (62% against 38%) [S6]. Those surveys are older than the JMP figures above and should not be added to them, but they show that the inequality is long-standing and that it is not uniform: in some countries men's share of collection is already substantial [S6].

The ethical stake is equal participation, not a special status for women as carers. Hours spent walking to a distant or broken pump are hours not spent in school, paid work, training or civic life. When a pump fails, the walk gets longer and the substitute often has to be paid for. In Kitui County, Kenya, alternative water for handpump users in the dry season cost up to USD 0.38 a day, or 6–14% of daily household spending [S20]. A maintenance system that keeps water close to where people live is therefore a participation reform.

## 3. The functionality problem: a quarter to a third broken

High rates of handpump failure have been documented for decades, and the headline figure has moved only slowly.

- In 2009 the Rural Water Supply Network (RWSN) compiled estimates for 20 countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Of 345,071 handpumps in that compilation, 124,709 (36%) were listed as not working [S7]. RWSN later described this as, on average, one in three handpumps not working at any given time, and noted that many of the underlying figures were educated estimates rather than full inventories [S8][S11].
- A 2016 update, using Water Point Data Exchange records from 11 countries, found that 78% of water points were functional (so about 22% were not). The more troubling finding was how early failure arrives: almost 15% of water points were non-functional after one year, and 25% by the fourth year after installation. That points to poor siting, construction and supervision, not only to later neglect. Handpumps were not uniquely unreliable: other water-point types showed similar functionality levels [S8].
- A 2020 review of data from 47 countries, now the standard scholarly update of those RWSN figures, estimated that about one in four handpumps in sub-Saharan Africa was non-functional at any point in time, equivalent to roughly 175,000 inoperative water points in 2015. Country rates in that review ranged from 11% in Burkina Faso to 30% in Togo and Côte d'Ivoire [S9][S11].
- An earlier paper on mobile monitoring used the older "one in three" figure for Africa [S16]. The honest summary is that a quarter to a third of handpumps are down at any time, depending on the year, the countries included and how "working" is defined. Binary functionality is a blunt indicator: it is a snapshot, often misses seasonal failure, and does not record water quality or yield [S11][S13].

Failure is not only a "software" problem of unpaid fees and weak committees. The Hidden Crisis study dismantled and tested 145 handpump boreholes in Ethiopia, Uganda and Malawi [S13]:

- 19% were sited in aquifers whose transmissivity was too low to sustain a handpump;
- the pump cylinder sat less than 10 metres below the water table at 38% of sites, which raises the risk of the pump running dry in intensive use or in a weak aquifer;
- screens were shorter than 10 metres at 29% of sites, and often poorly placed.

Physical siting and construction failures have to be diagnosed before money is spent on a new pump head. A study of more than 25,000 community-managed handpumps in Liberia, Sierra Leone and Uganda found that non-functionality was associated with the age of the system, distance from the capital, and the absence of user fees [S12]. Fees help, but they do not fix a borehole that was drilled in the wrong place.

Component quality and corrosion are a third, long-neglected cause. RWSN's *Stop the Rot* research documents rapid corrosion of galvanised iron rising mains and pump rods, and widespread departure from the official specifications for the India Mark II and Afridev, the two most common community handpumps in the region [S11]. In the field samples reported there, large shares of galvanised rising mains and rods were corroded or thinner than the standard, and Uganda has prohibited continued use of galvanised steel components for this reason [S11]. A pump that tastes of rust or drops its pipes into the borehole is not a maintenance problem that a village committee can reasonably be expected to solve with a rubber seal.

## 4. Cheap upgrades, rough costs and what they need to last

The upgrades below are ordered from civil works at the wellhead, through the pump itself, to treatment, power and monitoring. Costs are reported in the prices and years of the source. They are not updated to 2026 prices.

### 4.1 Seal the wellhead: apron, drainage, cover and sanitary seal

The cheapest upgrade with a clear public-health rationale is to stop contaminated surface water running back into the well. WHO sanitary-inspection guidance for a dug well with a handpump sets out the standard package [S21][S23]:

- a raised, sealed cover slab, with the pump tightly attached and no gaps;
- a watertight headwall;
- a reinforced apron at least one metre wide all around, sloping away from the well, with a collar that directs water into a drainage channel;
- a working drain or soakaway, so water cannot pond against the apron;
- a fence or barrier to keep animals off the well area.

WHO's technical note on cleaning and rehabilitating boreholes shows the same idea on a drilled well: a sanitary seal and a concrete apron, illustrated at about 2.5–3.5 metres in diameter, plus disinfection after work on the hole [S22]. Damage (deep cracks, erosion under the apron, a blocked drain, a loose pump flange) should be repaired as soon as it appears [S21]. These are local-materials jobs: cement, aggregate, a mason and a day's labour from users. This study did not find a recent multi-country price for a well apron alone. As a rough analogue, a randomised evaluation of spring protection in rural Kenya, which poured concrete, installed fencing and drainage and set up a user committee, cost an average of US$956 (standard deviation US$85) to build, plus about US$32 a year to maintain [S32]. That is not a well-apron price, but it shows that source-protection works of this kind sit in the low hundreds to around a thousand US dollars, not in the same band as a new borehole.

These works last only if cracks are patched and drains kept clear. They also do not treat water that is already contaminated in the aquifer, and they do not prevent recontamination after collection. In the Kenyan spring-protection trial, faecal contamination at the source fell by 66%, but household water quality improved less because of recontamination, and reported child diarrhoea fell by about one quarter [S32]. Sealing the wellhead is necessary. It is not sufficient on its own.

### 4.2 India Mark II and III, Afridev, and spare-parts supply

The India Mark II (or a variant) is used in 25 sub-Saharan African countries and is the most common community pump in 17 of them; the Afridev is used in 21 countries and is the most common in four [S11]. Both are public-domain designs intended for communities of about 300 people [S10][S11][S24][S25][S26].

They are not interchangeable, and the difference matters for any retrofit:

| Design | Recommended lift | Village-level repair | Corrosion resistance | Source |

| India Mark III | 10–30 m with a 63.5 mm cylinder; 10–50 m with a 50 mm cylinder | Better than the Mark II. | Afridev | 10–45 m | Designed for community-based maintenance. Open-top cylinder; the piston comes out without dismantling the rising main; the foot valve can be fished. A village caretaker can do ordinary repairs. Practical upgrades, short of replacing the whole water point, therefore include: converting a Mark II to a Mark III open-top cylinder where the depth allows; replacing an India Mark family pump with an Afridev in aggressive groundwater; replacing galvanised rising mains and rods with stainless steel or uPVC; and, in all cases, putting a stock of the parts that actually wear (U-seals, bobbins, bushes, rods, rising-main sections) within reach of a trained mechanic [S11][S18].

In the 2013 professional-maintenance trial in Kyuso, Kenya, the annualised recurring cost of repairs averaged USD 62 per repair visit, or a crude USD 127 per handpump for the year, including sensor-related costs. Spare parts were 28% of those costs. Cheap, frequently failing parts (rubber U-seals in 85% of visits, rod centralisers in 43%) were replaced as a matter of routine; three-quarters of spare-part spending went on three larger items: cylinders, rising mains and rods [S18]. That is a usable budget figure for what it costs to keep a fleet of community pumps in repair, not just to buy a pump head.

These upgrades last only if the spare-parts chain is real: the right parts, of the specified quality, in a shop or store that a mechanic can reach, at a price users or a service provider can pay. *Stop the Rot* shows that this chain is often the weak point, and that "stainless steel" parts are not always what the label claims [S11]. A public-domain design is not, by itself, a supply chain.

### 4.3 Rope pumps

Rope-and-washer pumps can be made and repaired locally, and they are often proposed as a cheap option for shallow wells and for household "self-supply" rather than as heavy-duty community pumps [S11]. That is a fair use-case. It is not a reason to treat them as a general replacement for the India Mark or Afridev on deep community boreholes.

A peer-reviewed pilot in Rumphi District, Malawi, inspected 48 rope-and-washer pumps installed on manually drilled boreholes, interviewed 472 users and tested water at the 24 sources that still had water. Where the pumps worked, most provided safe drinking water. But only 4 of the 48 pumps (8%) combined good water quality, a flow of more than 20 litres per minute, and unanimous user satisfaction. Using a professional driller did not guarantee a working pump or safe water [S27]. Malawi had at the time approved only three official handpump designs, and had no clear process for evaluating a new one [S27].

The ethical reading is double-edged. A technology that a local workshop can build and a household can repair can reduce dependence on imported pump heads. A poorly specified pilot, installed without a maintenance system or a quality standard, can leave communities with a broken device and a story that "alternative pumps don't work". Rope pumps belong in the upgrade mix for shallow, privately shared wells, with the same honesty about functionality that is now applied to public-domain lever pumps.

### 4.4 Rehabilitate a viable borehole before drilling a new one

A cluster-randomised trial in Ghana's Volta Region, with a cost-benefit study using 2014 prices, compared new drilling with rehabilitation of existing boreholes (India Mark II or Afridev). The initial investment was US$10,487 to drill one borehole and US$2,823 to rehabilitate one that was not working. The return on a US$1 investment was estimated at US$9.4 for drilling and US$14.1 for rehabilitation, with time savings the main benefit [S28]. Rehabilitation, in that setting, cost about one quarter as much as a new borehole **(our arithmetic)** and had the higher benefit-cost ratio.

WHO's technical note describes what rehabilitation usually involves: inspecting records, removing the pump, cleaning and developing the hole, repairing the sanitary seal and apron, and disinfecting with chlorine before the pump goes back [S22]. It is not always the right choice. If the aquifer cannot sustain a handpump, the screen is in the wrong place, or the casing has collapsed, a cheaper repair will fail again [S13]. The ethical rule is to diagnose first: test yield and water quality, inspect the hole, then rehabilitate when the borehole is still an asset, and drill only when it is not.

### 4.5 Solar pumping retrofits on existing boreholes

Solar pumping is not cheap in the same sense as an apron or a seal kit, but it can be a rational retrofit on an existing borehole that already serves a large village, a school or a clinic, especially where a diesel pump is running up a fuel bill.

The World Bank / ESMAP guide *Solar Pumping: The Basics* (2018) treats retrofits of diesel systems as a distinct market. In its worked comparison, a typical diesel pumping system had an initial cost of about US$13,000 and fuel costs of more than US$5,000 a year. A solar alternative had a higher initial cost of about US$30,000. Over the life cycle, solar came in at about US$59,000 against US$93,000 for diesel (36% lower), with a 3.6-year payback [S29]. For a community of about 2,000 people, the same guide puts a typical installed solar pumping cost at under US$3.2 per watt-peak, or about US$32,000–32,900 in the example, **excluding** borehole development, tanks and distribution [S29]. That exclusion is the point for retrofits: the expensive hole is already there.

A UNICEF review of experience with solar-powered water systems compared indicative initial costs per beneficiary of US$10–20 for handpumps and US$10–90 for solar, depending on context and size. It also recorded the trade-off: solar has low day-to-day running costs and a longer expected life, but when something does fail the repair is more specialised and more expensive, so a maintenance arrangement is essential [S30]. Solar can also abstract more water than a handpump. In aquifers with low transmissivity, that is a risk, not a benefit [S13][S30]. A solar retrofit should follow a pumping test, not a catalogue.

### 4.6 Chlorine dispensers at the water point

A point-of-collection chlorine dispenser is a simple tank of dilute chlorine at the well or tap, so that users can treat a jerrycan as they fill it. The idea is to make treatment free, visible and easy at the moment of collection, rather than depending on a bottle bought and used at home.

The health evidence is promising but should be stated carefully. A 2011 randomised evaluation of spring protection, not of dispensers, is discussed above [S32]. For water treatment more broadly, an NBER working paper (revised February 2026, not a journal article) meta-analysed randomised trials and estimated a 24–27% reduction in the odds of all-cause deaths among children under five, with a modelled cost of US$28–72 per disability-adjusted life year averted for dispensers, inline chlorination and free chlorine solution [S34]. A related Kenya working paper, also not journal-published, reported a much larger local reduction (about 63% in under-five mortality) from community-wide free chlorine, including dispensers, with a wide confidence interval; the authors themselves note that this is larger than the pooled estimate from other studies [S33]. These papers are scholarly but not peer-reviewed journal articles, and their mortality results should not be quoted as settled fact.

Two further cautions are required. First, household water-treatment trials do not always find an effect on diarrhoea: the large WASH Benefits trial in Kenya is the best-known null result, and is discussed in the companion water-and-sanitation case. Second, a dispenser lasts only as long as someone refills it, and as long as users actually dose their water. If refill is paid for entirely by an outside programme, the upgrade can become a new form of aid dependence. Taste and odour also lead some users to skip the dose. A dispenser is a high-value add-on at a working, protected water point with a funded refill route. It is not a substitute for a sanitary wellhead or a working pump.

### 4.7 First-flush devices and sand filters

These are often listed with well upgrades, but they do different jobs.

**First-flush** belongs to rainwater harvesting, not to wells. WHO's sanitary-inspection package for rainwater collection and storage describes a first-flush system as a device that diverts the first, dirtier run-off from the roof (where dust, droppings and debris have built up since the last rain) away from the storage tank, to waste or to a non-drinking use. The diverter has to be sized to the roof, kept unblocked, and drained. A coarse filter box on the gutter keeps leaves out but, as WHO notes, does not remove harmful microorganisms [S23][S35]. Where a community already collects roof water to supplement a well, installing or repairing a first-flush device and a covered tank is a cheap, standard improvement. It should not be confused with wellhead protection.

**Sand filters** treat water after it has been lifted, or treat turbid water at a small supply. WHO's 2024 *Guidelines for drinking-water quality: small water supplies* set the framework for choosing treatment at this scale, including filtration, as part of a water-safety plan rather than as a stand-alone gadget [S23]. A household biosand or slow sand filter, or a roughing filter at the water point, can reduce microbes when it is built and used correctly, but it needs a mature sand bed, a pause period, and consistent use. Like chlorine, it does not make a collapsed well safe, and it does not remove the need to protect the source. Recontamination between the water point and the cup remains a live risk [S32].

### 4.8 Sensor-based breakdown monitoring

Oxford researchers fitted GSM transmitters in handpump handles so that movement (use) and silence (likely breakdown) could be reported over the mobile network without a user having to walk to a office [S16][S18][S19]. In a one-year trial in Kyuso, Kenya, in 2013, covering 66 community Afridev pumps, a professional maintenance service used those data (and a call-in route) to cut average downtime from 27 days to under three, a ten-fold improvement, with 98% of pumps functioning at the end of the period [S18]. Information costs (daily messages and batteries) were 27% of recurring maintenance costs in that trial [S18].

A later cross-sectional study of a professional repair service in Kwale County, Kenya, found that 90% of handpump faults were repaired within three days of being reported, an order-of-magnitude improvement over community management. Reported diarrhoea in children was lower only in households whose pumps were repaired within 24 hours (adjusted odds ratio 0.35, 95% confidence interval 0.24–0.51). There was no reduction where repairs took longer than 24 hours. Reliability gains do not automatically become health gains [S19].

The ethical and practical limit is simple: a sensor does not tighten a bolt. Monitoring is worth the cost only where a mechanic, spare parts and a rule for response times already exist. Batteries and transmitters also need their own maintenance [S18].

## 5. Who pays, and who actually maintains the pump

### 5.1 Community committees

The default model since the 1990s has been a village water point committee: collect fees, hold a small cash reserve, do preventive maintenance, and call a mechanic when the pump fails. A mixed-methods study of 679 water points in four districts of Malawi found that this model performed poorly in practice. Preventive maintenance was rare, repairs were slow and of poor quality, and committees could not collect and save what was required: average savings were only 2% of expected levels. The arrangement had, however, "worked" for the state and for donors as a way of handing off responsibility for a public service [S15].

That is not an argument for ignoring communities. A study of more than 25,000 handpumps found that the absence of user fees was associated with a higher chance the pump was down [S12]. Users often will pay something for a service that actually arrives. It is an argument against treating a one-week training and a padlocked cash box as a maintenance system.

### 5.2 Professional service providers

The alternative tested most carefully in Kenya is a professional maintenance company paid to meet a repair-time standard, with government coordination and some blending of user fees, public funds and investor support [S17][S18][S20][S31].

- In the Kyuso trial, downtime fell from 27 days to under three, and 98% of pumps were working [S18].
- In Kwale, 90% of faults were repaired within three days [S19].
- In Kitui, professionalised maintenance reduced repair times to under two days for piped schemes (otherwise 46–67 days) and to about one day for handpumps (otherwise 43 days). Uptime for professionally maintained handpumps was 99%, against 86% under community management [S20]. Over a ten-year period, the brief estimates that users can spend as much on coping with failed water points as is spent on the capital cost of the handpumps or piped schemes themselves [S20].

The published "FundiFix" model makes the performance rule explicit: repair within three days, or the community receives a free month of service. Users pre-pay by mobile money; a local office holds quality spare parts; contracts are with a maintenance provider, not with a company that also has to live down a legacy of poor drilling [S31]. An institutional paper on the same Kenyan setting argues that financial risk has to be pooled across many water points if the model is to stand up, and that mobile payments can make those contributions more transparent [S17].

This is more expensive in cash than an unpaid committee, and it needs a dense enough cluster of pumps, a working mobile-money system, and a government that will play its part. It is cheaper than the hidden costs of months of downtime [S20].

### 5.3 Pay-as-you-go and prepaid fees

Prepayment, including mobile pay-as-you-go, can improve collection efficiency and show users what they are paying for [S17][S31]. It also creates a distinctive ethical risk at a drinking-water point that does not arise in the same way for, say, a household solar lamp: a remote lock-out, or a pump left unrepaired because a tariff lapsed, cuts people off from a basic need. Any prepaid or pay-as-you-go design should collect money for a maintenance guarantee, not reserve the right to disable the only improved source. Unlocking and repair should work when the network is down. Terms need to be clear, and a public or humanitarian backstop should exist for households that cannot pay in a given month. User payment is part of a fair system. It is not a licence to turn off drinking water.

## 6. Ethical analysis

**Who maintains it.** A water point without a named, skilled, paid maintainer and a stock of parts is not a finished public work. Community caretaking is valuable for daily upkeep (cleaning the apron, tightening bolts, reporting a break). It has not, on the evidence from Malawi and from the Kenyan comparisons, been enough for timely, high-quality repairs [S15][S19][S20]. Professional maintenance is not a substitute for local voice; it is a way of making a promise to users that can be checked (repairs in a stated number of days).

**Local ownership versus donor-led drilling.** "Build and walk away" produces the early failure rates in the 2016 water-point data: a substantial minority of points are already down within a year [S8]. New drilling still has a place where there is no adequate source, or where diagnosis shows that the existing hole cannot be saved [S13][S28]. But counting new boreholes as the main success metric rewards the visible capital spend and hides the idle stock. Genuine local ownership means influence over technology choice, over tariffs, and over whether a contractor is paid, not a transfer of all future repair costs onto people who were not given a working supply chain [S15]. Governments remain the duty-bearers for a basic service. Donors that fund only capital, and not the first years of professional maintenance, are part of the problem they then come back to "fix" with another drilling campaign.

**Women's and girls' time, framed as equal participation.** The JMP evidence is clear that women and girls do most off-premises water collection in sub-Saharan Africa and spend longer on it than men and boys [S1][S4][S5]. The right response is to cut that time by making water closer in, more reliable and safer, and to measure success by time saved and by who can take part in school, paid work and public life as a result. It is also to keep men in the analysis: they already collect water in a minority of households, they use the same points, and equal participation includes sharing the remaining labour rather than treating water work as a female specialism [S4][S6]. Nothing in this evidence requires, or is helped by, arguments about family roles.

**Avoiding aid dependence.** Dependence is not the same thing as receiving public or donor finance. A professional service that is part-funded by government, with user fees and a published repair standard, is a public service. Dependence is when a point works only while an external project is on site: a dispenser with no refill budget, a solar pump with no local technician, a sensor with no mechanic, a pay-as-you-go lock that only the vendor can open. Upgrades should be chosen for whether they can be repaired with skills and parts that will still be there when the project ends [S11][S15][S30].

## 7. Fair counterarguments

- **Some committees do manage.** The Malawi findings are from one country and one period [S15]. User fees are associated with better functionality in the large West and East African data set [S12]. With real post-construction support, some community systems perform better than the average. The case for professional providers is that the average, and the downtime that users actually experience, has been too poor for too long, not that no committee ever succeeds.
- **Sensors and professional firms bring their own failures.** Transmitters break, subscriptions lapse, and a company can over-promise a three-day guarantee it cannot meet in the rains [S18]. Concentration of maintenance in one provider creates a new single point of failure. Contracts and public oversight have to be designed for that.
- **Health effects are not guaranteed.** Faster repairs were associated with less reported diarrhoea only when they happened within 24 hours [S19]. Water-treatment mortality results from working papers should be treated as provisional [S33][S34]. Sealing a wellhead improves source water more than household water [S32]. Anyone claiming a health dividend from an upgrade should say what downtime, what use rate and what evidence they are relying on.
- **Solar is not always "easy".** It costs more up front than a handpump retrofit, needs a specialist when it fails, and can over-pump a weak borehole [S13][S29][S30]. For many small communities a well-maintained handpump is still the right technology.
- **Rehabilitation can be a false economy** if the hole is dry, badly sited or contaminated by something an apron will not fix (for example, naturally occurring fluoride or a badly placed latrine). Diagnosis first [S13][S22].
- **Rope pumps and other local designs can disappoint** if they are rolled out without standards, spare rope and user support, as the Malawi pilot shows [S27].
- **User payment can exclude.** Fees and prepayment raise money and may improve functionality [S12][S17], but they can also push the poorest back to unsafe sources. A professional model that cannot subsidise those users, or that locks a pump for non-payment, trades one injustice for another.

## 8. The cheapest upgrades with the highest proven impact

In rough order of (low cost × evidence that the benefit lasts), the priority list is:

1. **Seal and drain the wellhead** — cover slab, sanitary seal, apron of at least one metre, drainage to a soakaway, and a fence — and patch it when it cracks. This is local civil work with a clear contamination logic [S21][S22][S32].
2. **Pay for maintenance of pumps that already exist**, with a repair-time standard measured in days, a trained mechanic, and a stock of specified spare parts. This is the intervention with the strongest operational evidence: downtime falling from weeks to one to three days, and uptime rising to around 99% in the Kenyan professional-service sites [S18][S19][S20][S31].
3. **Rehabilitate a viable borehole** (clean, reseal, redevelop, disinfect, refit the pump) before drilling a new one. In the Ghana trial this cost about US$2,800 against about US$10,500 for a new borehole (2014 prices) and had the higher benefit-cost ratio [S28].
4. **Re-fit the pump for the water it sits in:** open-top cylinders (Afridev or India Mark III) so ordinary repairs do not need a lifting crew; corrosion-resistant rising mains and rods in aggressive groundwater; an end to off-specification galvanised parts [S11][S25][S26].
5. **Add a chlorine dispenser only where refill and use will be sustained**, as a complement to a protected, working source, not as a substitute [S33][S34].
6. **On rainwater roofs that already supplement the well, add first-flush and a covered tank;** use sand filtration where water is turbid, with honest maintenance of the sand bed [S23][S35].
7. **Add breakdown sensors only where a repair service is already paid to respond** [S16][S18][S19].
8. **Consider a solar retrofit** on an existing, tested borehole that serves a large village, school or clinic, especially where diesel fuel is the alternative; not as the default replacement for every handpump [S29][S30].

The test of a good upgrade is not whether it was installed. It is whether the water is still there, clean enough, and close enough, a year and four years later, and whether women, girls, men and boys all gained time and a safer supply as a result.

## 9. Limits of this study

- Several important papers (the 2020 functionality review, the 2013 multi-country predictor study, and the chlorine-mortality working papers) are used from open abstracts, author or institutional copies, or, for the working papers, the NBER text. The chlorine-mortality results have not been through journal peer review.
- Apron and seal kits do not have a verified multi-country unit price in this study; the Kenyan spring-protection cost is an analogue, not a well-apron quote.
- Solar and Ghana drilling prices are from 2014–2018 and will have moved with cement, steel, labour and panel costs.
- Professional-maintenance evidence is unusually strong in a few Kenyan counties and should not be treated as already proven at continental scale.
- This study did not re-open the large household water-treatment trials (including WASH Benefits Kenya) in full; they are a reason for caution on health claims and are covered in the companion case.
- Binary "functional / not functional" remains a poor measure of service. Yield, quality, seasonal reliability and actual downtime should be reported wherever they exist [S11][S13].
