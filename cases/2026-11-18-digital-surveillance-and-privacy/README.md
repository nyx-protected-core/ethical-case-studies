# Digital surveillance and privacy: spyware, AI monitoring, shutdowns and a rights floor for every country

*Case study, 2026-11-18*

## The ethical question

States have real duties to stop crime, terrorism and trafficking, and digital tools help them do it. The same tools let governments, and the companies that sell to them, watch journalists, opposition figures, human rights defenders and whole populations at low cost. When does watching people protect them, and when does it become control? And how can every region, not just a few rich democracies, set and enforce limits that hold?

## Scale, with real figures

- **AI surveillance is widespread and cuts across regime types.** The Carnegie Endowment's AI Global Surveillance Index found that at least 75 of 176 countries were actively using AI for surveillance, including smart-city or safe-city platforms in 56 countries and facial recognition systems in 64 (Feldstein, 2019, Carnegie). The index itself does not separate lawful from unlawful use.
- **Commercial spyware reaches across borders.** Internet scanning by the Citizen Lab at the University of Toronto identified 36 likely Pegasus spyware operators and suspected infections in 45 countries; at least 10 operators appeared to be conducting cross-border surveillance (Marczak et al., 2018). The authors note that the list of countries where infections appear is not a list of customers.
- **Internet shutdowns are at a record.** Access Now and the #KeepItOn coalition documented 283 shutdowns in 39 countries in 2023, the highest yearly count since monitoring began in 2016 and a 41% rise on 2022 (Access Now, 2024). India issued 116 of them, the most of any country for the sixth year in a row (same source). Shutdowns were linked to conflicts, protests, elections and even school exams.
- **Legal protection is uneven.** UNCTAD reports that 71% of countries had data protection and privacy legislation by the end of 2021, up from 55% in 2015, but only 48% of least developed countries did (UNCTAD, 2022).
- **Accuracy is unequal too.** In an audit of three commercial gender classification systems, darker-skinned women were misclassified at error rates of up to 34.7%, against a maximum of 0.8% for lighter-skinned men (Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018). The US National Institute of Standards and Technology also documented demographic differences in face recognition error rates across many algorithms (Grother, Ngan and Hanaoka, 2019).

## Root causes

1. **Cost collapse.** AI and cheap data storage make it far cheaper to watch many people at once, which lowers the cost of repression (Feldstein, 2019, *Journal of Democracy*; Feldstein, 2021).
2. **A private market for intrusion.** A commercial industry builds and services hacking and monitoring tools for governments, with little transparency about who buys them or how they are used (Deibert, 2022; Kaye, 2019, A/HRC/41/35).
3. **Weak export and use controls.** Many states lack independent judicial authorisation or oversight of surveillance, and export licensing is opaque (Kaye, 2019).
4. **Law without enforcement capacity.** Even where data laws exist, regulators in poorer countries often lack the staff and budget to enforce them (UNCTAD, 2022).
5. **Security framing.** Shutdowns and mass monitoring are usually justified as public order or security measures, which makes them hard to challenge in the moment (Access Now, 2024).

## What has worked, or shows promise

- **Forensic research by independent labs.** Most public knowledge of spyware abuse comes from academic and civil society digital forensics, not from vendors or buyers (Kaye, 2019; Marczak et al., 2018). Funding this work makes abuse visible.
- **Regional treaties outside Europe.** The African Union Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection (Malabo Convention) entered into force on 8 June 2023 after its 15th ratification, giving Africa its first continental data protection treaty (African Union treaty record).
- **Export controls tied to human rights.** The EU's recast dual-use regulation (Regulation (EU) 2021/821) adds controls on cyber-surveillance items and asks exporters to weigh the risk of human rights abuse.
- **Buyer-side restraint.** The United States barred its own agencies from operational use of commercial spyware that poses risks to national security or has been misused by foreign governments against activists and dissidents (Executive Order 14093, 2023). Buyer restrictions shrink the market without needing every seller to agree.
- **Multi-state processes.** The Pall Mall Process declaration (February 2024), launched by the United Kingdom and France, brought states, companies and civil society together on the spread and irresponsible use of commercial cyber intrusion capabilities.

## Ethical tensions

- **Security versus liberty.** Lawful interception can stop serious crime, including trafficking and child exploitation. A blanket ban would remove real protections; no limits at all invites abuse. The question is authorisation, necessity and proportionality, not whether surveillance exists at all.
- **Chilling effects.** Research suggests that knowing one is watched reduces willingness to speak or search on sensitive topics, especially among people holding minority views (Stoycheff, 2016; Penney, 2017). Harm can occur even when no one is ever arrested.
- **Who sets the rules.** Standards written mainly in Europe or North America may not fit, or may be seen as imposed. Yet democracies are also buyers and hosts of vendors, and rights law binds everyone. Fair governance needs real authorship from Africa, Asia, Latin America and the Middle East.
- **Tools that help also harm.** Data tools used against trafficking (see this collection's case on financial data and trafficking detection) need the same safeguards as any other surveillance, or they can end up targeting the very migrants they aim to protect.
- **Biased systems.** Unequal error rates mean some groups bear more of the cost of false matches (Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018; Grother et al., 2019).

## Original proposals (this study's own)

These are proposals of this study, not established policy.

1. **A "rights floor" licence that any country can adopt.** A short model law, drafted jointly through the African Union, ASEAN, the Organization of American States, the Arab League and the EU, setting five minimums for any intrusive surveillance: independent judicial authorisation, a time limit, a named target, after-the-fact notice where safe, and a published yearly count of warrants. Sharing one text lowers drafting costs for smaller states.
2. **Buyer clubs, not only seller controls.** States that sign the floor agree to buy intrusion tools only from vendors that publish their customer countries and accept independent audits. This turns market demand into the enforcement tool, and any state from any region can join.
3. **A pooled regulator fund for the least developed countries.** A small levy on licensed surveillance exports, matched by development banks, pays for data protection authority staff, training and forensic capacity in countries that have laws but no means to enforce them.
4. **Shutdown impact statements.** Before any network shutdown, authorities file a public notice with its legal basis, area and end time, reviewed by a court within 48 hours. Exams and elections are excluded as grounds.
5. **Equal-accuracy testing before public deployment.** Face recognition used by public bodies must pass published accuracy tests broken down by skin type and sex, run by an independent lab, with a maximum allowed gap between groups.

## Risks

- Authoritarian and democratic governments alike may sign and ignore the rules; a floor without monitoring becomes window dressing.
- Vendors may move to jurisdictions outside any club.
- Over-tight rules could slow legitimate investigations into trafficking, child abuse and terrorism.
- A levy could be passed on to buyers or used to legitimise sales.
- Shutdown review deadlines may be ignored in a real crisis.

## Recommendation

Treat surveillance as a regulated public power, not a product. Every state should require independent authorisation and public reporting for intrusive surveillance; buyer and seller states should tie purchases and exports to published audits; and richer states and development banks should fund enforcement capacity where laws already exist but regulators do not. Build the rules through regional bodies in every part of the world so that no single bloc writes them for the rest.
