# Climate justice and loss and damage: who pays when the harm has already happened?

*Case study, 2026-11-18*

## The ethical question

Some climate harm can no longer be prevented or adapted to: homes lost to rising seas, harvests lost to repeated drought, cultures uprooted. The people who suffer most often emitted least. What do high emitters, both countries and wealthy individuals everywhere, owe to those who carry the losses? And how can the money move quickly, as grants rather than new debt, without the whole question being stuck in arguments over blame?

## Scale, with real figures

- **Emissions are very unequal between people.** In 2019 the top 10% of the world's population emitted 48% of global greenhouse gas emissions, while the bottom 50% emitted 12% (Chancel, 2022). The same study finds that 63% of the inequality between individuals is now *within* countries, not between them, so high emitters exist in rich and emerging economies alike.
- **Damage can now be traced to emitters.** Callahan and Mankin (2022) estimate that warming caused by the five largest emitters (the United States, China, Russia, Brazil and India) produced about US$6 trillion in income losses worldwide since 1990. The United States and China each account for more than US$1.8 trillion of losses. The study finds that high-income, high-emitting countries have largely benefited while low-income, low-emitting countries have been harmed.
- **Adaptation money falls far short.** UNEP's Adaptation Gap Report 2023 estimates developing countries' adaptation costs or needs at US$215–387 billion a year this decade, 10–18 times the roughly US$21 billion of international public adaptation finance in 2021. Public adaptation flows fell 15% that year (UNEP, 2023). Every gap in adaptation turns into more loss and damage later.
- **The new loss and damage fund is small next to demand.** As of 31 December 2025, pledges to the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage totalled about US$822 million, of which about US$414 million had been paid in (World Bank, as interim trustee, 2026). The fund's first call, the Barbados Implementation Modalities, now has US$342 million allocated. By late June 2026 it had received 176 funding requests from 119 countries asking for about US$2.8 billion in total (FRLD, 2026).
- **The wider finance goal.** At COP29, parties agreed a goal of at least US$300 billion a year by 2035 for developing countries, led by developed countries. They also called on all actors to scale finance from all sources to at least US$1.3 trillion a year, and encouraged voluntary contributions from developing countries, including through South–South cooperation (UNFCCC decision 1/CMA.6, 2024).

## Root causes

1. **Historical and ongoing emissions are concentrated.** A small share of countries and people produced most of the warming (Chancel, 2022; Callahan and Mankin, 2022).
2. **Voluntary, unpredictable money.** All contributions to the loss and damage fund are voluntary, so its size depends on yearly politics (World Bank trustee report, 2026).
3. **Liability fears.** Rich emitters have long resisted loss and damage finance that could be read as legal liability. That has slowed agreement and kept sums small.
4. **Debt and cost of capital.** The COP29 decision itself names high costs of capital, limited fiscal space and unsustainable debt as barriers for developing countries (UNFCCC, 2024).
5. **Under-funded adaptation.** When prevention is not funded, losses grow (UNEP, 2023).

## What has worked, or shows promise

- **A fund that grants, not lends.** The loss and damage fund exists and has launched its first call for country-led funding requests. Its Barbados Implementation Modalities are designed to support bottom-up, country-owned responses (FRLD, 2025 press release).
- **Global authorship.** The push for loss and damage finance came mainly from small island states, least developed countries and other climate-vulnerable nations. The fund's board is hosted in the Philippines and its first call was launched at COP30 in Belém, Brazil (FRLD, 2025).
- **Law is clarifying duties.** On 23 July 2025 the International Court of Justice delivered an advisory opinion on the obligations of states in respect of climate change, at the request of the UN General Assembly (ICJ, 2025). The request was driven by Vanuatu and other Pacific states. It gives states, courts and negotiators a common legal reference.
- **Attribution science.** Peer-reviewed methods that link emitters to losses, such as Callahan and Mankin (2022), turn claims of responsibility from rhetoric into numbers that can be weighed.

## Ethical tensions

- **Countries or people?** Responsibility can be counted by country or by individual. Chancel's finding that most inequality is now within countries means the wealthy in poorer and emerging nations also hold a share, while poor people in rich countries emit little.
- **Historical versus current emitters.** Older industrial powers carry the largest historical share; some emerging economies are now very large emitters. A fair scheme has to weigh both without letting either side escape.
- **Liability versus solidarity.** A liability frame may be more just but harder to agree; a solidarity frame raises money faster but may be smaller and easier to drop.
- **Speed versus safeguards.** Grants must reach affected communities quickly, yet they also need protection against corruption and capture (see this collection's case on corruption and accountability).
- **Non-economic losses.** Lost cultures, burial grounds and homelands cannot be priced, and a money-only frame may undervalue them.
- **Adaptation competes with loss and damage.** Both draw on limited public budgets; money for one should not be taken from the other.

## Original proposals (this study's own)

These are proposals of this study, not established policy.

1. **A two-key contribution formula open to every country.** Each country's suggested yearly share combines (a) cumulative historical emissions and (b) current emissions per person above a global threshold. Under (b), any country with large high-emitting groups contributes, whatever its region. Contributions stay voluntary but are published against the formula each year, so shortfalls are visible.
2. **A small levy on very high individual emitters' investment portfolios.** Chancel (2022) finds that most of the top 1%'s emissions come from their investments. Countries could agree a tiny coordinated charge on the carbon footprint of large investment holdings and send part of it to the fund. This study has not costed it, and the figure would need independent modelling.
3. **Pre-agreed payouts by event type.** For common events such as cyclones, floods and droughts, the fund agrees payout scales in advance with each country. Money then flows within weeks of a trigger instead of after a long application, alongside the existing request process for slow-onset losses.
4. **Community windows with local audit.** A fixed share of each grant goes straight to affected local and Indigenous communities through accountable local bodies, with simple public spending reports and spot audits.
5. **Debt pause clauses.** When a certified disaster strikes, lenders, including bilateral lenders from every region, pause debt repayments for the affected country for an agreed period so that loss and damage grants are not swallowed by debt service.

## Risks

- Formula-based expectations may push some contributors to leave the fund.
- An investment-emissions levy could be dodged through tax havens and would need the kind of tax cooperation covered elsewhere in this collection.
- Pre-agreed triggers may miss real losses or pay for minor events.
- Local windows can be captured by local elites.
- Debt pause clauses may raise borrowing costs for vulnerable countries if lenders price in the risk.

## Recommendation

Treat loss and damage as a shared global duty that grows with each party's emissions and capacity, not as charity and not as a single region's burden. In the short term: fill the fund with predictable multi-year pledges, pay grants rather than loans, and close the adaptation gap so future losses shrink. In the longer term: publish a transparent contribution formula covering every high-emitting country and group, use pre-agreed fast payouts for common disasters, and build community-level accountability into every grant.
