# Antimicrobial resistance: too little access, too much use, and a market that does not pay for the cure

*An ethics case study. Research only: nobody was contacted in preparing it. Every figure below was checked against the source's own text (abstract, full text or official document), and every DOI and link was resolved on 18 November 2026. The Sources section records how each check was done. Anything that could not be confirmed has been left out.*

---

## The ethical question

Antibiotics are a shared resource that wears out with use. Every dose, given to a person or an animal, helps select for bacteria that the next dose will not kill. At the same time, millions of people, mostly in poorer countries, still die of infections that a cheap, old antibiotic would have cured, because they never got one.

So the question is not simply "how do we use fewer antibiotics?" It is: **how should the world share a resource that some people overuse and others cannot reach, pay for new drugs that should be used as little as possible, and govern farm use that feeds billions, in a way that is fair to the people who carry most of the burden?**

## Scale

**Deaths.** The Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance (GRAM) study estimated that in 2019 there were 4.95 million deaths associated with bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR), including 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to it. The attributable death rate was highest in western sub-Saharan Africa (27.3 per 100,000) and lowest in Australasia (6.5 per 100,000). The authors conclude that AMR is a leading cause of death worldwide, "with the highest burdens in low-resource settings" [1].

**Trends and forecasts.** A follow-up analysis covering 1990–2021 estimated 4.71 million associated and 1.14 million attributable deaths in 2021. AMR deaths among children under five fell by more than half over the period, while deaths among adults aged 70 and over rose by more than 80%. The forecast for 2050 is 1.91 million attributable and 8.22 million associated deaths a year, with south Asia and Latin America and the Caribbean having the highest mortality rates. Under a "better care" scenario (better care of severe infections and improved access to antibiotics), 92 million deaths could be averted cumulatively between 2025 and 2050 [2].

**Lack of access kills too.** In a 2016 Lancet series, Laxminarayan and colleagues wrote that "no access and delays in access to antibiotics kill more people than antibiotic resistance." They estimated that universal provision of antibiotics could avert about 445,000 of the roughly 590,000 community-acquired bacterial pneumonia deaths in children under five across the 101 countries studied, a 75.4% reduction. In India, only 12.5% of children with suspected pneumonia were reported to receive antibiotics; in Ethiopia, 6.8% [3].

**Use is rising fastest where incomes are rising.** Human antibiotic consumption in 76 countries rose 65% between 2000 and 2015, driven by low- and middle-income countries, while consumption per person in high-income countries fell 4% [5]. A separate model covering 204 countries found global consumption rose 46% per person between 2000 and 2018, ranging in 2018 from 5.0 daily doses per 1,000 people per day in the Philippines to 45.9 in Greece. The authors found "both high rates of antibiotic consumption and a lack of access to antibiotics" [6].

**Farms.** Antimicrobial use in food animals was estimated at about 99,500 tonnes in 2020 and projected to rise 8% to about 107,500 tonnes by 2030; 67% of the use hotspots were in Asia, fewer than 1% in Africa [9]. A 2019 analysis described consumption in animals as "threefold that of humans." Across 901 surveys in low- and middle-income countries, the proportion of tested antimicrobials with resistance above 50% rose from 0.15 to 0.41 in chickens and from 0.13 to 0.34 in pigs between 2000 and 2018, with the largest hotspots in China and India and new ones in Brazil and Kenya [8].

**Money and poverty.** The World Bank's 2017 modelling found that, in a high-impact scenario, AMR would cut annual global GDP by 3.8% by 2050, push an additional 24 million people into extreme poverty by 2030 (more than 28 million by 2050, mostly in low-income countries), and could cut livestock production in low-income countries by up to 11% [20].

**A note on the numbers.** The widely quoted claim of "10 million deaths a year by 2050" comes from the 2016 UK-commissioned review chaired by Jim O'Neill [21]. Researchers have argued that this projection rests on uncertain assumptions and that such estimates should be presented with their uncertainties stated [22]. This study therefore relies mainly on the peer-reviewed GRAM figures above, which come with uncertainty intervals.

## Root causes

1. **Weak health systems, not just bad behaviour.** Where diagnostics, clean water, vaccines and trained prescribers are missing, antibiotics are used both too little (people who need them cannot get them) and too much (they substitute for diagnosis and hygiene). Mendelson and colleagues describe access to quality-assured antimicrobials as part of the right to health, undermined in low- and middle-income countries by missing diagnostic tools and weak health systems [4].

2. **Rising, unevenly regulated consumption.** Between 2000 and 2010 antibiotic consumption in 71 countries rose 36%, with Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa accounting for three-quarters of the increase [3]. Use of last-resort classes such as carbapenems and polymyxins grew quickly in rich and poor countries alike [5].

3. **Routine farm use.** Intensive livestock systems use antimicrobials routinely at sub-therapeutic doses. Van Boeckel and colleagues found that up to a third of the projected increase in livestock use between 2010 and 2030 came from middle-income countries shifting to large intensive farms [7]. Resistance can move from farms to people: the colistin-resistance gene *mcr-1*, first reported from China in 2016, was found in 15% of raw-meat samples, 21% of animals and 1% of hospital inpatients tested, and the authors warned that it marked "the breach of the last group of antibiotics" [11].

4. **A broken market for new antibiotics.** A new antibiotic that works against resistant bacteria should be held in reserve and used sparingly. But companies earn revenue per unit sold. Outterson summarises the result: even with research grants, "antibacterials have failed on the market because revenues are tied to volume sold" [23]. WHO's 2024 pipeline review found 32 antibiotics in development against its priority bacteria; only 12 counted as innovative, and only 4 of those were active against at least one "critical" pathogen. WHO also stressed that even newly approved products often do not reach patients, "in countries of all income levels" [26].

5. **A collective action problem across borders.** Resistance spreads through travel, trade, food and water. No single country can protect its antibiotics alone, but each bears the full cost of its own restraint. Littmann and Viens argue that AMR is therefore a distinct ethical problem, not only a technical or medical one, requiring contributions from citizens, policy makers, practitioners and industry [27].

## What has worked

**China: ending growth promoters.** China announced a ban on colistin as an animal feed additive in 2016 [28]. Afterwards, production of colistin sulphate premix fell from 27,170 tonnes in 2015 to 2,497 tonnes in 2018. Colistin-resistant *E. coli* in pig faeces fell from 34.0% to 5.1% of samples, and human carriage of *mcr-1*-positive *E. coli* fell from 14.3% (2016) to 6.3% (2019). The authors concluded that the policy "had a significant effect on reducing colistin resistance in both animals and humans in China" [12]. China then went further: Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs Announcement No. 194 (dated 9 July 2019) withdrew all growth-promoting drug feed additives other than traditional Chinese medicines from 1 January 2020, and barred feed makers from producing feed containing them from 1 July 2020 [13]. National data show that the share of antimicrobial use in food animals going to growth promoters fell from 51.78% in 2018 to 28.7% in 2020, although total use rose over 2018–2020 and use shifted from tetracyclines towards macrolides and beta-lactams [14].

**Thailand: a costed, cross-sector national plan.** Thailand's cabinet endorsed its first National Strategic Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (2017–2021) in 2016, giving it legal standing. Built on a One Health approach, it set five goals, including a 20% cut in human and a 30% cut in animal antimicrobial consumption by 2021 [16]. Thailand's official One Health monitoring report for 2021 records human consumption of 35.5 defined daily doses per 1,000 inhabitants per day, 33.6% below the 2017 baseline, and veterinary consumption of 399.8 mg per population correction unit, 39.3% below 2017 [17]. These reports do not by themselves separate the plan's effect from other influences in those years, such as the COVID-19 pandemic.

**India: professional societies setting the agenda.** In August 2012, medical societies from across India met in Chennai with central and state policy makers, WHO and national regulators. The resulting Chennai Declaration was the first joint roadmap by Indian medical societies for tackling resistance from an Indian perspective [18]. It is a model of a middle-income country's clinicians framing the problem in their own terms rather than receiving a plan from outside.

**The Netherlands: farm targets with transparency.** Total therapeutic antimicrobial use in Dutch farm animals doubled between 1990 and 2007. A mix of compulsory and voluntary measures with clear reduction goals, agreed between government, veterinarians and the livestock sector, then cut use by 56% between 2007 and 2012 [15].

**Evidence that farm restrictions help.** A WHO-commissioned systematic review found that interventions restricting antibiotic use in food animals were associated with 10–15% lower resistance in animals in most comparisons, and with 24% lower resistance prevalence in the human groups studied, most strongly in people in direct contact with the animals. The evidence for the general population was thinner [10].

**Paying for new antibiotics without rewarding overuse.** England's Antimicrobial Products Subscription Model pays a fixed annual fee, in bands of £5 million to £20 million, for access to new antibiotics against WHO priority bacteria, regardless of how much is used. Suppliers must also meet a certified standard for responsible manufacturing waste, to limit antibiotic pollution [25]. A review of national approaches found that the UK and Sweden were piloting "entirely novel" models, while the measures in France, Germany and the US were relatively minor, and that "international coordination will be important for 'pull' incentives to be effective" [24]. Outterson estimates that an effective global pull incentive must add several billion dollars to the revenue of a highly innovative antibiotic [23].

**Worldwide commitments.** In September 2024 the UN General Assembly's high-level meeting adopted a political declaration (formally adopted as resolution 79/2 on 7 October 2024) [19]. It:
- aims to cut deaths associated with bacterial AMR by 10% by 2030 against the 2019 baseline of 4.95 million;
- aims for at least 70% of human antibiotic use globally to come from WHO's "Access" group (older, lower-risk antibiotics);
- recognises that equitable and timely access to antimicrobials, diagnostics and vaccines "remains a challenge for developing countries" and commits to ensure greater supply in low- and middle-income countries;
- commits to "strive to meaningfully reduce" antimicrobial use in the agrifood system by 2030 and acknowledges the need to phase out medically important antimicrobials as growth promoters;
- sets a target of US$100 million in catalytic funding so that at least 60% of countries have funded national action plans by 2030;
- asks the Quadripartite organisations (WHO, the Food and Agriculture Organization, the World Organisation for Animal Health and the UN Environment Programme) to formalise their joint secretariat and to establish an independent panel on evidence for action against AMR in 2025.

## Ethical tensions

- **Access versus excess.** Restricting antibiotics protects future patients but can kill present ones where access is already poor. The pneumonia figures above [3] show that, for many low-income countries, the more urgent wrong is too little use, not too much. A policy designed for over-prescribing in rich countries can do harm if copied where children die untreated.
- **Who should restrain first?** High-income countries used antibiotics freely for decades. Their per-person use is now flat or falling while use in middle-income countries rises [5]. Asking countries that are now gaining access to bear the cost of restraint, without help, repeats an unfair pattern familiar from climate policy.
- **Livelihoods versus shared risk.** For small farmers, especially in low-income countries, antimicrobials can be a cheap substitute for vaccines, veterinary care and biosecurity. Bans without support can hurt the poorest producers, and the World Bank modelling shows those same producers have the most to lose if resistance spreads [20].
- **Reward versus access.** A pull incentive must pay developers a lot for a drug that is barely used. If that reward is funded only by a few rich countries, the new drug may be registered and affordable only there, while the resistant infections are concentrated elsewhere [1][26].
- **Present data versus present action.** Burden estimates for many low-income settings rest on sparse data [1][22]. Waiting for perfect data is itself a choice with costs, but policies should state their uncertainty honestly.

## Original proposals

*The following are this study's own proposals. They draw on the evidence above but have not been tested as described.*

1. **Pair every "use less" target with a "reach more" target.** National action plans and the UN 2030 targets should report, side by side, a stewardship measure (such as the share of Access-group antibiotics) and an access measure (such as the share of children with suspected pneumonia who receive an appropriate antibiotic). A country that cuts total use while access falls should be counted as failing, not succeeding.

2. **A worldwide subscription pool with access duties.** Building on the English model [25] and the call for international coordination [24], a pool of countries, open to all and with contributions scaled to national income, would jointly pay subscription fees for new priority antibiotics. In return, developers would have to register the drug, supply it at an affordable price, and license generic production in low- and middle-income countries. Middle powers with strong pharmaceutical industries, such as India, China, Brazil, Thailand and South Africa, would take part as co-funders and manufacturers, not only as recipients. Shared purchasing should reduce the risk that each country waits for others to pay.

3. **A farm transition fund financed by high-intensity use.** A modest levy on antimicrobials sold for routine prevention in large intensive operations would fund vaccines, veterinary services and biosecurity for small producers. The aim is to follow the sequence seen in China, Thailand and the Netherlands (clear targets, published data, then restriction) without leaving poor farmers to bear the cost alone [13][15][17]. Use data should be published at national level so substitution between drug classes can be seen early, as happened in China [14].

4. **South–South learning as the default.** The Quadripartite joint secretariat [19] should run peer exchange between countries that have cut use, starting with Thailand's One Health monitoring system and China's growth-promoter phase-out, for countries now designing their own plans. Lessons from countries with similar incomes and farm structures are more likely to transfer than lessons from rich countries.

5. **Fund the catalytic target first, and fund it widely.** The UN's US$100 million target for national action plans [19] is small next to the potential economic losses [20]. Contributions should come from all regions, including Gulf states, East Asian economies and large middle-income countries, so that no single group of donors sets the agenda.

## Risks

- **Restriction without access.** Stewardship rules applied where children already go untreated could raise deaths. Proposal 1 is meant to guard against this, but only if the access measure is collected and acted on.
- **Substitution and leakage.** Banning one drug class can shift use to another, as Chinese data show [14]. Bans can also push sales into informal markets.
- **Cost passed to the poor.** A farm levy could raise food prices or fall on small producers if it is badly targeted. It should apply only above a size threshold and be returned to the sector as support.
- **Pull incentives that reward weak drugs.** A subscription that pays for products with little clinical advantage would waste public money. WHO's finding that only a third of the pipeline is innovative [26] argues for strict eligibility.
- **Uncertain numbers.** Forecasts can be wrong in either direction [22]. Overstating them risks loss of public trust; understating them risks inaction.
- **Sovereignty concerns.** Countries may see worldwide targets as outside interference. Framing proposals as shared commitments with shared funding, rather than conditions imposed by donors, reduces this risk.

## Recommendation

Treat antimicrobial resistance as a problem of fair sharing, not only of restraint. The most defensible policy is one that **expands access to existing antibiotics where people die without them, while cutting unnecessary use where it is high**, and that pays for new antibiotics through jointly funded subscription schemes whose rewards come with duties to supply poorer countries. On farms, the record in China, Thailand and the Netherlands shows that clear targets, published data and phased bans can cut use quickly; the next step is to finance the same shift for small producers so that they are not the ones who pay. The 2024 UN declaration gives every country, in every region, a common set of targets. Its access commitments deserve the same weight as its restraint commitments, and its modest funding target should be met by contributors from across the world.
