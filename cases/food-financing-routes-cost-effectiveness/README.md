# Money that works against food insecurity: a deeper companion on costs, South–South finance, risk pools, debt swaps and Islamic social finance

*Ethics case-study source brief 10. Anonymous author. Prepared November 2026. Every factual claim is tied to a numbered source in the list at the end. Items marked **UNVERIFIED** could not be confirmed against a primary text that was read live for this brief.*

*Revisions (18 November 2026).* Revised after an independent review. The changes correct facts, fill gaps and tighten wording. The review is not named here.

Evidence labels: **RCT** randomised trial · **QE** quasi-experimental · **OBS** observational/descriptive · **MOD** model or simulation · **SR** systematic or portfolio review · **REP** programme or agency self-reporting · **EVAL** independent evaluation commissioned by the programme or its funders · **WP** working paper (not peer reviewed) · **ADV** advocacy analysis · **ANN** announcement or press release.

---

## Gaps this companion fills

An earlier case study in this series covers *which* routes exist: farm-support repurposing, debt swaps, IFAD and GAFSP, anticipatory finance, shock-responsive cash, school meals, remittances, zakat/waqf, South–South alliances and blended finance. It also gives a qualitative comparison and recommends sending the next dollar to national shock-responsive cash plus school meals. This companion does not repeat that overview. It adds:

1. **Real cost numbers on stated metrics.** These are cost-transfer ratios for cash vs in-kind aid, school-meal cost per child by income group, what GAFSP and IFAD evaluations actually say about efficiency, the US$0.37 blended-finance ratio in its full context, and what a remittance-price cut is worth. Every figure is labelled with its metric type, because the metrics are not interchangeable.
2. **South–South and non-DAC finance in depth.** This covers the volumes and evaluated effects of China's FAO trust fund and agricultural technology demonstration centres (ATDCs), Brazil's school-feeding model via the WFP Centre of Excellence, and India's in-kind wheat and food credit lines. Announced volumes are kept separate from evaluated effectiveness.
3. **Risk pools in depth.** This covers ARC's premium/payout record, payout speed, reinsurance cost, basis risk, and the Centre for Disaster Protection critique of evidence and subsidy.
4. **Debt swaps in depth.** This covers deal-level transaction costs and net fiscal space for Belize 2021, Barbados 2022, Ecuador 2023 and Gabon 2023, and why the IMF and others treat swaps as a niche tool.
5. **Islamic social finance flows.** This covers what the IsDB Group's Food Security Response Programme has approved and disbursed, and what (little) effectiveness evidence exists for zakat-funded food assistance.

## Read this first: the routes are not measured on a common metric

- **Cost-efficiency** is the cost of delivering one dollar of benefit. It is often expressed as a total cost-transfer ratio (TCTR, sometimes called "alpha"). A TCTR of 1.2 means US$1.20 is spent to put US$1.00 in a recipient's hands. It says nothing about outcomes.
- **Cost-effectiveness** is the cost per unit of outcome, for example per child-year of learning or per household kept food-secure.
- **Benefit–cost ratios (BCRs)** put monetised benefits over costs. They are usually **modelled** and depend on assumptions about discount rates, wage returns and avoided losses.
- **Cost per beneficiary** is a budget figure divided by a headcount. It is neither of the above, and it varies with the size of the transfer.

Most route-level numbers below are programme self-reporting or programme-commissioned evaluation. Very few routes have been compared head-to-head. The table in section 1 is a **juxtaposition of differently defined numbers, not a league table**. Currencies and years are stated each time and have not been converted or deflated unless said.

**What can and cannot be read across the rows:**

- **Within-metric comparisons are meaningful.** Cash is consistently cheaper to deliver than food: TCTR 1.2–1.3 vs 1.9 in Ethiopia [2], and lower per-transfer cost in four countries [1]. The DFID review still warns that "no transfer – cash, vouchers or in-kind aid – is universally more efficient than another" [2]. It depends on local food prices versus agencies' bulk purchase and delivery costs. The review also found that only two randomised studies analysed the full cost of assistance [2].
- **School-meal costs are not comparable across the three sources.** The 2020 report gives a *median* [4], the 2024 report gives *averages used for estimation* [5], and the survey gives *budget* per child [6]. The US$8 vs US$41 vs US$23.89 "lower-middle-income" figures should not be read as a trend.
- **GAFSP does not publish a cost per beneficiary in its evaluation.** As this brief's own arithmetic (not a source figure), US$1.4bn ÷ 8.6M gives roughly US$160 per direct beneficiary. That is misleading, because the US$1.4bn is a committed portfolio, not spending to date, and beneficiaries are defined differently across projects [8]. **No independent cost-per-beneficiary figure for IFAD was found.** That item is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **The US$0.37 is a leverage ratio, not a value-for-money ratio** [17]. It says how much private money came alongside public money in LICs. It says nothing about whether hunger fell. The same report shows mobilisation is concentrated in rated, middle-income markets, which is where food insecurity is least concentrated [17].
- **Value of a remittance-price cut.** No source read for this brief estimates welfare gained per dollar of remittance-cost reduction. That item is **UNVERIFIED**. What is supported:
  - Remittances to LMICs were about US$685bn in 2024 [15].
  - In Gallup World Poll data from 92 LMICs, remittances are associated with lower food insecurity, with larger effects for international remittances in lower-income countries [16]. That is an association, not a trial.
  - As illustrative arithmetic only, mixing 2024 flows with 2025 prices: closing the gap between the 5.04% weighted average and 3% on US$685bn would leave about US$14bn a year more with senders and recipients. That US$14bn figure is an **upper bound**, because 5.04% is the weighted average cost of a US$200 send; larger transfers typically cost less as a share of the amount sent [15].

## 2. South–South and non-DAC finance

### China

- **Volumes (self-reported).** China's Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs (MOA) says that since the China–FAO South–South Cooperation Trust Fund began in 2009, China has announced US$130 million in donations to FAO. It also says the fund has carried out 25 projects, sent more than 300 experts, and "benefited 100,000 small farmers directly and millions indirectly" [22].
  - These are **ANN/REP** figures. "Announced" is not "disbursed", and no independent evaluation of the trust fund's outcomes was found. That item is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **ATDC evidence (peer-reviewed).**
  - A triple-difference study on a country–crop panel for 2000–18 finds that Chinese agricultural technology demonstration centres in Africa raised crop production [23] (**QE**).
  - A farm-level study at the Tanzania centre found trained rice farmers' yields were more than double those of untrained farmers *on irrigated plots*, with no significant difference on non-irrigated plots [23] (**OBS**, one site).
  - Read together: there is some evidence of production effects. The effect depends on complementary inputs such as irrigation. Neither study measures household food security or cost.

### Brazil

- The WFP Centre of Excellence against Hunger was set up in 2011 to share Brazil's school-feeding (PNAE) and Fome Zero experience. WFP says it has worked with more than 80 countries [24] (**REP**).
- Its external evaluation for 2011–16 used mixed methods: 28 countries and 66 interviews. It assessed policy uptake and capacity, **not child outcomes, and had no counterfactual** [24] (**EVAL**). Claims of millions of children reached through partner programmes are self-reported.
- **Not sourced:** Embrapa-led technical cooperation was not documented with a source read for this brief. That item is **UNVERIFIED**.

### India

- India's aid is mostly in-kind food plus concessional credit lines, not grants through multilateral funds.
  - India committed 50,000 tonnes of wheat for Afghanistan, delivered in five consignments through WFP in 2022–23 [21] (**ANN**).
  - WFP reports that milling and fortification by ten Afghan companies produced 41,500 tonnes of flour. It says the donation supported 4.7M people in 2022 and more than 1M in 2023 [21] (**REP**).
  - For Sri Lanka's 2022 crisis, India reported a US$1bn credit facility for food, medicines and essentials, a US$500M fuel line of credit, a US$400M currency swap and a US$55M fertiliser credit line [21] (**ANN**, government statement).
- Credit lines are loans tied to purchases from India. They are not grants, and no outcome evaluation was found.

### FAO South–South and Triangular Cooperation (SSTC) generally

- No independent cost-effectiveness evaluation of FAO SSTC as a whole was located. That item is **UNVERIFIED**.

**Bottom line:** non-DAC food finance is large in announcements and thin in evaluation. The only peer-reviewed effectiveness evidence found is for China's ATDCs, and it is about production, not hunger.

## 3. Risk pools in depth: ARC

- **Premiums vs payouts.**
  - ARC Ltd reports US$56.1M gross written premiums and US$18.5M claims paid in 2023, and more than US$170M in claims since 2014 [12] (**REP**).
  - Its timeline records Malawi receiving an US$8.1M payout on a US$4.7M premium [12]. The report also shows a "65% loss ratio", but the period it refers to is not clear in the text read. That item is **UNVERIFIED**.
  - Premium/payout ratios in any single year are a poor guide for catastrophe cover. A year with few droughts looks bad, and a drought year looks good.
- **Pricing.** In 2021, reinsurance premiums of US$17.8M were 81% of expected payouts of US$22M. The original design assumed no more than 15% of risk would be reinsured. That raises the price countries pay for each dollar of expected cover [12] (**EVAL/REP**).
- **Speed.** The independent evaluation of 16 drought payouts found:
  - a median of **116 days** from the end of season to ARC payment, or 68 days excluding basis-risk cases;
  - only two payouts within 30 days of season end;
  - once the board approved a payout, ARC Ltd usually paid within its 10-business-day target. The delays came from basis-risk disputes and approval of the plans for using the money [12] (**EVAL**).
  - The evaluation also found most responses were not faster than traditional aid, and that insurance covers "a modest fraction of total need" [12].
- **Basis risk and evidence.** The Centre for Disaster Protection finds:
  - pre-arranged instruments were only about 1.1% of crisis finance in 2022 (about US$852M of US$76bn);
  - there is insufficient evidence that they benefit poor people;
  - no provider publishes basis-risk or trigger-validation analysis;
  - ARC premiums were about 60% subsidised in 2021, yet ARC was cheaper to countries than the World Bank's IDA Cat DDO (a contingent credit line) for risks rarer than about 1-in-6 years (2020 data) [13] (**OBS/WP**).
- **Parametric vs indemnity.** Parametric triggers pay on a modelled index, which allows speed and verification but creates basis risk. Indemnity cover pays on assessed loss, which is slower and costlier to adjust. The evaluation's speed data show that, in practice, the parametric speed advantage was largely lost to process and basis-risk disputes [12][13].
- **Comparator: social-protection payments.**
  - Kenya's Hunger Safety Net Programme (HSNP) emergency top-ups made 381,132 payments to 97,922 households between November 2016 and May 2017 (KES 868M). The evaluation found the impacts were limited because payments were small and unpredictable [10] (**EVAL**).
  - In Ethiopia, Productive Safety Net Programme (PSNP) transfers reduced the initial drought impact on food security by 57% and removed it within two years [9] (**WP**, QE).

Debt Justice's percentages are net present values, net of conservation commitments. Official and sponsor figures are typically nominal (undiscounted), and some are gross of conservation payments, so they are larger and are not like-for-like with the NPV column.

- **Across the four deals,** debt fell about 3% and the financial burden about 1%. The five most recent post-2020 restructurings cut external debt by about 21% on average, as of May 2025 [20]. Only four of eight recent swaps had enough public information to analyse [20].
- **Who supplied the savings.** The IDB describes Ecuador's deal as the world's largest debt-for-nature conversion. The IDB estimates US$323M of conservation funding over 18.5 years [19] (**ANN**). DFC and Ecuador's finance ministry put the total, including endowment growth to more than US$227M by 2041, at more than US$450M [25][27] (**ANN**). Debt Justice's analysis attributes most of the savings to public guarantees and insurance, not to the swap structure itself [20].
- **Why analysts call swaps niche.** An advocacy briefing attributes to "IMF and World Bank analysis" the sentence that "Debt swaps are generally not the right tool to address unsustainable debt situations," and links that sentence to the 2022 working paper [20]. The exact wording is **UNVERIFIED** in the working-paper text (the imf.org pages were blocked). The working-paper abstract, which was read, says debt-climate swaps "are generally less efficient forms of support than conditional grants and/or broad debt restructuring," because some of the benefits accrue to non-participating creditors. They could be superior where the climate commitment can be made "de facto senior to debt service," or "when grants or more comprehensive debt relief are just not on the table" [18a] (**WP**). The IMF/World Bank 2024 debt-for-development framework is cited for a similar policy point, but its full text was not opened here [18b] (**UNVERIFIED** at source).
- **For food security specifically,** no food-security debt swap of comparable scale with outcome evaluation was found. That item is **UNVERIFIED**.

## 5. Islamic social finance: flows and evidence

- **IsDB Group Food Security Response Programme (FSRP)** [14] (**REP**):
  - US$10.54bn endorsed in 2022.
  - By end-2022: US$1.41bn approved, US$152.03M disbursed by the IsDB across 78 projects, plus US$366M from its trade-finance arm (ITFC).
  - By end-2023: US$4.6bn approved (44% of the pledge). Accelerated disbursements reached US$0.58bn, 34% of the US$1.7bn planned for ongoing projects.
  - The development effectiveness report lists *expected* outputs (tonnes of crop and livestock production), not measured food-security outcomes.
- **The gap:** pledged US$10.54bn vs about US$0.58bn of accelerated disbursements by end-2023.
- **Zakat effectiveness.** No randomised or quasi-experimental study of zakat-funded food assistance measuring food-security outcomes was found in sources read live for this brief. Indonesian zakat-agency (BAZNAS) studies appear to measure poverty, not food security. That item is **UNVERIFIED**, and no study is cited for it.

## KEY FINDINGS

1. **On cost-efficiency, cash beats in-kind food in most documented settings, but not universally.** Examples are TCTR 1.2–1.3 vs 1.9 in Ethiopia and lower cost per transfer in four countries [1][2]. Apparent in-kind advantages in portfolio data come from scale, not modality [2].
2. **Early and pre-arranged spending is modelled to save money,** about 30% of US drought response costs in the Horn of Africa, or about US$3 per US$1 [3]. This is a model, not an observed result.
3. **Delivery through existing national safety nets is the best-evidenced crisis channel.** Ethiopia's PSNP cut the drought hit on food security by 57% [9]. Kenya's HSNP shows a local income multiplier of 1.38–1.93 [10]. Small, unpredictable top-ups underperform [10].
4. **School-meal cost per child varies widely by income group and by definition,** from US$8 budget per child in LMICs to US$665.76 estimated in HICs [4][5][6]. BCRs of US$4–9 per US$1 are modelled [4].
5. **ARC has paid more than US$170M since 2014,** but payouts were slow (median 116 days to payment) and expensive to reinsure (81% of expected payouts in 2021). The evidence of benefit to poor households is insufficient [12][13].
6. **Commercial debt-for-nature swaps freed little net fiscal space**: about 1% burden reduction across four deals, a net increase for Gabon. Their savings come mostly from public guarantees and political-risk insurance [19][20][25][29]. The IMF working-paper abstract says they are generally less efficient than conditional grants or broad restructuring [18a].
7. **Blended finance mobilises little private money in LICs** (US$0.37 per US$1), and mobilisation concentrates in rated countries [17]. Impact bonds mostly hit their targets but cannot show attribution without counterfactuals [11].
8. **Non-DAC food finance is visible mainly as announcements.**
   - China: US$130M to FAO, self-reported [22].
   - India: 50,000 t of wheat and a US$1bn food/essentials credit line [21].
   - IsDB: US$10.54bn pledged vs US$0.58bn of accelerated disbursements by end-2023 [14].
   - Evaluated effectiveness is limited to production effects of China's ATDCs [23] and a process evaluation of Brazil's Centre of Excellence [24].

## CONTESTED / UNCERTAIN

- **Cash vs food when markets fail.** TCTR advantages assume functioning local markets. Where food prices spike, food aid procured in bulk can be cheaper per calorie delivered [2].
- **Modelled BCRs** for resilience and school meals depend on discount rates, assumed wage returns and avoided-loss estimates [3][4]. They should not be compared directly with measured outcomes.
- **School-meal cost data** come from government self-report and differ in definition (median, estimation average, budget) [4][5][6]. The 2024 per-income-group averages are **UNVERIFIED** here because of a live block.
- **ARC's value** depends on the counterfactual. CDP finds it cheaper than contingent credit for rarer risks, but heavily subsidised [13]. The independent evaluation finds limited speed gains [12].
- **Debt-swap estimates** are an advocacy group's calculations from incomplete public data [20]. Sponsors did not provide data or respond. The direction (small net relief) is consistent with the IMF working-paper abstract [18a].
- **GAFSP's 22% income gain** rests on a "very small sample" [8]. IFAD's portfolio ratings are evaluator judgements, not cost-effectiveness [7].
- **Non-DAC volumes** mix grants, in-kind aid and tied loans, which are not comparable with each other or with DAC grants [14][21][22].

## WHERE THE NEXT DOLLAR DOES THE MOST (ranked by strength of cost-effectiveness evidence, not by size of claimed benefit)

1. **Cash through existing national safety nets, scaled up early in a shock.** This has the strongest evidence. There are within-metric cost-efficiency comparisons [1][2] and quasi-experimental outcome evidence [9][10], backed by modelled savings from early action [3]. Its weakness is that emergency top-ups must be large and predictable [10].
2. **School meals in low-income settings.** There is good cost data (though inconsistent in definition) [4][5][6] and modelled BCRs [4]. Measured food-security outcomes per dollar are rarer than learning outcomes.
3. **Remittance-cost reduction.** It is cheap to pursue in policy terms and has large flows [15], with a robust association with lower food insecurity [16]. There is no welfare-per-dollar estimate (**UNVERIFIED**).
4. **IFAD/GAFSP smallholder investment.** These are independently evaluated but rated weakest on efficiency, with outcome estimates on small samples [7][8].
5. **Sovereign risk pools (ARC).** Payouts are documented [12], but slow, costly to reinsure, and unproven for the poor [12][13]. They are best treated as a complement to safety nets, not a substitute.
6. **South–South technical cooperation (ATDCs, policy transfer).** There is some production evidence [23] and process evaluation [24]. There is no cost-effectiveness evidence for food security.
7. **Blended finance and impact bonds.** Leverage is low in LICs [17] and attribution is missing [11].
8. **Debt-for-nature or debt-for-food swaps.** Net fiscal relief is small and transaction-heavy [18][20]. There is no food-outcome evidence.
9. **IsDB FSRP and zakat channels.** Flows are large, disbursement is slow, and outcome evaluations have not been found [14] (**UNVERIFIED** for zakat).

Routes 3 and 6–9 are ranked low because of **evidence**, not because they are shown to fail.

## FAIR COUNTERARGUMENTS

- **Dependency.** Recurrent transfers can be argued to reduce self-provision. The HSNP evaluation instead finds transfers spent locally with income multipliers above 1 [10]. That is a model result, not proof of no long-run dependency.
- **Crowding out local markets and production.** In-kind food can depress local prices. India's wheat was milled and fortified locally by Afghan firms, partly offsetting this [21]. Cash can raise local prices: HSNP's real multiplier (1.38) is below its nominal multiplier (1.93), consistent with some inflation [10].
- **Fungibility.** Money earmarked for food or conservation frees other budgets. Swaps' conservation earmarks absorbed all of Barbados' savings [20]. Debt relief and budget support are fungible by design, which is why the IMF working-paper abstract treats conditional grants as generally more efficient [18a].
- **Conditionality.** Swaps, results-based finance and tied credit lines impose donor priorities or procurement from the lender [18][20][21]. National ownership may matter more than per-dollar efficiency.
- **The limits of cost-per-beneficiary.** It rewards thin coverage and large headcounts. It ignores transfer size, duration and outcomes. That is why the GAFSP arithmetic above is flagged as misleading [8], and why school-meal costs per child differ by a factor of about 80 across income groups [6].
- **Evaluated ≠ better.** Routes that are easy to evaluate (cash) are favoured by evidence-based rankings. Harder-to-evaluate routes (debt relief, South–South policy transfer) may be undervalued because of that.
