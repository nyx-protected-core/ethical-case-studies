# Ethical case study

## Academic senates for Ontario's public colleges: would a university-style bicameral model serve students, faculty and the public better than today's advisory councils?

Status date: 18 November 2026. Each factual claim carries a source tag `[S#]` that points to SOURCES.md, where every source has a public link and a note on how it was checked. Items marked **(unverified)** could not be confirmed from a text opened for this study. Items marked **(our reading)** are interpretations of a cited legal text, not statements found in it. When a working paper and a published article differ, the version used is named. This case is one of three on Ontario's public colleges. The companion cases cover full-time faculty staffing and digital textbook fees.

## 1. The ethical question

Ontario's 24 public colleges of applied arts and technology [S2] teach hundreds of thousands of students in roughly 3,000 programs [S10]. More of them now grant bachelor's degrees, and since 2024 some may grant applied master's degrees [S21][S22]. Legally, though, a college is still governed like a Crown agency. A mostly appointed board of governors holds the authority, and the Minister can issue binding directives [S1][S2]. Faculty, students and staff have an *advisory* council that gives advice to the president [S3]. No college has a university-style senate with its own legal authority over academic matters. Sheridan College built one in 2012 and dissolved it after a 2021 governance review found it inconsistent with the provincial framework [S7][S8].

The question here is not whether faculty ought to be "heard". Every party agrees they should. The real question is **who should hold binding authority over academic standards**: what is taught, how students are assessed, who is admitted, and what a credential certifies. Should it be a board accountable through the Minister, or a body in which the teaching staff hold a large share of the votes?

Three things are at stake ethically:

- **Integrity of credentials.** Students, employers and licensing bodies rely on a diploma or degree meaning what it says. When budget pressure can override academic judgment, that trust is at risk.
- **Professional voice and academic freedom.** Faculty carry professional responsibility for teaching. A governance system that gives them advice rights but no decision rights asks for responsibility without authority.
- **Public accountability.** Colleges are publicly funded and have a statutory career-focused mission [S1]. A body controlled by employees could put insiders' interests ahead of students, employers and taxpayers.

## 2. How Ontario colleges are governed now

### 2.1 Statute and regulation

The *Ontario Colleges of Applied Arts and Technology Act, 2002* sets each college's objects. They are explicitly vocational: to offer "a comprehensive program of career-oriented, post-secondary education and training to assist individuals in finding and keeping employment, to meet the needs of employers and the changing work environment and to support the economic and social development of their local and diverse communities" [S1]. Each college is an agent of the Crown [S1]. The Minister may issue policy directives that boards must follow, and the Act gives the government further powers to intervene [S1]. The same Act creates the College Employer Council, the colleges' joint bargaining agent [S1].

Regulation 34/03 sets each board at 12 to 20 appointed members plus the president. The Lieutenant Governor in Council appoints a minority of the appointed members (four for a 12-member complement, rising with board size), and the board appoints the rest. One student, one academic employee, one administrative employee and one support staff member are elected [S2]. Ministerial approval is needed for admission requirements and for the categories of credentials a college may grant [S2]. The regulation once had a separate provision on college councils. That provision was revoked in 2010 [S2] (O. Reg. 169/10), when the current framework took its place.

### 2.2 The binding directive on advisory councils

The Minister's Governance and Accountability Framework (2010) is a binding policy directive. It requires each board to ensure that "an advisory college council is established, the purpose of which is to provide a means for students and staff of the college to provide advice to the president on matters of importance to students and staff" [S3]. Each board sets its council's structure in a by-law, and the college's annual report must describe the council's work [S3].

College documents follow this design closely. Algonquin College's by-law says its Academic Council "acts in an advisory capacity to the President" [S4]. Fanshawe College's terms of reference likewise define College Council as a body that advises the President [S6]. Durham College sets out its council in a consolidated by-law [S5]. Under all of these, the council can recommend, but the president decides, and the board answers to the Minister.

### 2.3 The employer's position

The College Employer Council states the employer view directly: "The legislative and regulatory framework Colleges operate under makes a Senate unlawful" [S7]. It cites Sheridan's experience as proof [S7].

### 2.4 The Sheridan experiment, 2012–2021

Sheridan was pursuing university status when it created a Senate in 2012 [S7][S8]. The February 2021 governance review commissioned by its board describes that Senate as 72 voting members: 48 elected faculty, 19 ex officio members (including the president, vice-presidents, deans and the president of the faculty union local) and five elected students. Two-thirds of the seats were reserved for faculty, and both full-time and non-full-time faculty could hold them [S8]. The Senate's terms of reference gave it *exclusive* power over admission requirements, program content, graduation requirements and standards, and "all academic policies" [S8].

The review reached three main conclusions [S8]:

1. **Legal conflict.** Exclusive Senate powers over admissions and programs conflicted with Regulation 34/03, the Governance and Accountability Framework and the Minister's program framework. Those instruments make the board accountable for such matters. The review found no recognized power that let the board hand them to another governing body, although it could delegate to the president.
2. **Structural mismatch.** Universities usually link their two bodies by putting some board members on the senate and some senators on the board. That tool was largely unavailable because the regulation prescribes board membership. Administrative staff had no seats on the Senate, although the directive expects the advisory council to serve them too.
3. **Operational weakness.** People interviewed said it was "not uncommon that only one or two senators speak" at a meeting. A 72-member body also had little meeting time per member.

The review recommended either disbanding the Senate or revising it heavily into an advisory body. It also set out ways to keep collegial engagement: an advisory council that complies with the directive, a consultation policy that names the decisions requiring prior consultation, and better use of technology for broader participation [S8]. The board went ahead with abolition. On 9 April 2021, the faculty union's college division called the decision "a blatant and cynical refusal to work meaningfully with faculty." It said the legal concern was "spurious" and asked the government to "modernize college governance structures through a simple policy directive" [S9].

**(our reading)** The two sides are partly arguing past each other. The review did not say colleges are barred from having *any* body named a senate. It said a body holding exclusive decision powers that the regulation and directives assign to the board conflicts with the framework. The union's 2021 bargaining document quotes the Canadian Association of University Teachers as saying that nothing in the Act prohibits a senate "to advise on academic matters" [S13]. Both statements can be true. A senate that only *advises* is lawful. A senate that *decides* would need the framework to change.

### 2.5 Governance in the 2017 strike and after

Governance was a central issue in the 2017 strike. It was the longest in the college system's history and began on 16 October 2017 [S10]. In a 2026 decision on the union's Charter challenge, the Ontario Superior Court of Justice summarized the union's main issues as academic freedom, college governance and fewer contract faculty [S10]. The court recorded that the Council would not accept a settlement that "fundamentally changed" governance, "such as the establishment of senates or the inclusion of academic freedom language" [S10]. Back-to-work legislation took effect on 19 November 2017. The arbitrated award was issued on 20 December 2017 [S10].

That award did two things relevant here. It added a collective agreement article on academic freedom. Article 13 now protects "the right to enquire about, investigate, pursue, teach and speak freely about academic issues without fear of impairment to position or other reprisal", subject to legal duties, institutional regulations, Ministry directives, accreditation and program requirements [S11][S12]. It also created a Letter of Understanding, "Ontario's Public Colleges: The Next 50 Years". This set up a provincial task force to study staffing models and precarious work, funding, accessibility and student success, and "academic governance structures and intellectual property issues" [S11][S12]. The Task Force issued an interim report. After the 2018 change of government, the government revoked its orders in council in July 2018, and the work ended [S10][S14].

The court dismissed the union's claim that ending the Task Force breached freedom of association [S10]. Separately, the Court of Appeal decided in 2026 that the 2017 back-to-work law limited freedom of association but that the limit was justified [S15]. **(unverified)** We read this appellate result only in a third-party case summary, not the judgment itself.

In 2021 bargaining, the union called senates "the gold standard" for postsecondary governance. As a compromise, it proposed a **Faculty Academic Council** at each college: at least 24 members, at least two-thirds of them faculty, meeting at least nine times a year, with the right to advise the board directly [S13]. Governance came back in the 2024–2025 round. In the July 2025 arbitration award, the employer described the union's governance proposals as "beyond jurisdiction as set out in the governing legislation". The union argued that the financial crisis made them "even more imperative". The award dismissed every proposal it did not specifically address, and it did not award the governance proposals [S36]. The 2024–2027 collective agreement contains the academic freedom article and still carries the Letter of Understanding's list of topics, including academic governance. It contains no faculty academic council provision [S12]. **(our reading)** The proposal was therefore not adopted in the agreement now in force.

## 3. What a university bicameral senate actually is

In Ontario's bicameral universities, legal authority is split by statute. The *York University Act, 1965*, a typical example, says "The Senate is responsible for the academic policy of the University". It gives the senate power over admission standards, curricula, examinations, graduation requirements and degrees, while the board of governors holds the property, finances and senior appointments [S16][S17].

The University of Toronto is often given as the standard example, but it has not been bicameral since 1972. Since 1 July 1972, a single 50-member Governing Council has held the powers of the former Senate and Board [S18]. This matters for the argument. One of Ontario's most prestigious academic institutions has a *unicameral* design, so a separate senate is not the only route to strong academic self-governance.

Research on Canadian university senates is not uncritical. A national survey by Jones, Shanahan and Goyan (2004) raised "concerns over the effectiveness of the senate" and called for a review of its role [S23]. A follow-up survey a decade later examined senate size, composition and authority [S24]. Its companion paper found "a sizeable discrepancy between senate members' perceptions of the importance of effective academic oversight, and their success at achieving this" [S25]. The authors recommended clearer spheres of authority, better senate performance reviews and a culture of trust between governing bodies [S25]. In short, a senate does not guarantee collegial governance.

## 4. Models closer to home: colleges with legislated academic bodies

A university senate is not the only alternative to Ontario's advisory council. Other provinces give *college* academic bodies defined decision powers without copying the university design.

### 4.1 British Columbia: education councils

BC's *College and Institute Act* requires every institution to have an education council [S19]. It has 20 voting members: ten elected faculty, four students, four administrators appointed by the president and two support staff [S19]. The board must seek the council's advice, and the council must give it, on the development of educational policy for a statutory list of matters. These include the mission and educational goals, proposals for new credit programs, priorities for new programs, cancellation of programs or courses, program evaluation, the academic schedule, policies on faculty qualifications, admission policies and the criteria for awarding credentials. The board must send the council the agenda items at least ten working days before it deals with such a matter [S19].

The council also has its own **power** to set policy on examinations and evaluation of student performance, student withdrawal, academic standing and grading, the criteria for awards, and academic appeals. It can also "set curriculum content for courses leading to certificates, diplomas or degrees". All of these powers are subject to the minister's policy and directives [S19]. Decisions on curriculum equivalency (whether courses or credits from elsewhere count as equivalent), and any other matters the two bodies agree to share, need **joint** approval of the board and council. If they do not agree within 60 days, the minister may decide [S19]. The board keeps control of the property, revenue, expenditures and business of the institution, and decides which courses or programs are offered or cancelled [S19].

This is a hybrid. Faculty-majority bodies decide academic standards, while the publicly accountable board decides money and program mix. It differs from a university senate in two ways. Faculty hold half the voting seats rather than a large majority, and the board, not the council, decides which programs exist.

Levin's comparative study (2000) of community college law in Canada and the United States found that "except in one legal jurisdiction, shared governance is either impeded legally or not supported by government legislation" [S26]. The abstract does not name the exception. **(unverified)** We believe from secondary descriptions that it is BC, but we did not confirm this in the full text. Dennison and Schuetze (2004) describe BC's later reforms, including recognition of the right of colleges and institutes to confer applied degrees [S27]. **(our reading)** BC shows that a college system can expand degree granting while keeping a non-senate council design.

### 4.2 Quebec: CEGEP academic councils

Quebec's *General and Vocational Colleges Act* requires each CEGEP board to set up an academic council [S20]. The academic dean chairs it. Its members include program staff, teachers and professionals elected by their peers, and students [S20]. It advises the board on programs and on the evaluation of learning, and the board must consult it on specific matters. These include the institutional policy on evaluating student achievement, program proposals, admission rules and the parts of the strategic plan within its field [S20]. The Quebec body is advisory, but the matters on which it must be consulted are set in law rather than left to a college by-law.

### 4.3 Alberta: academic councils

Alberta's *Post-secondary Learning Act* creates academic councils for comprehensive community colleges and polytechnics [S28]. Members are the president (who chairs unless a chair is elected), up to four senior officials, up to ten academic staff elected by the staff association (who must be at least one-third of the members), up to ten students and up to five board appointees [S28]. The council must make recommendations or reports to the board on matters the board refers to it, including admission standards, programs and academic awards. It must review proposed programs, and it may report on any other matter it thinks advisable. Its reports go to the board in writing through the president [S28]. With the Minister's written approval, an institution that meets criteria set by regulation may set up an alternative academic council [S28].

### 4.4 What the comparison shows

The four provinces span a spectrum:

| Province | Body | Faculty share of votes | Legal powers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ontario | Advisory college council (by-law) | Set by each college | Advice to the president [S3] |
| Quebec | Academic council (statute) | Teachers among several groups | Mandatory advice on listed matters [S20] |
| Alberta | Academic council (statute) | At least one-third | Recommendations and program review [S28] |
| BC | Education council (statute) | 10 of 20 | Decides listed academic policy and curriculum content; joint approval on some matters [S19] |
| Ontario universities, e.g. York | Senate (statute) | Usually a large majority (varies) | Academic policy, admissions, curricula, degrees [S16] |

Ontario is at the weakest end. Its council's only formal right is to advise the president, and each college writes its council's own rules.

## 5. Why the question is more pressing now: colleges granting degrees

Ontario first allowed colleges to grant bachelor's degrees within limits. In April 2022 the government expanded this to three-year applied degrees and raised the cap on degree programs to 20% of programs at institutes of technology and 10% at other colleges [S21]. A 2024 change allows applied master's degrees under conditions set in regulation [S22].

The Postsecondary Education Quality Assessment Board (PEQAB) reviews college degree proposals. Its 2026 manual for four-year college degrees sets eleven standards [S29]. They include an "Academic Freedom and Integrity" standard. They also require that at least half of a student's degree-program experience be in courses taught by faculty holding the terminal credential, and that the institution specify faculty teaching loads [S29].

**(our reading)** This creates a tension. The quality standard expects the faculty voice and academic freedom typical of degree-granting institutions. The governance law still treats academic matters as the board's business, with faculty only able to advise. As more of a college's programs become degrees, the case that degree standards should be set by people who teach and assess degree work gets stronger. It does not follow that every certificate and diploma decision should move to a faculty-majority body.

## 6. Ethical analysis

### 6.1 The case for a stronger academic body

**Responsibility should match authority.** The collective agreement defines a professor's duties to include designing and revising courses, defining course objectives, specifying learning approaches and "selecting or approving textbooks and learning materials" [S12]. Faculty are responsible for academic quality but have no formal right to decide the policies that shape it. When responsibility and authority are split like this, accountability gets blurred. Each side can blame the other when standards slip.

**Expertise and information.** Economists of university governance have asked whether faculty control helps or harms institutions. An early study by McCormick and Meiners (1988) used a 1970 survey of faculty participation. As summarized in a later paper, it found that university performance fell as faculty control over decision making rose [S30]. Brown then split the same survey by decision type. In the **1999 Claremont working paper** version, he argued that "the optimal level of faculty participation varies by decision type". The results fit the view that faculty participation matters "in decisions where faculty members have better information and better incentives than administrators or trustees", such as appointments, promotion and tenure. Faculty participation in general administrative decisions, by contrast, was associated with lower performance [S30]. In the working paper, faculty control over financial decisions showed no consistently significant negative relationship [S30]. The **2001 published version** in the *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* carries the same title and research question [S31]. **(unverified)** We did not read its full text, so we do not claim its estimates match the working paper's. Two cautions apply to both versions. They are correlational, and they use US four-year university data that are now very old. Still, the pattern supports a division of labour. Faculty should decide academic matters where they have the best information, and boards should decide finances.

**Protecting the mission from short-term pressures.** Ontario colleges are under acute financial strain. In 2021–22, provincial funding per college student was $6,891, 44% of the figure for the rest of Canada [S32]. In 2020–21, international student tuition made up 31% of college revenue [S32]. After the federal cap on study permits, the colleges' own association reported cutting $1.4 billion in costs, suspending more than 600 programs and eliminating more than 8,000 positions [S33]. Under that pressure, academic choices such as class sizes, delivery mode, assessment design and program closures can be made mostly on budget grounds. A body with defined academic authority would not prevent cuts. It would make the academic consequences of a cut something that must be argued and recorded in public.

**Shared governance and effectiveness.** Birnbaum argues that proposals to make governance "more efficient" by limiting faculty participation "are likely to diminish institutional effectiveness" [S34]. This is an argument from the literature, not a causal estimate, but it fits a widely held view among governance scholars.

**Academic freedom needs institutional support.** Hogan and Trotter (2013) examine academic freedom in BC and Ontario colleges and institutes through their history. They treat collegiality, faculty engagement and governance as factors that shape how academic freedom works in practice [S35]. Since 2017, Ontario college faculty have had a contractual academic freedom clause [S12]. A right to speak freely about academic issues matters less if no body is obliged to act on what is said.

**Fairness to contract faculty.** Most people teaching in Ontario colleges are not full-time faculty (see the companion case). Sheridan's Senate had seats for both full-time and non-full-time faculty [S8]. A legislated academic body could give contract faculty a voice they lack under advisory models. That depends on the body's design.

### 6.2 The case against a university-style senate

**The statutory mission is different.** Colleges exist in part "to meet the needs of employers" [S1]. Employers and the public sit on program advisory committees and boards. A faculty-majority senate with exclusive power over curriculum could give employees control over a mission defined partly by outside needs. This argument should be taken seriously. Employers, students and communities depend on colleges responding quickly to labour-market changes.

**Accountability runs through the board to the Minister.** Colleges are Crown agents, and their boards answer to a Minister who answers to the legislature [S1]. An exclusive academic authority held by employees would create a second line of authority that the Minister cannot direct. That is the point for its supporters, and the problem for its critics.

**Senates underperform.** Canadian research reports real gaps between what senates are supposed to do and what they achieve [S23][S25]. Sheridan's review recorded weak engagement in a 72-member body [S8]. A large, slow body could delay program changes that students need.

**Conflict of interest.** Faculty are also bargaining-unit members. Some academic decisions, such as delivery mode, workload-related assessment design and program closures, affect jobs. The Council sees governance demands as bargaining issues [S7][S10]. A senate controlled by bargaining-unit members could blur the line between academic judgment and labour interest.

**The advisory model can work.** Ontario's colleges vary widely. Sheridan's own review found councils ranging from about ten to 20 voting members and from monthly to three-to-five meetings a year [S8]. A well-run advisory council with a president who listens may achieve much of what a senate would.

### 6.3 Weighing the arguments

**(our reading)** The counterarguments are real, but most of them argue against *a university-style senate with exclusive powers*. They do not argue against *any binding academic authority*. The BC model answers several of them:

- the board keeps finances and decides which programs exist, which protects the employer-responsive mission [S19];
- faculty hold half the votes, not a large majority, and students, administrators and support staff are included [S19];
- matters on which board and council must agree have a deadline and a ministerial tie-break [S19];
- decision powers are listed and narrow (assessment, grading, academic standing and appeals, curriculum content) [S19].

What Ontario's current model lacks is any matter on which the academic body's view is *binding*. It also lacks any statutory guarantee that its advice will be sought before specified decisions, as Quebec provides [S20]. On a fairness test, a system in which the people responsible for academic quality cannot decide even grading and appeals policy is hard to defend. That is especially so as colleges move into degrees, where the quality assessor itself expects academic freedom and faculty qualifications typical of universities [S29].

## 7. Original proposals

These proposals come from this study. They are not drawn from any cited source.

1. **Amend the regulation, not just the collective agreement.** Governance is a public matter, so it should be set in public law. A regulation under the Act and a revised binding directive should create an **Academic Council** at every college, replacing the advisory college council's academic functions. It should not be won or lost in bargaining.
2. **Composition.** Set a 20 to 30 voting-member body. Elected faculty would fill half the seats, including at least a quarter of those faculty seats for partial-load or sessional faculty. The rest would be students, academic administrators appointed by the president, and support staff. One board member would sit as a non-voting liaison, and the council would elect one member to attend board meetings. This would recreate the cross-membership that the Sheridan review found missing [S8].
3. **Listed decision powers (BC-style).** Give the council final authority over policies on assessment and grading, academic standing and progression, academic integrity procedures, academic appeals, and the course-level content of approved programs [S19]. The board would keep the budget, which programs exist, tuition and ancillary fees, and labour relations.
4. **Joint approval with a deadline.** For new credentials, major program changes and the academic plan, require both bodies to approve. If they disagree after 60 days, the matter goes to the Minister or an independent panel, with the council's reasons published [S19].
5. **Mandatory advice with reasons.** For program suspensions and closures, the board must ask the council for advice. If it rejects that advice, it must publish its reasons.
6. **Degree programs first.** If government prefers to phase this in, start by giving the council final authority over academic standards in degree programs, which PEQAB already assesses against university-type standards [S29].
7. **Measure performance.** Require an independent review of each council every five years. The review would use the criteria that Canadian senate researchers suggest: clear spheres of authority, attendance and participation, and the quality of oversight [S25].
8. **Keep bargaining separate.** Workload, staffing and pay would stay in bargaining. Members would declare an interest on items that directly affect their own employment.

## 8. Risks of the proposals

- **Slower change.** Joint approval with a 60-day limit could delay new programs by a term. The deadline is there to limit that risk.
- **Dominance by a few.** Like Sheridan's Senate, a council can be captured by a vocal few [S8]. Smaller size, staggered terms and published attendance help.
- **Two-tier faculty politics.** Without reserved seats, contract faculty may be outvoted by full-time colleagues on matters that affect them.
- **Legal friction.** Until the regulation and directives change, any college that gives a council exclusive powers repeats Sheridan's problem [S8]. Colleges should not act alone. The province should change the framework.
- **Symbolism without substance.** A council with powers on paper but no secretariat budget, data access or release time will fail. Funding the council's work is part of the reform.

## 9. Recommendation

Ontario's colleges would probably benefit from a **legislated academic council with defined decision powers over academic standards**, modelled on BC's education councils. A copy of the university senate would be neither lawful under the current framework nor clearly better on the evidence. Today's advisory councils give faculty and students a voice. They give them no authority, not even over grading and appeals, at a time when colleges are granting degrees and cutting programs under severe financial pressure. A hybrid body, with the board retaining money and program mix, answers the strongest objections about mission and accountability. The change should be made in public law, by regulation and binding directive, not left to individual colleges or to bargaining.

## 11. What this does not replace

This is an ethics case study based on public documents. It is not legal advice on the Act, the regulation or the collective agreement. It is not a governance review of any particular college, and it does not substitute for consultation with the faculty, students, staff, boards and Ministry who would live with any change. Anyone acting on these proposals should obtain a legal opinion and read the current statutes, regulations and directives, which may have changed since the status date.
