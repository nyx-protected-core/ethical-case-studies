# Living Wages in Global Supply Chains

Figures are quoted as each source gives them. Where I compare two figures myself, the text says "own arithmetic". Comparisons across different years, currencies or definitions are flagged.*

## 1. The question

Millions of people who sew clothes, pluck tea, grow cocoa and pack bananas for export earn less than it costs to live decently where they work. The minimum wage, where one exists, is often set well below that cost. This case asks three things:

- How is a "living wage" measured?
- Who is responsible for the gap: governments, suppliers, or the global buyers whose purchasing decisions set the economics of the supply chain?
- Which remedies have evidence behind them, and what do they cost?

## 2. How living-wage benchmarks are set

### 2.1 The Anker methodology and the Global Living Wage Coalition

The Anker methodology, set out in *Living Wages Around the World: Manual for Measurement* (Anker & Anker, 2017) [S1], is a way to estimate "a decent but basic standard of living in different countries and how much workers need to earn to afford this" [S1]. It is the shared basis used by the Global Living Wage Coalition (GLWC) [S2], and a 2025 review calls it the reference "for almost all" living-income and living-wage methodologies to date [S3].

The GLWC defines a living wage as remuneration for a standard work week "sufficient to afford a decent standard of living for the worker and her or his family", covering food, water, housing, education, health care, transport, clothing and other essentials, "including provision for unexpected events" [S2]. In outline [S2]:

| Step | What is done |
| --- | --- |
| Food | Cost of a low-cost nutritious diet meeting WHO recommendations, priced locally with worker input |
| Housing | Costed against UN-HABITAT and national decency standards, from visits to local housing |
| Other needs | Extrapolated from household expenditure data, then "post checked" for health care, education and transport |
| Margin | A small margin for unexpected events |
| Family and earners | Total cost for a typical family, divided by the typical number of full-time-equivalent earners |
| Checking pay | Overtime and non-guaranteed bonuses excluded; taxes considered; in-kind benefits counted only at a "fair and reasonable" value |

The benchmark also changes over time. For Dhaka the GLWC gross living wage was BDT 16,460 a month in March 2016, BDT 25,497 in 2023 [S8], and BDT 30,459 (about US$248) in its 2025 estimate, for a family of four with 1.58 workers [S9]. The 2023 and later values are inflation updates of the 2016 Anker study, not new field estimates of needs [S8][S9]. USD values in those updates are indicative only, because exchange rates are volatile [S8].

### 2.2 WageIndicator

The WageIndicator Foundation has run a living-wage methodology since 2013 and says it now operates in 208 countries [S5]. Its estimates use cost-of-living data from web surveys, price monitoring and fieldwork [S5]. Key choices [S4]:
- **"Typical family":** two adults, with the number of children set by the national fertility rate. One adult works full time; the second adult's hours follow national labour-force participation and part-time rates.
- **A range, not a point:** the lower bound uses 25th-percentile prices and the upper bound uses median prices.
- **Gaps:** WageIndicator reports that, for 2020 to 2022, gaps between statutory minimum wages and living wages ranged from 43% to 493%. By October 2025, the minimum wage exceeded the typical-family lower bound in 23 of the 182 countries where it collects living-wage data [S4].

### 2.3 Methodological debates

- **Which household?** Family size and the number of earners drive the result. The Anker method uses a typical local family and earner count [S2]; WageIndicator links both to national fertility and participation data [S4]. A KIT review finds methodologies "vary substantially" in their indicator definitions, how they compute components, cost and update time [S3].
- **In-kind benefits.** Counting housing, meals or firewood from plantations as part of pay can shrink measured gaps. The GLWC Kenya tea report says valuing in-kind benefits "is very controversial since workers and employers often disagree on their value", and that its own valuations were "approximate only" and "likely to be on the high side for some workers" [S15].
- **Gender and care work.** A 2022 critique argues that most methodologies and initiatives "fail to adequately embed gendered considerations", and that brands "evade responsibility through performative membership" in initiatives [S6].
- **Regional floor or local estimate?** A study of the Asia Floor Wage campaign in Indonesia and Cambodia found it "has failed to gain traction", because national economic, political and institutional contexts drive stakeholders' priorities [S7].
- **Normative judgement.** Every benchmark embeds choices about what "decent" means: diet standards, housing standards, schooling to what level. The Anker method uses explicit normative standards and local consultation [S2], [S15]. That makes it transparent, but its outputs still rest on those choices.

### 3.3 Cocoa: Living Income Reference Price and the Living Income Differential

Most cocoa farmers are smallholders, so the yardstick is a *living income*, not a wage.

- **Fairtrade Living Income Reference Price (LIRP).** From 1 October 2025 the LIRP is US$2.68 a kilo for Ghana (a 26% increase) and €2.65 (US$2.80) a kilo for Côte d'Ivoire (a 20% increase) [S18], [S19]. The price is built from the costs of decent living and sustainable production, farm size and a realistic target yield; the Ghana target yield was cut from 800 to 600 kg a hectare [S18], [S19].
- **Recent prices above the reference.** After the 2024 to 2025 cocoa price surge, Fairtrade notes that regulated farmgate prices exceed the revised LIRPs: Ghana raised its farmgate price from GHS 20.9 a kilo in 2023/24 to GHS 49.6, and Côte d'Ivoire from XOF 1,000 to XOF 1,800 a kilo [S18]. For the season starting 1 October 2024, no LIRP differential applied because farmgate prices were higher [S20]. Fairtrade warns "there is no certainty that these cocoa prices will hold" and argues for embedding the LIRP in long-term sourcing as a floor [S18].
- **Living Income Differential (LID).** Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire, which together supply about 60% of world cocoa, introduced a fixed LID of US$400 a tonne on all cocoa sold from the 2020/21 season [S21]. Its goal was a target floor price of US$2,600 a tonne, of which at least 70% (US$1,820) would go to farmers [S22]. Ghana's cocoa board later said that low world prices and buyers' "discounting of country origin differentials" had "reduced the inflows" and so farmer incomes [S22].

### 3.4 Bananas

- **IDH retailer data (UK).** Nine UK retailers committed in 2023 to a living wage in their banana supply chains by the end of 2027 [S23]. The average living-wage gap was 14.39% on 2024 wage data, against 14.78% for 2023 and 17.41% for 2022. About 70% of farms had a gap and about 30% of workers did [S23]. The data are self-reported, and IDH links part of the fall to advance severance payments by some Colombian producers [S23].
- **Fairtrade.** The Fairtrade Base Wage for banana companies is set at 70% of the Anker net living-wage benchmark, less an in-kind allowance [S24]. It is a floor on the way to a living wage, not a living wage itself. Fairtrade also publishes a Living Wage Reference Price and differential for bananas, with new values valid from 1 January 2027 [S25].

## 4. Buyer purchasing practices

The economic argument is that buyers set the price and the timing; suppliers absorb the shocks; wages are what gives.

- **Price and sourcing squeeze.** Anner's study of Bangladesh describes "a price squeeze and a sourcing squeeze in which lead firms pay increasing lower prices to suppliers while also imposing short lead times and high order volatility" [S26]. It used trade data and original surveys of factories and workers from 2016 and 2017 [S26].
- **ILO global supplier survey (2016 fieldwork).** Of 1,454 suppliers in 87 countries [S27]:
  - 39% had accepted orders priced below production costs; the share was 52% in textiles and clothing [S27];
  - the commonest reason for accepting such orders was to secure future orders [S27];
  - imposing prices below production costs was associated with a 16% increase in outsourced production [S27].
- **COVID-19 cancellations (Bangladesh, March 2020).** A survey of 316 suppliers found [S28]:
  - 45.8% said "a lot" to "most" of their in-process or finished orders were cancelled, and 5.9% had all of them cancelled;
  - when orders were cancelled, 72.1% of buyers refused to pay for raw materials already bought and 91.3% refused to pay cut-make-trim costs;
  - 98.1% of buyers refused to help with the legally required partial wages for furloughed workers;
  - 72.4% of furloughed workers were sent home without pay.
- **Better Buying Purchasing Practices Index 2025** (supplier ratings of their customers) [S29]:
  - 51% of suppliers reported high-pressure cost negotiations, up from 48.4% the year before;
  - orders priced to cover compliant production costs fell from 48.9% in 2024 to 43.2% in 2025;
  - only 43.6% of suppliers received payment terms of 60 days or less (the benchmark Better Buying recommends), and suppliers in China and South Asia more often reported terms of 91 days or more [S29].

Two caveats. The data are supplier-reported. And supplier pricing errors also play a part: 14% of suppliers in the ILO survey admitted that their own costing mistakes contributed to selling below cost [S27].

## 5. Binding approaches and their evidence

### 5.1 ACT in Cambodia (status as of 18 November 2026)

ACT (Action, Collaboration, Transformation) is an agreement between global brands and retailers and IndustriALL Global Union [S30]. Its Cambodia model has two parts [S31], [S32]:
- binding agreements between brands and IndustriALL, in which brands commit to support collectively bargained wages;
- a template collective bargaining agreement (CBA) between Cambodia's garment employers' association and IndustriALL-affiliated unions, which binds a factory once it is adopted there.

**Status.**
- On 17 June 2026 a renewed template CBA was launched. It runs from 1 July 2026 to 31 July 2029, raises the monthly base wage by US$6 until December 2027 and by a further US$9 from January 2028, and adds maternity and paternity leave [S31].
- The first two-year term ended in May 2026 and the programme was renewed for three years [S32].
- In June 2025, of 16 non-signatory brands surveyed by the Business & Human Rights Resource Centre, 13 replied; all said they had not committed to the agreement, and two said they were reviewing it [S33].

**Why the progress is contested.**
- The Clean Clothes Campaign credits the binding, enforceable sourcing-volume commitment and the labour-costing protocol [S32].
- It also reports, citing partners on the ground, that about 10 factories had signed a CBA, covering about 15,000 workers. It says the US$6 top-up "falls short of even compensating" for living-cost increases, so "real wages continue to decline" [S32].
- It says at least one of its member unions withdrew from the renewal talks, citing pressure on independent unions and the decision to hold the top-up at US$6 until 2028 [S32].
- The factory and worker counts come from an advocacy group's partners and were **not confirmed** by ACT or IndustriALL in the sources checked.

### 5.2 Worker-driven social responsibility: the Fair Food Program

The Fair Food Program (FFP) grew out of the Campaign for Fair Food that the Coalition of Immokalee Workers launched in 2001 [S34]. It presents itself as a model of worker-driven social responsibility [S35]. It combines a worker-written code of conduct, buyer commitments and market consequences: suspended farms cannot sell to participating buyers until they are re-audited [S34].
- **Premium.** Participating buyers pay a Fair Food Premium, the "penny per pound", passed to workers as a bonus in their pay. More than US$50 million has been paid since 2011 [S34].
- **Enforcement.** The program reports US$638,948 in recovered wages through its complaint process [S34].
- **Evidence caveat.** These are the program's own figures. No independent, peer-reviewed impact evaluation was verified for this study.

### 5.3 The Bangladesh Accord as an analogue

The Bangladesh Safety Agreement is a "legally binding agreement" between global brands and the unions UNI and IndustriALL [S36]. The Accord's Bangladesh page marks 13 years since the Rana Plaza collapse, which killed at least 1,138 people [S36]. As of 31 October 2026 it covers 248 brands and retailers, 1,772 factories and 2.8 million workers. It reports 77,665 inspections, 299,902 safety findings and an 81% average remediation progress rate [S36].

The lesson for wages is about design: enforceable brand commitments, public factory-level data and union co-governance. Safety is easier than wages, though. Safety has engineering standards, while wages sit in national bargaining and price competition.

### 5.4 Fairtrade minimum prices and premiums

- **Theory and review.** Fairtrade offers a minimum price and a premium. A review in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* sets out both the potential benefits and the potential pitfalls, and notes the debates over whether it makes "economic sense" [S37].
- **Farmer income.** A meta-analysis of 97 studies found that certified farmers receive 20% to 30% higher prices and 16% to 22% higher household incomes, with "substantial heterogeneity" [S38]. This covers sustainability standards broadly, not Fairtrade alone.
- **Hired labour.** Four years of fieldwork in Ethiopia and Uganda found "relatively poor payment and non-pay working conditions" in sites dominated by Fairtrade producer organisations, and concluded that Fairtrade "is not an effective way to improve the welfare of the poorest rural people" [S39].

The evidence is mixed: gains to certified farmers, and weak or negative findings for the workers those farmers hire.

### 5.5 Price floors and the cocoa LID

The LID is the boldest producer-country floor in the sector. Its record, from Ghana's own regulator, is that buyers could offset it by cutting other premiums [S22]. When world prices then rose sharply, farmgate prices overtook the living-income reference [S18], [S20]. A floor matters most when prices fall, and only if buyers cannot bargain it away elsewhere.

## 6. The ILO's 2024 agreement on living wages

- **Meeting of experts:** Geneva, 19 to 23 February 2024 [S41].
- **Definition agreed by the experts.** The ILO "denotes that the concept of the living wage is" [S43]:
  - "the wage level that is necessary to afford a decent standard of living for workers and their families, taking into account the country circumstances and calculated for the work performed during the normal hours of work";
  - "calculated in accordance with the ILO's principles of estimating the living wage";
  - "to be achieved through the wage-setting process in line with ILO principles on wage setting".

  "Normal hours" excludes overtime [S43].
- **Operationalisation.** The conclusions include "promoting incremental progression from minimum wages to living wages", through statutory minimum wages or collective bargaining. They state that "the needs of workers and their families and economic factors are the two pillars of wage-setting processes" [S43]. They also list informality and the unfair distribution of value among the root causes of low pay [S43].
- **Governing Body, 13 March 2024. The wording matters here.** The formal decision says the Governing Body "took note of the conclusions" and authorised the Director-General "to publish and disseminate them" [S40]. ILO News described the agreement as "endorsed by the ILO's Governing Body" [S42]. Describe it accurately: the experts agreed the conclusions; the Governing Body took note of them and authorised follow-up.
- **Follow-up.** The decision asked for an implementation report to the 355th Session (October to November 2025) [S40]. Whether that report was presented, and what it said, was **not verified** for this study.

## 7. Counterarguments and the evidence on both sides

| Concern | Evidence that supports the concern | Evidence that tempers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Job losses** | A survey of developing-country studies finds effects "more consistently negative" where studies focus on vulnerable workers, use formal-sector data, cover strongly enforced laws, and where minimum wages bind [S45], [S46] | The authors note that one reading of the literature is "simply no clear evidence of disemployment effects", though they find the variation systematic [S46]. In Indonesia, anti-sweatshop campaigns raised real wages substantially at targeted firms, with "some costs" in investment, profits and closures of smaller plants, but no significant employment effect was found [S47] |
| **Informality** | Higher formal wages may push work into informal subcontracting; the ILO survey linked below-cost prices to more outsourcing [S27] | The ILO treats informality as a root cause to address, by extending minimum-wage coverage to all workers [S43], [S44] |
| **Buyers relocating** | Brands can shift orders to cheaper countries. No figure on wage-driven relocation was verified here | ACT's binding sourcing-volume commitment is designed to stop that [S32]; country-level benchmarks show gaps almost everywhere [S4], [S14] |
| **Automation** | An ILO study estimated that 64% (Indonesia), 86% (Viet Nam) and 88% (Cambodia) of textile, clothing and footwear wage workers are at high risk of automation [S48] | The same study judged sewbots "unlikely to displace current workers in ASEAN garment factories", and more likely to be deployed in destination markets [S48]. The risk is reshoring, not immediate replacement |
| **Who bears the cost?** | Hall and Suh find that most output from low-income countries with large living-wage gaps is consumed domestically, so closing the gap would raise domestic prices of goods such as food and textiles [S50] | Closing the gap for a pair of jeans would raise Indian cotton and textile wages by 137% and 52% but the Western retail price by only 8% [S50]. Earlier work finds the price rises needed to absorb large garment wage increases are small [S49], and that higher unit labour cost in FOB prices "would not impact in any serious way" on brand margins [S51] |

**Reading the evidence fairly.** Export-sector wage increases paid for through buyer prices are cheap relative to retail prices. That is the strongest argument for buyer responsibility. Economy-wide minimum-wage hikes in poor countries are riskier for formal jobs, especially where they bind and are enforced. The policy question is therefore not "living wage: yes or no", but who pays, through which channel, and at what pace. That is close to the ILO's "incremental progression" [S43].

## 8. Trade-offs

| Option | Benefits | Risks and costs | Evidence strength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Raise statutory minimum wages towards living-wage benchmarks | Universal coverage, democratic legitimacy | Job losses where binding and enforced [S45]; informality | Mixed |
| Brand-funded top-ups through sectoral collective bargaining (ACT) | Shares cost with buyers; binding; volume commitments | Small sums so far; union-freedom concerns; few signatories [S32], [S33] | Early, contested |
| Worker-driven social responsibility (FFP) | Worker enforcement; premiums paid [S34] | Hard to scale beyond concentrated buyers; self-reported data | Promising, mostly self-reported |
| Certification price floors and premiums (Fairtrade) | Higher farmer prices and incomes on average [S38] | Weak results for hired workers [S39]; small market share [S37] | Mixed |
| Producer-country price floors (cocoa LID) | Sovereign; covers all exports | Buyers offset through other premiums [S22]; supply response | Mixed |
| Responsible purchasing (pricing, payment terms, no unpaid cancellations) | Targets the root cause [S26], [S27] | Voluntary; performance is falling on some measures [S29] | Strong on the problem, weak on the remedies |
| Transparency and open wage data | Lets workers, unions and buyers see gaps | Disclosure without action; data quality | Low cost, indirect effect |

## 9. Recommendations

1. **Use one credible benchmark per location and publish it.** Prefer transparent, locally validated estimates that meet the ILO's estimation principles [S43]. Disclose the household assumptions and how in-kind benefits are valued [S15].
2. **Price labour in.** Buyers should ring-fence the labour cost in FOB prices, as ACT's costing protocol aims to do [S32], and track the share of orders priced to cover compliant production, which fell to 43.2% in 2025 [S29].
3. **Fix payment terms and cancellations in contracts.** Commit to paying for goods produced or in production, and to payment terms of 60 days or less [S28], [S29].
4. **Back binding, union-led bargaining.** Brand support for sectoral CBAs should come with commitments on volume and freedom of association. Gains in real wages, not just nominal top-ups, should be the test [S31], [S32].
5. **Progress steadily, not in jumps.** Governments should move statutory minimums towards living-wage benchmarks on a published path, with monitoring of employment effects [S43], [S45].
6. **Make floors stick.** For commodities, embed reference prices in long-term contracts so that floors hold when world prices fall, and stop offsetting them through other price components [S18], [S22].
7. **Fund independent evaluation** of ACT, the FFP and buyer living-wage commitments. Much of the current evidence is self-reported.

### A modest web and technology angle

- **Open facility data.** Open Supply Hub lists 2,544,468 production locations, contributed by 5,274 organisations, across 225 countries and territories [S52]. Linking wage benchmarks and audit results to open facility identifiers would let anyone match a factory to its local living-wage gap.
- **Open wage data.** The Anker Research Institute publishes a public table of benchmarks [S14], and WageIndicator publishes living-wage ranges [S4]. Publishing them in machine-readable, versioned formats with stated years, currencies and household assumptions would prevent the cross-year comparisons flagged in section 3.
- **Disclosure gap.** In the 2023 Fashion Transparency Index, only 1% of 250 large brands disclosed the percentage of their supply-chain workers who earn a living wage [S53].
- **Limits.** Data portals are not remedies. They help only when unions, regulators and buyers act on them, and when workers can see and contest them safely.

## 10. Limits of this study

- **Bangladesh minimum wage:** comes from a news report of the gazette [S11]; the official gazette index [S12] confirms the gazette's date and title but not the figure. Later annual increments were not checked.
- **India tea:** gap not quantified, because no Anker benchmark was verified for Assam or West Bengal.
- **Fair Food Program:** figures are self-reported [S34].
- **ACT Cambodia:** coverage counts come from an advocacy group's partners [S32].
- **ILO follow-up:** the implementation report to the 355th Session was not checked.
- **Mismatched years:** many figures compare different years; the text flags this, but readers should avoid computing precise gaps from them.
- **Automation:** the risk estimates are a model of exposure, not a forecast of job losses [S48].
