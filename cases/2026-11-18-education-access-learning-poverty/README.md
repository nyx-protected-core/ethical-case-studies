# In school but not learning: out-of-school children, learning poverty and what actually moves both

## The ethical question

Almost every government has signed up to the right to free primary education, and enrolment has grown enormously since 2000. Yet one child in six of school age is still outside school, and in low- and middle-income countries most of the children who are in school reach age ten unable to read a simple text. Two failures sit side by side: an access failure, concentrated in the poorest and most conflict-affected places, and a learning failure, spread across systems that look successful on enrolment statistics.

The ethical question is how scarce public and international money should be divided between getting the last children into school and making sure the children already there actually learn, who should pay, and how the countries that have made the fastest progress, many of them in the Global South, can lead the shared effort rather than receive instructions about it.

## Scale

**Out of school.** The UNESCO Institute for Statistics (UIS) and the Global Education Monitoring (GEM) Report estimate that 273 million children, adolescents and youth were out of school in 2024: 79 million of primary age, 64 million of lower secondary age and 130 million of upper secondary age [1][2]. The total comprises 140 million boys and 133 million girls [1]. The out-of-school population fell by 33% between 2000 and 2015 but has risen for seven years in a row since, up 3% on 2015; the out-of-school rate has stayed at about 17% because school-age populations keep growing [1][2]. Almost all of the rise since 2015 is in low-income countries, where the out-of-school population has grown by 29% [2]. The model almost certainly undercounts in conflict settings: humanitarian data for the ten most affected countries suggest at least 13 million more, which would raise the total to about 286 million [1][3].

**Completion.** Globally, 88% of each cohort completed primary school on time in 2024, 78% lower secondary and 61% upper secondary; in low-income countries the on-time figures were 60%, 37% and 20% [1].

**Learning poverty.** The World Bank defines learning poverty as the share of 10-year-olds who cannot read and understand a short, age-appropriate text, adjusted for children who are out of school [5]. The most recent global estimate is still the 2022 update by the World Bank, UNESCO, UNICEF and partners: about 70% of 10-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries, up from 57% before the COVID-19 school closures, with about 89% in Sub-Saharan Africa, 78% in South Asia and 80% in Latin America and the Caribbean (regional figures are partly simulated) [4]. The country database was updated in April 2024 to cover 125 countries, but as of the status date no newer global aggregate has replaced the 70% figure; the World Bank's 2025 foundational learning brief still cites it [5][6]. This should be read as an estimate with large modelling uncertainty, not a measured census.

**Conflict and crisis.** Education Cannot Wait (ECW) estimates that 234 million school-age children in 60 crisis-affected countries need support to access quality education, of whom about 85 million are completely out of school (52% girls); five crises (Sudan, Afghanistan, Ethiopia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Pakistan) account for nearly half of those 85 million [7]. ECW itself describes these as indicative estimates [7].

**Girls.** Globally the out-of-school total is now slightly larger for boys than girls [1], which is a real achievement and a warning against treating "girls' education" as the whole equity story. But exclusion of girls remains extreme in specific places. In Afghanistan, UNESCO reported in August 2024 that at least 1.4 million girls had been deliberately denied secondary education since the 2021 bans, the only country where secondary and higher education is forbidden to girls and women [8].

**The financing gap.** The GEM Report calculated in 2023 that low- and lower-middle-income countries face an average gap of about US$97 billion a year from 2023 to 2030 to reach the national education targets they set for themselves, about US$70-77 billion of it in Africa depending on regional definition [9][10]. Households already carry a large share: in the median country they pay about a quarter of the cost of education, and in lower-middle-income countries about 44%, against 20% in high-income countries [9]. Donor money is about 0.3% of global education spending but up to 17% of public education spending in low-income countries [9][11]. The share of total development aid going to education fell from 9.3% in 2019 to 7.6% in 2022 [12], and the GEM Report projects aid to education may fall by about a quarter between 2023 and 2027 [11].

## Root causes

**Schooling is not learning.** The World Development Report 2018 named the "learning crisis": enrolment rose fast, but many children, especially from poor households, leave school without foundational skills [13]. A spending study of low- and lower-middle-income countries found that primary spending per student rose by about 80% in real terms between 2000 and 2015 with no corresponding change in average learning [14]. Money spent on the same things, in the same way, did not buy learning.

**Curricula that outrun children.** In many systems the official syllabus moves at a pace most children cannot follow, so teachers teach to the top of the class while those who fall behind early never catch up. Randomized evidence from India and Kenya shows that reorganising teaching around what children actually know produces large gains, which implies that the mismatch itself was a cause of low learning [15][16][17][18]. Reviews of the wider literature consistently favour teaching matched to pupils' actual level [43].

**Teacher time, knowledge and support.** Unannounced visits and teacher tests in seven Sub-Saharan African countries found large shortfalls in time spent teaching, in teachers' own subject knowledge and in pedagogical skill [19]. This is mostly a system design failure (training, support, deployment and accountability), not an individual moral failing.

**Fees and hidden costs.** Even where tuition is formally free, uniforms, exam fees, informal "contributions", transport and lost household income keep the poorest children out. The large household share of education spending above [9] is the aggregate sign of this.

**Distance, safety and crisis.** Distance to school and the safety of the journey matter especially for girls in rural areas [20][21]. Conflict destroys schools, displaces teachers and breaks data systems, which is why out-of-school numbers in crisis countries are the least reliable [2][7].

**Fiscal squeeze.** Debt service and falling aid compete with education budgets; the 2024 Education Finance Watch notes that some low- and lower-middle-income countries spend nearly as much per person on debt service as on education [12].

## What has worked

**Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL).** Developed by the Indian organisation Pratham, TaRL groups children by learning level rather than grade for part of the day and uses simple assessment and activities aimed at basic reading and arithmetic. An early randomized evaluation of a Pratham remedial programme in urban India raised average test scores by 0.28 standard deviations, with the largest gains for the lowest-scoring children [15]. Over a decade of further trials, Banerjee, Duflo and colleagues showed that some government-school versions failed and others succeeded, and documented how the design was adapted until it worked inside regular government schools [16]. A tracking experiment in 121 Kenyan schools found that grouping pupils by level helped lower-achieving pupils because teachers could teach to their level [17], and personalised adaptive software in urban India produced gains of 0.37 SD in mathematics and 0.23 SD in Hindi in 4.5 months [18].

The scale evidence is more sobering. Zambia's government-run version, Catch Up, now covers all provinces and more than a million pupils a year; a randomized evaluation found gains of 0.10 SD in literacy and 0.15 SD in numeracy after two years, at about US$4.82 per pupil per year, held back by the fact that only about a third of pupils attended a remedial class on a given day [22]. In Côte d'Ivoire a teacher-delivered trial found modest gains in learning levels but no significant effect on functional literacy and numeracy [23]. J-PAL notes that recent syntheses find TaRL weaker and less consistent when rolled out by government teachers than when run by NGOs or volunteers **(attributed)** [22]. The honest reading: the idea works, but delivery quality and attendance decide how much.

**Structured pedagogy.** Structured pedagogy combines scripted or semi-scripted lesson plans, matched pupil books and ongoing coaching. In Kenya's PRIMR trial, the combination of teacher training, coaching, one book per pupil and structured lesson plans was the most effective of the tested packages for early literacy and numeracy [24]. Kenya then scaled the approach nationally as Tusome; a study of the scale-up found that the government set clear national reading benchmarks, delivered core inputs fairly consistently across the country, and built simple but functional feedback and accountability loops [25]. A review of 145 recent African studies finds promising impacts from structured pedagogy and instruction in the home language [26].

**The 2023 "Smart Buys".** The Global Education Evidence Advisory Panel (GEEAP), convened by the World Bank, FCDO, UNICEF and USAID, rated three approaches as "Great Buys": giving households information on the benefits, costs and quality of education; structured pedagogy; and targeting instruction by learning level rather than grade [27]. "Good Buys" include quality pre-primary education, reducing travel time to school, merit-based scholarships for disadvantaged children and school-based deworming where worm loads are high [27]. Cash transfers and school feeding were rated effective but relatively expensive as a tool for learning; "Bad Buys" include hardware such as laptops and tablets alone, and extra inputs (textbooks, smaller classes, buildings, grants) alone when other problems are not addressed [27]. A benefit-cost analysis estimates that structured pedagogy and TaRL delivered to 90% of pupils in low- and lower-middle-income countries would cost about US$18 per pupil per year and return about US$65 per dollar spent [14]. These are model-based estimates and depend on scale-up quality.

**Sobral and Ceará, Brazil.** Ceará, one of Brazil's poorer states, had the largest increase in the national education quality index (IDEB) in primary and lower secondary education after 2005, and in 2017 ten of its municipalities were in Brazil's top 20, with Sobral first [28]. The state's 2007 reforms combined a literacy programme (PAIC) aimed at universal literacy by the end of grade 2, with materials, training, assessments and technical support to municipalities, and a results-based rule under which 18% of a municipality's share of state consumption tax (ICMS) depends on education outcomes, with the money not tied to any sector [28]. In June 2023 Brazil's federal government launched a national literacy commitment (Compromisso Nacional Criança Alfabetizada) modelled on Ceará's approach [29]. The Ceará evidence is mostly descriptive rather than randomized, and should be read that way.

**Vietnam.** Despite being the poorest or second-poorest participant, Vietnam outperformed all other developing countries and many richer countries on PISA in 2012, 2015 and 2018 [30]. After correcting for sample problems, including its large out-of-school population at age 15, Vietnam remains a large positive outlier, and measured child, household and school variables explain at most about 30% of the advantage [30]. The GEM Report also lists Vietnam among the countries that cut adolescent out-of-school rates by at least 80% since 2000 [1]. The lesson is humility: the success is real but not yet well explained, so it cannot be copied as a recipe.

**Abolishing fees.** Uganda's 1997 Universal Primary Education reform sharply increased attendance and narrowed gaps by income, gender and region, but the enrolment surge strained quality [31]; it also increased on-time school entry, especially for girls and rural children [32]. Malawi's 1994 abolition raised enrolment dramatically, with the largest gains for the poor, while informal "contributions" persisted and quality became the next problem [33]. Kenya's 2003 free primary education raised primary completion and access for disadvantaged pupils without substantially lowering scores of those who would have attended anyway [34]. In Ghana, randomized secondary scholarships raised educational attainment, knowledge and skills over twelve years of follow-up [35]. The African review also finds long-term benefits from eliminating primary fees and positive effects of eliminating secondary fees [26].

**Cash transfers.** Mexico's Progresa raised enrolment through grants conditional on school attendance [36]. A systematic review of 35 studies found that both conditional and unconditional transfers raise enrolment and attendance, with the largest effects where conditions are explicit, monitored and enforced, but effects on test scores are small at best [37]. A Malawi experiment for adolescent girls found the conditional arm cut dropout more and improved reading comprehension, while the unconditional arm protected girls who dropped out from early marriage [38]. That trade-off between conditionality and protection is itself an ethical choice.

**Reaching girls and remote children.** Building village-based schools in rural Afghanistan raised girls' enrolment by 52 percentage points and closed the enrolment gender gap [21]. In Bihar, India, giving girls bicycles to reach secondary school raised their age-appropriate enrolment by 32% and was more cost-effective than comparable conditional cash transfers in South Asia [20]. Both fit the Smart Buys finding that reducing travel time is a good buy [27].

**Pooled finance.** ECW, created at the 2016 World Humanitarian Summit and hosted by UNICEF, is the global fund dedicated to education in emergencies and protracted crises [39]. The Global Partnership for Education (GPE) is the largest global fund dedicated to education in lower-income countries, working through country-owned reform plans and mobilisation of domestic as well as external money [40]. The International Finance Facility for Education (IFFEd) uses donor guarantees and grants to expand lending by multilateral development banks to lower-middle-income countries; its first partnership with the Asian Development Bank (2024) aims to unlock at least US$500 million [41], and its first co-financed programme, a US$182 million loan for public schools in Karnataka, India, was announced in July 2026 [42]. Its record is too short to evaluate.

## Ethical tensions

**Access versus learning.** Every dollar spent getting the last 5% of children into school could alternatively raise learning for the 70% already in school but not learning. Fee abolition shows that access drives can overwhelm quality [31][33]. But "learning first" can quietly abandon the hardest-to-reach children, who are disproportionately poor, disabled, displaced or in conflict zones [7]. A just policy needs both, sequenced honestly.

**Fees versus quality.** Fees and informal charges exclude the poor; removing them without replacing the revenue can starve schools. Fee abolition is right in principle, but only with funded replacement.

**Conditions on cash.** Conditional transfers raise attendance more, but conditions penalise exactly the households least able to comply, and in Malawi the unconditional arm protected girls who left school [37][38].

**Scripts and teacher autonomy.** Structured pedagogy works partly because it limits teacher discretion. That can feel like deskilling. The evidence supports structure as a floor for foundational skills, not as a ceiling for the whole curriculum.

**Measurement and narrowing.** Learning poverty measures reading only, and Zambia's trial showed gains concentrated in exactly the skills the programme targets in the short run [22]. Systems that chase a single indicator can narrow education.

**Who leads.** The best-documented successes in this case (Pratham's TaRL, Kenya's Tusome, Ceará, Vietnam, Zambia's Catch Up) come from the Global South. Yet much of the money and evaluation capacity still sits in high-income donors and northern universities, and ten donors account for over three-quarters of education aid [11]. Cooperation that treats southern governments as implementers of northern designs is both less respectful and, on this evidence, less effective.

**Engagement with restrictive governments.** In Afghanistan, funding community-based education helps girls now but risks normalising a ban; refusing to fund harms the same girls. There is no clean answer.

## Original proposals

All proposals in this section are **(original proposal)**: this study's own ideas, offered for debate and not tested.

1. **A South-South foundational learning compact.** Middle powers and regional bodies with proven systems (for example Brazil's federal literacy commitment, India's TaRL ecosystem, Kenya's Tusome team, Zambia's Ministry of Education, Vietnam's education ministry) would host peer exchanges in which ministry staff, not consultants, spend time inside each other's systems. Funding would come from GPE, regional development banks and IFFEd-backed loans, with African Union and ASEAN education bodies setting the agenda alongside UNESCO.

2. **Ceará-style results transfers inside countries.** National governments that redistribute revenue to sub-national governments would tie a modest share of that transfer to measured foundational learning and to out-of-school reduction together, so no municipality is rewarded for pushing weak pupils out to raise averages. Ceará shows the mechanism is fiscally feasible [28]; including the out-of-school measure is this study's addition.

3. **"Zero hidden cost" guarantee for primary school.** Governments would publish a list of every charge a household can face (uniforms, exams, contributions, transport) and replace each with a capitation grant to schools, with a public hotline for reporting illegal charges. This builds on the Malawi finding that informal contributions persisted after fee abolition [33].

4. **Attendance first inside learning programmes.** Zambia's evidence suggests attendance, not pedagogy, is the binding limit on TaRL at scale [22]. Pair remedial programmes with low-cost attendance measures already rated as good buys, such as reducing travel time [20][27], and report remedial-class attendance as a core indicator.

5. **A counter-cyclical education guarantee.** When a country enters debt restructuring, creditors (including bilateral lenders such as China, Gulf states and middle-income lenders, as well as Paris Club members and multilaterals) would agree that primary education budgets are protected at a floor, consistent with the Transforming Education Summit call not to require cuts to education as a condition of new financing [12][44].

6. **Crisis education that starts on day one.** ECW and humanitarian appeals would budget foundational learning (structured pedagogy and level-based teaching in community spaces) from the first month of a displacement, rather than treating schooling as a later development phase.

## Risks

- Scale-up decay: interventions that work in trials often lose force in government systems [16][22][23].
- Gaming results-based finance: excluding weaker pupils from tests, or teaching narrowly to the measure [22].
- Fee abolition without replacement funding may leave schools overcrowded and under-resourced [31][33].
- New lending through IFFEd-type facilities adds to debt in already-stressed countries [12][41].
- Aid cuts may undermine pooled funds just as evidence about what to fund has improved [11].
- Data gaps, worst in the countries with most children out of school, make targets unreliable [2].
- Copying a famous model (Sobral, Vietnam) without the local politics and institutions that made it work.

## Recommendation

Make foundational learning and access a single goal, not two competing ones. Concretely:

1. Abolish primary fees and the hidden charges behind them, with funded replacement grants to schools [26][31][33][34].
2. Put most new learning money into the Great Buys: structured pedagogy, teaching at the right level, and accurate information for households about school quality and returns [14][27]. Fund attendance alongside them [22].
3. Spend less on stand-alone hardware and inputs that do not change what happens in the classroom [27].
4. Use targeted transfers, bicycles and nearby schools where the barrier is cost or distance, especially for girls and rural children [20][21][37].
5. Protect education budgets in debt restructuring and move towards the 4-6% of GDP and 15-20% of public spending benchmarks set out in the Incheon and Paris declarations [44], recognising that in poor countries these shares may still not be enough in absolute terms [12].
6. Keep ECW and GPE funded, and let IFFEd prove its model before scaling it [39][40][41][42].
7. Let the Global South lead: the countries that achieved the gains should shape the agenda, with northern donors as financiers and partners, not designers.
