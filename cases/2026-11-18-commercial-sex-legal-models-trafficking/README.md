# Legal models for commercial sex and their relationship to human trafficking: what the evidence shows and what it cannot settle

*Ethics case-study source brief 11. Anonymous author. Prepared November 2026. Policy level only: this brief deals with law, data, evaluations and published research, not individual cases. Every factual claim is tied to a numbered source in the list at the end. Items marked **UNVERIFIED** could not be confirmed against a text read live for this brief. The brief does not recommend a model.*

**Source labels used throughout:**
- **[PR]** peer-reviewed research
- **[GOV]** government evaluation or government-commissioned review
- **[NGO]** NGO or advocacy policy or report (not peer-reviewed)
- **[TB]** UN treaty-body document
- **[INT]** intergovernmental data report

---

## Read this first: why causal claims in any direction are weak

1. **Trafficking is not directly counted.** The main cross-national dataset used in this literature is the UNODC 2006 *Trafficking in Persons: Global Patterns* citation index. UNODC itself states that the report "does not provide information regarding actual numbers of victims, nor does it provide statistics directly reflecting the severity of human trafficking" [24]. The index measures how often a country is *reported* as a destination [1][24].
2. **Reporting depends on enforcement.** A country that legalises, regulates or polices more intensively may *detect and report* more trafficking. The authors of the best-known study acknowledge this, and also that their source institutions are skewed towards Western Europe and North America [1].
3. **Most comparisons are cross-sectional or before/after with no control group.** Country fixed effects cannot be estimated with a single cross-section [1]. National evaluations rely on police and social-service estimates of hidden populations [6][8][9][23].
4. **"Model" labels hide large differences** in enforcement, migration law and brothel rules. A law on paper is a weak proxy for what police actually do [14][22].
5. **Two different questions are often merged:**
   - (a) whether a model changes the *volume of trafficking* into a country;
   - (b) whether it changes the *safety, police contact and reporting* of people who sell sex, including victims.
   The evidence base for (b) is much larger than for (a).

## 1. Full criminalisation (sale and purchase both illegal; e.g. most US states)

**Strongest evidence-based case.**
- Two cross-national studies find reported trafficking inflows lowest where prostitution is illegal [1][2].
  - Jakobsson and Kotsadam report trafficking for sexual exploitation is "least prevalent in countries where prostitution is illegal, most prevalent in countries where prostitution is legalized, and in between" where selling is legal but procuring is illegal [2] [PR].
  - The market-size ("scale effect") logic in [1] implies that shrinking the legal market should shrink trafficking demand [PR].
- The one quasi-experiment on a change in criminal law found that ending criminalisation enlarged the market. When a Rhode Island court ruling unexpectedly decriminalised indoor sex work (2003–2009), "decriminalization increases the size of the indoor sex market" [16] [PR]. Proponents can argue that a larger market is a larger potential market for traffickers.

**Strongest critique.**
- The same Rhode Island study found that reported rape offences fell about 30% and female gonorrhoea incidence fell more than 40% during decriminalisation [16] [PR, quasi-experimental].
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 40 quantitative and 94 qualitative studies found that repressive policing of sex workers was associated with:
  - higher odds of sexual or physical violence (OR 2.99, 95% CI 1.96–4.57);
  - HIV/STI (OR 1.87);
  - condomless sex (OR 1.42).
  It also found that threats of arrest displaced sex workers to isolated locations and deterred reporting [14] [PR, SR; all included studies observational].
- A Lancet review plus transmission model estimated that decriminalisation "would have the greatest effect on the course of HIV epidemics across all settings, averting 33–46% of HIV infections in the next decade" [15] [PR, modelled].
- The cross-national "illegal = least trafficking" results rest on the reporting index that UNODC warns is not a victim count [24]. Criminalised settings may simply detect and report less.

## 2. The Nordic / "equality" model (purchase criminalised, sale not; Sweden 1999, Norway 2009, Iceland, France 2016, Ireland 2017, Canada 2014)

**Strongest evidence-based case.**
- **Sweden's official evaluation (SOU 2010:49) [6] [GOV]** concluded:
  - street prostitution "has been halved". Social services knew of about 730 women in street prostitution in 1998 and 300–430 a year afterwards;
  - this is "a direct result of the criminalisation". In 2008 street prostitution in Norway and Denmark was estimated at three times Sweden's level;
  - according to the National Criminal Police, the ban "acts as a barrier to human traffickers and procurers";
  - the feared side effects (displacement underground, more abuse) "have not been realised".
- **Norway's commissioned evaluation (Vista Analyse 2014) [8] [GOV]** concluded:
  - Oslo's street market stabilised at 40–65% of its pre-law size, with an overall market reduction of roughly 10–20%;
  - combined with anti-pimping and anti-trafficking laws, the ban makes Norway "a less attractive country for prostitution based trafficking";
  - it found no evidence of more violence after the ban.
- **Theory [5] [PR]:** a formal economic model notes that coerced supply does not respond to penalties on sellers, but all demand is voluntary. So "criminal punishment of prostitutes always weakly increases trafficking". "Sufficient punishment of johns (the Swedish model) … can deter all demand … and thus eliminate trafficking", but only by also eliminating voluntary supply.
- **The CEDAW Committee [13] [TB]:**
  - states that "sexual exploitation persists due to States parties' failure to effectively discourage the demand that fosters exploitation and leads to trafficking";
  - recommends states "discourage the demand that fosters exploitation of prostitution and leads to human trafficking", citing the obligation in Article 9(5) of the Palermo Protocol.
  - The recommendation does not name a legal model in the passages reviewed. Reading it as an endorsement of the Nordic model is an interpretation, not its text.
- **Coalition positions [12] [NGO]:** a 2015 open letter coordinated by the Coalition Against Trafficking in Women (CATW) agrees that people who sell sex "must not be criminalized in any jurisdiction". It argues that full decriminalisation "in effect legalizes pimping, brothel owning and sex buying" and that Germany's regulated market "triggered an increase in sex trafficking".
- **SPACE International [27] [NGO, survivor-led]** is a group of people who describe themselves as sex-trade survivors. In a 2015 public statement it writes: "We support and endorse the Swedish Model, which decriminalises only the exploited person in a prostitution exchange." It distinguishes that from "the complete and total decriminalisation of all parties in the sex trade, including those who exploit for sexual gain (punters/johns) and those who exploit for financial gain (pimps/traffickers)" [27]. This is a lived-experience advocacy position, not an evaluation of trafficking volumes.
- **The European Parliament [28] [GOV, non-binding].** On 14 September 2023 the Parliament adopted a non-binding resolution on the regulation of prostitution in the EU (P9_TA(2023)0328, report A9-0240/2023). Contemporary reporting, including an official Parliament press release and a roll-call compilation, gives the vote as **234 in favour, 175 against and 122 abstentions**. The official adopted text and the official roll-call were live-blocked (HTTP 403) when re-checked on 18 November 2026, so the vote figures and the exact operative wording are **UNVERIFIED** against the official record. Secondary accounts describe the text as calling for measures that reduce demand and for those who sell sex not to be criminalised, and some describe it as supporting punishment or criminalisation of purchase. The narrow and divided vote is itself evidence that the question is not settled in European institutions.

**Strongest critique.**
- **Evaluation design.**
  - The Swedish evaluation itself says evaluating the ban "has proven to be a difficult task" and that knowledge of indoor and internet prostitution is "limited" [6].
  - Its trafficking claim rests on police judgement, not measured volumes [6].
  - The Norwegian evaluation notes "large uncertainty" in market estimates and no national estimates after 2010 [8].
  - Peer-reviewed critics argue there are "no reliable data demonstrating any overall decline in people selling sex" in Sweden and that the law increased dangers in some forms of sex work [7] [PR, partly drawing on the authors' own fieldwork].
- **Safety and reporting under client criminalisation:**
  - **Canada (Vancouver):** a qualitative study found that targeting clients meant sex workers "had to rush screening clients and were displaced to outlying areas with increased risks of violence" [17] [PR, n=31].
  - **Canada (five cities, 2014 law):** 31% of 200 sex workers said they could not call 911 in an emergency for fear of police detection. Only 16.5% of those who experienced violence or confinement reported it to police [18] [PR, cross-sectional].
  - **Ireland:** analysis of reports to a sex-worker safety service (Ugly Mugs) found crimes, including violent offences, increased after the 2017 Act, with continued low reporting to police [19] [PR; data are self-reports to a service, so trends may partly reflect reporting changes].
  - **Ireland, government review:** the Department of Justice's review (2025) found "demand has not decreased". From January 2017 to August 2024 there were 161 directed prosecutions and 15 convictions. The prevalence of trafficking among people selling sex "is not fully known", and both supporters and opponents cite the lack of reliable data [20] [GOV].
  - **France:** a survey of 583 sex workers after the 2016 law found 62.9% reported a worse quality of life and 78.2% lower earnings. Interviewees described clients imposing more conditions [21] [NGO-commissioned academic report, not peer-reviewed].
- The meta-analysis explicitly includes "laws and enforcement targeting the sale and purchase of sex" among the criminalisation harms it identifies [14].

## 3. Decriminalisation (criminal law removed from consensual adult sex work; New Zealand 2003)

**Strongest evidence-based case.**
- **New Zealand's statutory review (2008) [9] [GOV]** found:
  - "the number of sex workers in New Zealand has not increased as a result of the passage of the PRA" (based on a 2007 five-centre estimate of 2,332, and comparable Christchurch counts in 1999 and 2006);
  - "there is no link between the sex industry and human trafficking" in New Zealand;
  - immigration authorities identified no trafficking situations in the sex industry, and there were no prosecutions under the trafficking provision.
  - Of sex workers able to comment, the majority felt they were "now more likely to report incidents of violence to the Police".
- A peer-reviewed reflection by a lead researcher in the review process concludes that the research "has shown that decriminalization has been successful in making the industry safer" [10] [PR; author was involved in the evaluation research].
- **Amnesty International (2016) [11] [NGO policy]** calls for "the decriminalization of all aspects of adult consensual sex work". It pairs this with criminal law against "forced labour, human trafficking, abuse and violence", arguing that criminalisation means crimes against sex workers "often go unreported, under-investigated and/or unpunished".
- **WHO (2022) [25] [INT, public-health guideline]** is the main intergovernmental public-health position. The *Consolidated guidelines on HIV, viral hepatitis and STI prevention, diagnosis, treatment and care for key populations* treat removal of punitive laws as an enabling intervention. The accompanying sex-worker policy brief, presenting those guidelines, calls for "decriminalizing sex work (namely, removing all offences that criminalize sex workers, clients and third parties)" [25]. The limit of this source is important: it is framed around HIV, viral hepatitis and STI outcomes and does not assess effects on trafficking.
- **The Global Network of Sex Work Projects (NSWP) [26] [NGO, sex-worker-led]** states, in its own policy brief: "The global sex workers' rights movement calls for the full decriminalisation of adult sex work, including the decriminalisation of third parties." It argues that continuing to criminalise third parties "has a direct and harmful impact on sex workers themselves" [26]. This is an advocacy position, not a trafficking-volume study.
- The systematic review reports that "evidence from decriminalised settings suggests that sex workers in these settings have greater negotiating power with clients and better access to justice" [14] [PR]. The Lancet model gives the largest HIV benefit to decriminalisation [15] [PR, modelled].

**Strongest critique.**
- **The review's own limits [9]:**
  - "there is conflicting evidence on whether violence is reported more often since decriminalisation" and "a marked reluctance amongst sex workers to follow through on complaints";
  - the majority felt the law "could do little about violence";
  - immigration authorities monitor only the indoor sector.
  - New Zealand also prohibits non-residents from working in the sex industry. The review itself credits this, plus "geographical isolation", with protecting against traffickers. Migrants working in breach of that rule are "not protected" by the Act.
- Transferability: the decriminalisation researcher cautions that New Zealand is a small, isolated island state, and "it cannot be claimed that decriminalization will be experienced in the same way in other countries" [10].
- The formal model finds decriminalisation, like other current policies, "ineffective against trafficking" because it does not separate voluntary from coerced supply [5] [PR, theory].
- The Rhode Island quasi-experiment found a larger indoor market [16]. Opponents argue this raises demand that traffickers can exploit [12] [NGO].

## 4. Legalisation / regulation (legal and state-licensed; Germany 2002/2017, the Netherlands 2000)

**Strongest evidence-based case.**
- **Germany's evaluation of the 2017 Prostitute Protection Act (KFN, 2025) [22] [GOV]:**
  - surveyed about 2,300 sex workers, 800 officials, 3,400 clients and 280 operators;
  - found that the law "has not failed" (this brief's translation of "nicht gescheitert"). Working conditions in licensed businesses and the state's ability to monitor them appear to have improved, and registration informs workers of their rights.
- **Theory [5] [PR]:** licensing "dominates both decriminalization and criminalization of prostitutes" because it distinguishes voluntary from coerced supply. A hybrid of licensing plus penalties for buying from *unlicensed* sellers could in theory divert demand away from traffickers. This is a theoretical result, untested empirically.
- **Netherlands (WODC 2007) [23] [GOV]:** awareness among operators and workers about preventing involuntary work "seems to have grown", and licensing of businesses improved by 2006.

**Strongest critique.**
- **Cho, Dreher and Neumayer (2013) [1] [PR]:**
  - across up to 150 countries, "the scale effect dominates the substitution effect. On average, countries where prostitution is legal experience larger reported human trafficking inflows."
  - Case studies: Germany's ILO-estimated trafficking-victim stock (2004) was about 32,800, "about 62 times more than in Sweden", and Denmark's more than four times Sweden's [1]. The same paper also states that "the share of trafficked individuals among all prostitutes appears to be quite similar in both countries". The Germany–Sweden difference is therefore driven by market size, not by a measured difference in the share of trafficked people among those selling sex [1].
  - Jakobsson and Kotsadam reach the same ordering [2] [PR].
- **The German evaluation [22]** also found:
  - registration takes up only part of the population, because of fear of disclosure and data-security concerns;
  - some migrants are excluded;
  - trafficking or exploitation victims are "only rarely" ("nur selten", this brief's translation) identified through registration.
- **The Dutch evaluation [23]** found that because coercion is hard to detect, "it is virtually impossible to pronounce on possible developments in the number of prostitutes working under some degree of coercion". 8% of interviewed licensed-sector workers said they started under some form of coercion, and there "seems to be no decrease in the number of prostitutes with pimps".
- **Critiques of the legalisation–trafficking finding:**
  - The authors themselves state the analysis is cross-sectional, cannot control for unobserved country differences, found no valid instrument, and uses a reporting index biased by institutional quality and Western-centred sources [1].
  - UNODC says its index does not count victims [24].
  - The Dutch National Rapporteur on Trafficking judged that the data and methods in [1] and [2] "are insufficiently reliable to support the conclusions drawn or to provide a basis for policy" [4] [GOV].
  - Weitzer argues that popular macro-level claims about trafficking's magnitude "are neither evidence-based nor verifiable" [3] [PR]. In a separate review he cites [1] and [2] among comparative designs he finds "deeply flawed" or reliant on data "insufficient to support the conclusions drawn" [3] [PR]. The specific wording of his critique of each paper is **UNVERIFIED** (full text paywalled; abstract and reference list read).
  - Reverse causality is possible (countries with large existing markets may legalise), and better detection in regulated systems is a rival explanation [1][4].

## KEY FINDINGS

1. **Criminalising those who sell sex is consistently associated with worse safety and reporting.** Repressive policing is associated with roughly threefold odds of violence [14]. Ending criminalisation in Rhode Island coincided with a 30% fall in reported rape offences [16]. Groups on both sides of the debate agree that sellers should not be criminalised [11][12].
2. **The trafficking-volume evidence is weak in every direction.** The cross-national studies linking legalisation to more trafficking [1][2] use a reporting index that UNODC says does not count victims [24]. A government rapporteur found them insufficient for policy [4].
3. **Official evaluations of the Nordic model in Sweden and Norway report smaller street markets** (Sweden about halved; Oslo 40–65% of the previous level) and judge the ban a barrier to traffickers [6][8]. Their trafficking conclusions rest on police judgement and uncertain market estimates [6][8]. Ireland's review found demand had not decreased and trafficking prevalence unknown [20].
4. **Independent studies in Canada, Ireland and France report adverse effects of client criminalisation on safety and police contact** [17][18][19][21]. These are qualitative, cross-sectional or service-report designs; none is causal.
5. **New Zealand's decriminalisation did not measurably enlarge the industry and found no sex-industry trafficking cases** [9]. Its evidence on violence reporting is "conflicting" [9], and its migrant work ban and isolation limit transferability [9][10].
6. **Regulation in Germany and the Netherlands improved conditions inside licensed businesses but rarely identifies coerced workers** [22][23]. Coercion trends there are unmeasurable [23].
7. **Formal economic theory suggests every current model faces a trade-off** between eliminating trafficking and protecting voluntary sellers. It proposes an untested licensing-plus-buyer-penalty hybrid [5].

## WHAT THE EVIDENCE CANNOT SETTLE

- **Whether any model raises or lowers the actual number of trafficking victims.** No source reviewed measures victim numbers consistently across countries or over time. The main index measures reporting [24]. Higher detection under regulation and lower detection under criminalisation are both plausible, and each would bias results in a different direction [1][4].
- **The direction of causation.** Countries may legalise *because* they already have large markets, or adopt purchase bans *because* street markets were already shrinking. Cross-sections cannot separate these [1].
- **Whether reductions in visible markets are real reductions.** Sweden's halving refers to street prostitution. The evaluation concedes limited knowledge of indoor and online markets [6], and critics dispute any overall decline [7]. Norway's figures carry "large uncertainty" [8].
- **The causal effect of client criminalisation on violence.** Studies of harm under the Nordic model are observational, small or service-based [17][18][19][21]. The Swedish and Norwegian evaluations found no such harm, using different methods and informants [6][8]. Neither side has a randomised or strong quasi-experimental design.
- **Whether New Zealand's results transfer** to large, land-bordered or high-migration countries, given its migrant work ban and geography [9][10].
- **Moral premises.** Whether commercial sex is inherently exploitation [12][13] or legitimate work requiring labour rights [11] is a normative question. Data cannot resolve it, and it shapes which outcomes each side treats as decisive.

## POINTS OF GENUINE CONSENSUS vs GENUINE DISPUTE

**Broad consensus across opposing sources:**
- People who sell sex should not be criminalised [11][12][13][14][25][26][27][28].
- Criminalising and repressively policing sellers is associated with more violence, worse health and less reporting [14][15][16].
- Coercion and trafficking require dedicated criminal law and victim protection that does not depend on cooperating with prosecution [11][13].
- Trafficking data are poor [1][4][20][23][24].

**Genuine dispute:**
- **The effect of criminalising buyers** on trafficking volume and on seller safety. Official Nordic evaluations say positive or no harm [6][8]; independent studies and NGO reports say harmful [7][17][18][19][21]; the Irish review is inconclusive [20].
- **The effect of legalisation or decriminalisation on trafficking volume.** Cross-national studies say it increases [1][2]; critics say the data cannot show this [3][4][24]; New Zealand's review found no link there [9].
