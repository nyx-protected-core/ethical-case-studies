## Motion 1. Two-party politics makes debate illogical

### Strongest case for

In the United States, national politics really does collapse into a contest between two parties. In the official 2024 presidential count, the two main-party tickets together took 98.12 percent of the recorded vote [1]. Partisanship there works as a social identity. Ordinary Democrats and Republicans increasingly dislike and distrust one another, describe members of the other party as hypocritical, selfish and closed-minded, and are less willing to socialise across party lines. That hostility is driven more by identity than by clashing issue positions [2].

Once a question is tagged as a team question, people often judge arguments by whether they help their side. In experiments on contested issues, people rated fitting arguments as stronger, counter-argued contrary points, accepted supporting points too easily, and, when free to choose what to read, sought confirming material [3]. A public that votes mainly to stop the other side will treat politics as a zero-sum identity fight [2][3]. In a 2023 Pew Research Center survey, Americans asked to sum up politics in a word most often chose terms such as "divisive" and "corrupt", and 22 percent named partisan polarisation or a lack of cooperation as the system's biggest problem [4]. One contributor added that winner-take-all contests can make each loss feel total, which would speed hostility even if party count is not itself the spark.

### Strongest case against

The motion treats a two-party structure as the cause of illogical debate. The cross-country evidence does not support that causal claim. Across twelve OECD countries over about four decades, the United States had the largest rise in affective polarisation, five countries had smaller rises, and six had a fall [5][6]. Britain, which also uses plurality voting, became less polarised on this measure [5][6]. Canada is not a two-party system: Elections Canada lists a number of registered federal parties [7], and affective polarisation there has still risen, though more mildly than in the United States [5][8]. If party count were the main driver, similar systems should have moved together, and a multi-party ballot should have been protective. They did not, and it was not.

The motion also slides from "people dislike the other side" to "debate is illogical." Those are different claims. One contributor noted that people of all parties can update in the same direction when given the same evidence [9]. Another noted that five experiments which reduced partisan dislike found no clear change in democratic norms or accountability [10]. Two-party systems also have real advantages that the motion ignores: they can make it clearer which team was in power, and they can force broad coalitions that moderate platforms [see the related case on party structure]. Polarised is not the same as illogical.

### Points of agreement

Contributors treated the United States as a two-party system at the national level and Canada as a multi-party system, and they treated the rise in American affective polarisation as real [1][2][5][7]. They also agreed that hostility between camps, not only disagreement on policy, is the relevant phenomenon, and that a fair reading has to explain why polarisation rose in some democracies and fell in others, including some that share a voting rule with the United States [5][6]. Several arguments on both sides used the same core sources, especially Iyengar and colleagues (2019) and Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro [2][5][6].

### Where the evidence resolved a disagreement

The sharpest disagreement was how to read Canada in Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro, and whether that reading could support a "two-party amplifier" story.

The January 2020 working paper covered nine countries. In that version, the United States had an estimated linear trend of 4.8 points per decade (standard error 0.6) and Canada 3.9 points per decade (standard error 1.3). The authors could not reject equal slopes for the United States and Canada [11]. An NBER digest of that paper reports that the United States gap rose by almost 70 percent between 1978 and 2016, and that the Canadian gap rose by more than 50 percent between 1988 and 2015 [12]. Read that way, Canada looked like a close second, which one could take as evidence that a multi-party system does not protect against the same trend — or, less carefully, as evidence that the two countries were on a common path.

The November 2021 revision, which matches the 2024 journal article, covers twelve countries. In the baseline estimates, the United States trend is 5.6 points per decade. Canada's is 1.5 points per decade, with a 95 percent confidence interval that includes zero (test against a zero slope: *p* = 0.126). The United States trend is significantly larger than Canada's (*p* = 0.005) [5][6]. When the comparison is limited to the two largest parties in each country, Canada's estimated trend changes sign [5][6]. The authors also warn that, for no country, does a uniform confidence band contain the linear fit, so the linear trend is only a convenient summary, and statements of statistical significance should be read with caution, especially where there are few survey years [5].

The authors' own candidate drivers, already set out in the digest of the earlier paper and carried into the later version, point away from party count: ideological divergence rose fastest in Canada and the United States; partisan sorting, including sorting by race, is a leading candidate; the rise of partisan cable news aligns with the American timing; and the countries in which polarisation fell devote more per person to public media. The spread of the internet, income inequality, trade openness and the foreign-born share of the population are discounted, because those changes were common across the sample [5][12].

Once the two versions were placed side by side, the disagreement was resolved: Canada is not a close second in the published estimates, the earlier "equal slopes" finding does not survive the revision, and the result is sample-sensitive. Party count is not shown to be the main driver.

### Moderator's verdict

The first half of the motion is right about the United States and wrong about Canada. The second half has a real core — identity, hostility and motivated reasoning do shape a good deal of public argument — but it overreaches in two places. Party count is not the main driver of the cross-country pattern; sorting and media are better supported. The claim that two-party structure is a necessary amplifier remains unproven. And a rise in dislike is not, on its own, proof that debate has become illogical, or that democratic norms have given way [9][10]. Caveats: the linear trends are only summaries, and the Canada result is sensitive to which parties and which survey rounds are included [5][6][11].

## Motion 2. Populism as an election tactic is eroding Western democracy

### Strongest case for

The strongest evidence is not about campaign language. It is about what happens after populists win executive power, on the left and on the right. Funke, Schularick and Trebesch identify 51 populist presidents and prime ministers from 1900 to 2020 in 60 countries. After 15 years, GDP per capita is about 10 percent lower than in a synthetic non-populist comparison. "Economic disintegration, decreasing macroeconomic stability, and the erosion of institutions typically go hand in hand with populist rule," including weaker checks and balances and weaker judicial and press freedoms. The economic decline is larger under left-wing populists, but it is also visible under right-wing populists [13].

A think-tank assessment of 46 populist leaders or parties in executive office from 1990 to 2018 reports that 23 percent of populists caused significant democratic backsliding, compared with 6 percent of non-populist democratically elected leaders [14]. That paper is not peer-reviewed, and its definition partly builds institutional damage into who counts as populist, so it should be read as supporting rather than decisive evidence. Expert-coded sources point the same way: V-Dem's 2026 democracy report is titled *Unraveling The Democratic Era?* [15]. Studies of established democracies also find a negative association between populists in government and institutional safeguards such as the rule of law and state transparency [16].

### Strongest case against

The motion is about populism *used as an election tactic*. The leading studies measure populists *in office*. That is a different claim. Juon and Bochsler, covering 53 established democracies from 1990 to 2016, find that populist actors increase participation and representation, especially when they remain in opposition and especially on the left. The harm they find concentrates on institutional safeguards when populists govern [16]. On that reading, populism in a campaign can be a corrective that brings excluded voters in, rather than a solvent of democracy [17][18][19].

Two further limits weaken a sweeping "erosion of Western democracy" claim. First, the Funke and related samples are global and include many non-Western cases, so they cannot, by themselves, establish a specifically Western pattern [13]. Second, much of the backsliding evidence rests on expert-coded indices. A peer-reviewed critique finds that objective indicators, such as incumbent performance in elections, show "little evidence of global democratic decline during the past decade" and raises the possibility of coder bias [20]. Populism may also be a symptom of unanswered grievances — trade shocks, austerity and a loss of confidence in incumbents — rather than their cause. Treating it purely as a threat, and prosecuting populist speech, has been found to raise the target party's support in at least one well-identified case [21]. Mainstream parties adopting radical positions have not been shown to cut radical-right support [22].

### Points of agreement

Every contribution on this motion used Funke, Schularick and Trebesch as the central economic and institutional study, and every contribution stated limits: the paper measures leaders in office, the sample is global, and erosion is typically inferred from expert-coded institutional measures [13]. Contributors also agreed that left and right should be held to the same standard, and that a fair case has to distinguish populism in opposition from populism in power [16]. There was no serious dispute that some populists in office have weakened courts, the press and the rule of law. The dispute was about how far that finding can be stretched to cover "populism as an election tactic" and "Western democracy" as a whole.

### Where the evidence resolved a disagreement

The motion as worded was not supported. The evidence supports a narrower claim: populists who win executive power, on the left and on the right, tend to erode institutional safeguards and to leave a substantial economic cost. It does not show that populism used only as a campaign tactic is itself eroding Western democracy. In opposition, the same literature finds participation gains [16]. The measurement dispute between expert-coded decline and more stable objective indicators was not resolved, and both readings remain live [15][20].

On remedies, the debate did not need to pick a single cure, but the tested evidence has a clear pattern. Short inoculation videos that teach people to spot manipulation, and messages that correct partisan misperceptions, have experimental support, though there is only limited evidence that effects on antidemocratic attitudes last beyond about two weeks [23][24]. Prosecuting a politician's speech, and mainstream mimicry of radical positions, are associated with backfire or null effects [21][22].

### Moderator's verdict

Partly supported, in a narrower form. The evidence is strongest for populists in executive office, not for populism as an election tactic, and it is global rather than specifically Western. The honest conclusion is that populists who gain executive power often weaken institutional safeguards and impose economic costs, and that the tactic of running as a populist is not, on its own, shown to erode democracy. A further open question is whether expert-coded indices overstate recent decline [20].

## Motion 3. Equal gender participation should rest on equal merit rather than balancing numbers

### Strongest case for

The most careful version of the motion is not that any concern with women's or men's under-representation is misplaced. It is that the test of a fair rule is whether it opens a field to whoever can do the work, not whether it produces a target head-count. Numeric balance can be a useful warning light when a fair process keeps producing an odd result. It is a poor goal in itself. That is the framing of the related cases on quotas, evaluation and gaps in both directions.

Merit-focused tools can move outcomes without excluding anyone from a given post. Side-by-side comparison leads evaluators to rely more on individual performance and less on group stereotypes [25]. Assigning organisational responsibility for fair selection produced broader gains in managerial composition than training or evaluation exercises [26]. A 2023 meta-analysis of reactions to affirmative action finds small to moderate negative effects overall, stronger for stronger preferences, and, notably, that "tiebreak affirmative-action policies" do not appear to result in negative reactions [27]. Visible or opaque preference policies, by contrast, have been found to lower the rated competence of people labelled as beneficiaries [28].

Some headline "merit" tools also backfire, which is part of the case for designing them carefully rather than for abandoning merit as the test. In a French randomised programme, firms that received anonymised résumés became less likely to interview and hire minority candidates, because anonymising removed context that had offset negative signals [29]. That trial concerns ethnic minorities, not women, and is included only as evidence on the tool. In laboratory experiments, labelling an organisation "meritocratic" led managers to give a male employee a larger bonus than an equally performing woman [30].

### Strongest case against

"Merit" is not a neutral quantity waiting to be read off a file. It is shaped by who sets the criteria and who is already in the room. The strongest evidence that a numerical rule can raise merit, rather than trade it away, comes from Sweden. The Social Democrats' 1993 "zipper" rule, which alternated men and women on municipal ballots, "raised the competence of male politicians where it raised female representation the most." The mechanism was the departure of mediocre male leaders. Competent men gained ground; women's measured competence neither rose nor fell [31]. Men were not cut out as a class. Weaker incumbents were replaced. In that setting, a quota broke an insider arrangement and improved the quality of those who remained.

A related warning comes from generalising too quickly from that case, or from treating all numerical rules as alike. The Swedish evidence is from one party, in one proportional system, with competence measured from earnings residuals [31]. Norway's 40 percent board quota was followed, in one study, by a drop in firm value and by younger, less experienced boards [32]. A later re-analysis finds the valuation effect statistically insignificant and argues that the supply of qualified women was large enough to avoid those costs [33]. The size of any firm-value loss is therefore disputed, and the later paper should be read alongside the earlier one. Even then, the Norwegian quota had "very little discernible impact" on women in those firms beyond the directors who were appointed [34]. Hard numerical rules can also attach a stigma of incompetence to people who would have succeeded anyway [27][28].

### Points of agreement

Almost every contribution on this motion treated Besley, Folke, Persson and Rickne (2017) as the central political study, and almost every contribution stated the same limits: one country, one party's voluntary quota, and an earnings-based competence measure [31]. Contributors also agreed that fairness to men is a real criterion, and that a quota which removed low-competence incumbents is a different object from a quota that simply reserves seats. The Norway firm-value finding was not used as settled fact once the later re-analysis had been put on the table [32][33]. No contribution argued for numerical balance as an end in itself, and no contribution argued that today's gaps are already proof of a fair process.

### Where the evidence resolved a disagreement

The apparent conflict between "merit" and "balance" was narrower than the motion's wording suggested. In the one well-identified political case, a numerical rule raised male competence by removing mediocre incumbents [31]. That does not vindicate head-count targets in general. It shows that merit and a numerical rule need not conflict where selection has been captured by insiders. Transfer to binding quotas, to majoritarian systems, or to corporate boards was not demonstrated [31]. The stigma finding is also conditional: it is strongest for strong or unclear preferences, and it is not found for sex-neutral tie-breaks [27].

The best-supported practical package, consistent with the related evidence review, is therefore: transparent, structured, comparative selection, with wider shortlists and named accountability, and at most a sex-neutral tie-break. A time-limited quota is justified only where insiders can be shown to have captured selection, as in the Swedish party-list case [25][26][27][31].

### Moderator's verdict

Nuanced support for the motion, with an important correction. Equal participation should rest on equal merit and genuine equal opportunity, not on balancing numbers for their own sake. The correction is that merit and a carefully designed numerical rule are not strict opposites. In the best-identified case they coincided, because the rule removed unmerited advantage. That case has not been shown to generalise, and visible or strong preferences carry a documented stigma cost. The evidence favours repairing selection so that capable men and capable women can both be chosen, and favours head-count mandates only as a targeted, time-limited remedy for demonstrated insider capture.

## Logical debate practice

### Moves that strengthened the debate

**Premise checks.** Before arguing that "two-party politics" made debate illogical, contributors checked whether the countries under discussion are in fact two-party systems. The official Canadian party register settled that premise for Canada [7]. The same habit appeared when contributors refused to treat "polarised" as a synonym for "illogical," and when they refused to treat "populism as a campaign tactic" as the same thing as "populists in executive office."

**Steelmanning.** Arguments for each motion stated the strongest point against it in the same breath: that two parties may still be an amplifier even if they are not the spark; that populism may be a symptom of neglected grievances, or a corrective that raises turnout; that a quota can raise merit when it evicts weak incumbents. Those counters were drawn from the same literature as the case for the motion, not from caricatures.

**Conceding on evidence.** The Canada reading of Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro was not left as a clash of impressions. The working-paper figures and the published figures were compared, the change of sign under a two-party restriction was noted, and the earlier "equal slopes" claim was dropped [5][6][11][12]. On Motion 2, the claim was narrowed from the motion's wording to the claim the studies actually test. On Motion 3, the Norway firm-value finding was not treated as decisive once a later re-analysis had disputed it [32][33].

**Holding left and right, and men and women, to the same standard.** The populism evidence was read for both left-wing and right-wing governments [13][16]. The merit discussion treated unfairness to men as a real cost and treated men's under-representation in some fields as part of the same problem of equal opportunity, not as a rival claim [see the related case on gaps in both directions].

### Moves that weakened the debate

**Treating two versions of the same study as if they said the same thing.** Before the Canada check, arguments cited Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro both for the claim that Canada was nearly as steep a riser as the United States and for the claim that Canada was only a smaller, non-significant riser. Those are readings of two different versions. Until they were separated, the same citation was being used to support opposite causal stories.

**Sliding between a motion's wording and a narrower finding.** The second motion asked about populism as an election tactic in Western democracies. Several arguments answered with evidence on populists in executive office in a global sample. The evidence is important, but it does not, without a further step, establish the motion as worded.

**Equating adjacent phenomena.** Dislike of the other party is not the same as illogical argument, and a rise in affective polarisation is not the same as a collapse in democratic norms [9][10]. "Merit" is not the same as the absence of any numerical rule, and a zipper quota in one party is not the same as a binding reservation in another system [31].

**Over-generalising from a single setting.** The Swedish zipper-quota result is the best evidence that a numerical rule can raise competence. Using it as if it settled the case for binding quotas elsewhere, or using a single firm-value study as if it settled the case against them, both overreach [31][32][33].

**Leaving a measurement dispute unstated.** Expert-coded democracy indices and objective indicators can point in different directions [15][20]. An argument that quotes only one of those families, without saying so, looks stronger than the literature is.

The useful discipline, for later debates as well as this one, is straightforward: check the premise, state the best contrary argument, cite the version of the paper actually being used, and concede when the text does not reach as far as the motion.
