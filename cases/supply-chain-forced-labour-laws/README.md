# Forced Labour in Global Supply Chains: Import Bans, Due Diligence Laws and What They Do for Workers

*A case study in trade, labour rights and the ethics of regulating at a distance. Status of the law checked against official sources as of 18 November 2026.*

## 1. Summary

Between 2021 and 2024, the main importing economies moved from voluntary codes towards binding law on forced labour in supply chains. The United States has the most developed tool: an import ban dating from 1930, made much stronger by a statutory presumption against goods from China's Xinjiang region. The European Union adopted two instruments in 2024. One is a market ban on products made with forced labour, which applies from December 2027. The other is a directive requiring large companies to carry out human rights and environmental due diligence. That directive was cut back substantially in February 2026, before any company had to comply. Germany and France had already passed national due diligence laws. Germany has since stopped enforcing parts of its law while it amends it, and France's law is being tested in court. Canada, the United Kingdom and Australia have reporting-only modern slavery or supply-chain laws, with weak or no penalties for a thin statement.

This study sets out what these laws require, what enforcement data show, and what the evidence says about the question that matters most ethically: do bans and due diligence duties help the people in forced labour, or do they mainly reroute goods, shift risk onto suppliers, and let buyers walk away? The evidence is uneven. There are careful studies for a few cases, mainly conflict minerals in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Uzbek cotton and Thai seafood. For most others, including solar polysilicon and most of the impact of the Xinjiang measures, the available record consists of enforcement counts and trade flows, which say little about workers' welfare. The study tries to make that gap clear rather than fill it.

## 2. The scale of the problem

The ILO, Walk Free and IOM's 2022 *Global Estimates of Modern Slavery* put the number of people in forced labour on an average day in 2021 at 27.6 million, or 3.5 per thousand people worldwide [1]. Of these, 11.8 million were women and girls and 3.3 million were children. The total was 2.7 million higher than in 2016 [1]. Most forced labour (86 per cent) is imposed by private actors: 63 per cent in sectors other than commercial sex and 23 per cent in forced commercial sexual exploitation. The remaining 14 per cent, about 3.9 million people, is imposed by state authorities [1]. The estimates also find that adult migrant workers are more than three times as likely to be in forced labour as non-migrant adults [1].

The ILO's 2024 report *Profits and Poverty: The economics of forced labour* estimates that forced labour in the private economy generates US$236 billion a year in illegal profits, or close to US$10,000 per victim [2]. That is US$64 billion more than in 2014. Over the same period, profit per victim rose from US$8,269 to US$9,995, an increase of about 21 per cent [2]. The estimate is based on the 23.7 million people in forced labour in the private economy. The report notes that it leaves out profits from illegal recruitment fees, so the true figure is higher [2].

These numbers matter for policy design in two ways. First, state-imposed forced labour is a distinct category. Supplier audits and dialogue with employers cannot fix it, because the state is the perpetrator. This is the core of the Xinjiang problem. Second, most forced labour occurs in domestic economies and in work that never enters international trade. Trade measures can reach only the part that is linked to exports.

## 3. The legal instruments

### 3.1 European Union: the Forced Labour Regulation (EU) 2024/3015

The Forced Labour Regulation was adopted on 27 November 2024 and published in the Official Journal on 12 December 2024 [3]. It entered into force the following day. Most of its provisions apply from **14 December 2027**. Some provisions, mainly those on the Commission's guidelines, the risk database, the information portal and the penalty framework, have applied since 13 December 2024 [3].

**What it prohibits.** Article 3 bars economic operators from placing or making available on the EU market, or exporting from it, any product made wholly or partly with forced labour [3]. The ban is product-based rather than company-based. It covers goods made in the EU as well as imports, and it applies to companies of all sizes.

**What it does not require.** Article 1(3) says the Regulation creates no new due diligence obligations for operators [3]. The Commission's guidelines, adopted as C/2026/4637 and published in the Official Journal on 3 September 2026, explain how authorities should apply the risk-based approach and how they will take operators' existing due diligence into account [4]. Article 11 required those guidelines by 14 June 2026, so they were also late, by a little under three months [3][4].

**How a case proceeds.** Competent authorities in member states, or the Commission for risks outside the EU, follow a risk-based process:
- **Preliminary phase (Article 17).** The authority asks the operators concerned about their mitigation measures. Operators have 30 working days to respond. Within 30 working days of receiving the information, the authority decides whether there is a "substantiated concern" [3].
- **Investigation (Article 18).** Operators get between 30 and 60 working days to provide information. Under Article 19, inspections in a third country require the consent of the operators concerned and no objection from that country's government [3].
- **Decision (Article 20).** The authority should "endeavour" to decide within nine months. A decision prohibits the product, orders its withdrawal and orders its disposal [3]. If operators, or a public authority, fail to cooperate, the authority may decide on the basis of "facts available". This includes cases where the risk is identified as state-imposed forced labour [3]. Article 20(5) allows a product to be withheld rather than disposed of when the supply chain is of strategic or critical importance to the Union [3]. Other member states must recognise and enforce a decision.
- **Burden of proof.** The recitals place the burden of proving a violation on the competent authority, not on the operator [3]. This is the opposite of the US Xinjiang presumption (Section 3.3).
- **Penalties.** Member states set the penalties (Article 37) [3].

**The risk database.** Article 8 requires the Commission to make public, by 14 June 2026, a database of forced labour risk areas and products. The database is not to name individual companies, and it is to identify sectors where state-imposed forced labour exists [3]. On 18 November 2026, the Commission's page on the database still described it as "in development" [5]. The statutory deadline has therefore been missed by about five months, and operators have about a year left before the ban applies.

### 3.2 European Union: the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive and its rollback

**The original 2024 directive.** Directive (EU) 2024/1760 was adopted on 13 June 2024 and entered into force on 25 July 2024 [6][9]. As adopted, it covered EU companies with more than 1,000 employees and more than €450 million in net worldwide turnover, with thresholds for non-EU companies based on their EU turnover [6]. It required companies to identify, prevent, mitigate and end actual and potential adverse impacts across their own operations, their subsidiaries and their "chain of activities". It created an EU-wide civil liability regime and required climate transition plans. Member states were to set penalties with a maximum of "not less than 5 %" of net worldwide turnover [6]. Member states had to transpose it by 26 July 2026, and obligations were to apply in stages from 26 July 2027 [6]. On business relationships, the 2024 text provided for termination as a last resort where an enhanced prevention plan had failed and the impact was severe [6].

**"Stop-the-clock" (April 2025).** Directive (EU) 2025/794 of 14 April 2025 delayed the timetable by one year before any obligation had applied. Transposition moved to 26 July 2027, and the first wave of application, for companies with more than 3,000 employees and more than €900 million in turnover, moved to 26 July 2028 [7].

**Omnibus I (February 2026).** Directive (EU) 2026/470 of 24 February 2026 was published on 26 February 2026 and entered into force on 18 March 2026 [8]. It rewrote the directive before any company had to comply:
- **Scope.** EU companies are covered only above 5,000 employees and €1.5 billion in net worldwide turnover. Non-EU companies are covered only above €1.5 billion in net turnover generated in the EU [8].
- **Climate.** The obligation to adopt a climate transition plan (Article 22) was deleted [8].
- **Civil liability.** The harmonised EU civil liability regime in Article 29(1) was deleted. Civil liability therefore falls back on national law. The recitals and the remaining text still require member states to ensure access to justice [8].
- **Penalties.** The maximum fine is now a uniform cap of 3 per cent of net worldwide turnover, replacing the earlier floor of "not less than 5 %" for the maximum [8].
- **Depth of diligence.** Companies are to carry out an initial scoping exercise "based solely on reasonably available information". They may ask business partners with fewer than 5,000 employees for information only where it cannot reasonably be obtained some other way [8].
- **From termination to suspension.** This is the change that matters most for this study. Articles 10(6) and 11(7) now provide for *suspending* a business relationship "as a last resort", with no duty to terminate. Before suspending, a company must assess whether the harm from suspending would be "manifestly more severe" than the impact it cannot prevent. If so, it need not suspend [8]. The recitals describe suspension as a way to keep working with the supplier, using the leverage that suspension creates, until the impact has been addressed [8].
- **Timetable.** Member states must transpose by **26 July 2028**. Companies must comply from **26 July 2029**, and reporting applies to financial years starting on or after 1 January 2030 [8]. The Commission's CSDDD page, checked in November 2026, gives the same dates and lists both amending directives [9].

As of late 2026, then, the CSDDD is in force as amended, but no company is yet required to comply. The earliest compliance date is about two and a half years away. The number of companies covered is far smaller than in 2024, the EU-wide liability route is gone, and the ultimate sanction on a business relationship is suspension rather than exit.

### 3.3 United States: Section 307, Withhold Release Orders and the UFLPA

**Section 307 and WROs.** Section 307 of the Tariff Act of 1930 (19 U.S.C. § 1307) prohibits imports of goods "mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part in any foreign country by convict labor or/and forced labor, or/and indentured labor under penal sanctions" [16]. When US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has information reasonably indicating forced labour, it issues a Withhold Release Order (WRO). Port officers then detain the goods named in the order. Importers may export or destroy the goods, or try to show they were not made with forced labour [15][16]. CBP also issues Findings, which have replaced some WROs [12]. CBP lifts or modifies an order only once it has evidence that the producer no longer uses forced labour [14].

**The UFLPA.** The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (Public Law 117-78, 23 December 2021) created a rebuttable presumption. Goods mined, produced or manufactured wholly or partly in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, or by entities on a list maintained by the Department of Homeland Security, are presumed to be made with forced labour and are barred from entry [10]. An importer can obtain an exception only by fully complying with the government's guidance, responding completely to CBP's requests for information, and showing by "clear and convincing evidence" that the goods were not made with forced labour. CBP must report each exception to Congress within 30 days [10]. CBP began enforcing the presumption on 21 June 2022 [11].

The UFLPA Entity List had 210 dated entries when checked, and was last updated on 28 October 2026 [13]. Entries include the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps, listed on 21 June 2022, and Xinjiang Zijin Zinc Industry Co., Ltd., listed on 15 January 2025 [13].

**Enforcement data.** CBP publishes two data sets, and they should not be combined:

1. *UFLPA statistics.* CBP's dashboard guide covers 21 June 2022 to 24 November 2025 [11]:
   - 65,707 shipments stopped, worth about US$3.91 billion.
   - Of these, 24,215 were denied entry (worth about US$961 million), 39,829 were released, and 1,663 were pending.
   - By value, electronics was the largest industry (about US$3.27 billion). By count, apparel, footwear and textiles was the largest (27,994 shipments).
   - By value, the top countries of origin were Malaysia, Vietnam and Thailand, not China. Malaysia alone accounted for 3,449 shipments worth about US$1.62 billion, or 41.4 per cent of the value stopped.
   - In fiscal year 2024, HTS heading 8541, which includes photovoltaic cells and modules, made up about 91 per cent of the value of electronics stopped (2,813 shipments, about US$1.54 billion).
   - The guide notes that a "shipment" is now counted per import line. The figures are therefore not comparable with earlier versions of the dashboard [11].

2. *All forced labour authorities.* CBP's enforcement page, last modified 26 October 2026, reports 60 active WROs and 9 active Findings [12]:
   - WROs issued: one in FY2024, four in FY2025 and eight in FY2026.
   - Shipments stopped under all forced labour authorities: 4,850 worth US$1.75 billion in FY2024; 7,325 worth US$164.47 million in FY2025; and 4,079 worth US$326.55 million in FY2026 [12].
   - Eleven WROs have been superseded by the UFLPA presumption and six by Findings. One Finding is on hold pending litigation [12].

Recent actions show the range of the tool:
- **Distant-water fishing.** A WRO against the Chinese-flagged fishing vessel *Zhen Fa 7* (28 May 2025) covers squid and other seafood. CBP cited indicators including retention of identity documents, isolation, physical and sexual violence and debt bondage. CBP described it as the sixth WRO it had issued on a fishing vessel since 2020 [16].
- **Indonesian palm oil.** Two WROs (29 September 2026) cover palm oil from Mitra Aneka Rezeki and Hardaya Inti Plantation in Indonesia. CBP found nine and seven ILO forced labour indicators respectively [15]. That release gives "60 WROs and eight Findings", one fewer Finding than the enforcement page shows [12][15]. The counts move as orders are issued and modified.
- **Malaysian palm oil (remediation).** On 15 January 2026, CBP modified the September 2020 WRO against FGV Holdings Berhad's palm oil. CBP said that since 2019 it had issued and modified eight WROs and Findings in Malaysia's palm oil and glove sectors, and that "companies have repaid over $85 million in withheld wages and recruitment fees to workers trapped in debt bondage" [14].

### 3.4 Germany: the Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG)

The *Lieferkettensorgfaltspflichtengesetz* of 16 July 2021 took effect on 1 January 2023 [17]. It covered companies with at least 3,000 employees in Germany, and from 1 January 2024 those with at least 1,000 [17]. It requires risk management, risk analysis, preventive and remedial measures, a complaints procedure and documentation for companies' own operations and direct suppliers, with more limited duties for indirect suppliers. Section 10(2) requires an annual report to be filed within four months of the end of the financial year. Section 24 allows fines of up to 2 per cent of average annual turnover for the largest companies [17].

The relief came in two steps:
- **Administrative relief.** On 1 October 2025, the federal enforcement authority (BAFA) announced that, on the instruction of the responsible federal ministries, it had stopped reviewing company reports. It said it was dropping proceedings under the fine provisions that the planned amendment would delete, and would impose fines only for serious violations linked to particularly grave human rights abuses [18]. BAFA's reporting page states that reports can no longer be submitted [19].
- **Legislative amendment.** The federal government's bill (Bundestag printed paper 21/2474, 29 October 2025, following a cabinet decision of 3 September 2025) would abolish the reporting duty, keep the substantive due diligence duties, and limit sanctions to serious violations [20]. The bill had its first reading in the Bundestag on 16 January 2026, was referred to committee, and, as of the sources opened for this study, had not been enacted [20]. The bill presents this as a transition until the LkSG is replaced by a law transposing the CSDDD. It estimates that about 5,200 companies were required to report and that business would save about €4.1 million a year [20].

As of mid-November 2026, I could not confirm from an official source that the amendment has been enacted. The consolidated text on the federal law portal still contains Section 10(2) [17]. What has clearly changed so far is enforcement practice, not the statute.

### 3.5 France: the Duty of Vigilance Law (2017)

France's Law No. 2017-399 of 27 March 2017 requires large companies to draw up, publish and implement a "vigilance plan" setting out reasonable measures to identify and prevent or mitigate risks of serious harm to human rights and fundamental freedoms, the health and safety of persons and the environment arising from their activities and those of the companies they control [21]. It applies to companies with at least 5,000 employees in France, or at least 10,000 including foreign subsidiaries [21]. Enforcement is in the hands of private parties. An interested party must first send a formal notice, after which the company has three months to comply. The party can then ask the Tribunal judiciaire de Paris, which has nationwide jurisdiction over these claims, for an injunction [21]. The provision was originally Article L.225-102-4 of the Commercial Code and became Article L.225-102-1 from 1 January 2025 [22].

The Paris Court of Appeal's specialist chamber has begun to set out the law's procedural and substantive limits:
- **18 June 2024, three judgments** [21]:
  - In a case against **TotalEnergies** over greenhouse gas emissions, and a case against **EDF** over wind farms in Mexico, the court found some claimants admissible and sent the merits back to the tribunal.
  - In a case against **Vigie Groupe** (formerly Suez) concerning Chile, it held the claim inadmissible because the subsidiary, not the parent company that owed the vigilance duty, had been sued.
  - It held that local authorities may bring claims only on the basis of a local public interest, not a general global one.
- **17 June 2025, first merits ruling on appeal** [22]. The court upheld the Tribunal judiciaire's judgment of 5 December 2023 in a case brought by a trade union against **La Poste**. It ordered the company to complete its 2021 vigilance plan on four of the five measures challenged: a more detailed risk map, procedures for evaluating suppliers based on the identified risks, an alert mechanism set up in consultation with representative unions, and a monitoring system for the measures.

Two features of the French cases are relevant here. First, the remedy is an order to improve the plan, not compensation to workers. Second, after almost a decade, the case law is still mostly about admissibility and the content of plans rather than about outcomes for workers in supply chains.

### 3.6 Canada, the United Kingdom and Australia: reporting-only laws

Three other common-law jurisdictions require large organisations to report on modern slavery or forced labour in their supply chains. All three are disclosure regimes. Unlike the US import ban or the EU product ban, they do not themselves stop goods at the border, and unlike the original CSDDD they do not create an EU-style duty to end a business relationship. The shared enforcement critique is that a reporting-only law with weak or no penalties can produce statements without changing conditions for workers.

**Canada.** The *Fighting Against Forced Labour and Child Labour in Supply Chains Act* (S.C. 2023, c. 9, commonly S-211) received Royal Assent on 11 May 2023 and came into force on 1 January 2024, being 1 January of the year after assent [41][42]. Covered entities and government institutions must report annually to the Minister of Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness by 31 May on the steps they have taken in the previous financial year to prevent and reduce the risk that forced labour or child labour is used in their activities or supply chains [41][42]. Reports are published by the reporting body and listed in a Public Safety catalogue [42].

**United Kingdom.** Section 54 of the Modern Slavery Act 2015 requires a commercial organisation that supplies goods or services and has a total turnover of £36 million or more to publish an annual slavery and human trafficking statement [43][44]. Official guidance encourages organisations to add the statement to the government's modern slavery statement registry; it does not treat registry upload as a completed legal substitute for publication [43]. The government has announced future changes to the reporting requirements. Official guidance states that "these changes have not yet come into effect and organisations should continue to report under the current requirements" [43]. The current stage of any bill that would add penalties or make registry submission mandatory is therefore not yet law, and should be re-checked before the point is relied on as enacted (**UNVERIFIED** as to the present parliamentary stage).

**Australia.** The *Modern Slavery Act 2018* (Cth) entered into force on 1 January 2019. It requires entities with annual consolidated revenue of at least A$100 million to prepare annual modern slavery statements, which the government publishes on a public register [45]. Professor John McMillan AO led the statutory review of the first three years of the Act. The report was tabled on 25 May 2023 and made 30 recommendations, including penalties for non-compliance, a lower reporting threshold, and a due diligence system [45]. On 2 December 2024 the government released its response, agreeing in full, in part, or in principle to 25 of the 30 recommendations and noting five [45]. Consultation on strengthening the Act followed that response. As with the UK, the current stage of any amending legislation should be re-checked before it is treated as enacted.

## 4. Do bans and due diligence help workers, or reroute goods?

The direct evidence is thin. Enforcement statistics count shipments, not people. Trade data show where goods go, not what happens to workers. Most programmes have never been evaluated with a counterfactual. The cases below are ordered roughly by the strength of the evidence.

### 4.1 Uzbek cotton: pressure and engagement under international monitoring

Uzbekistan is the strongest example of state-imposed forced labour being reduced at scale. A coalition boycott of Uzbek cotton began in 2011, and 331 international brands and retailers joined it [29]. Alongside it, the ILO worked with Uzbekistan from 2013 to reform practices in the cotton sector, with the government, employers' and workers' organisations and civil society [29]. The ILO's monitoring of the 2021 harvest found that Uzbek cotton was free of systemic child labour and systemic forced labour [28]:
- 99 per cent of pickers interviewed (about 11,000 interviews) worked voluntarily.
- About 1 per cent reported direct or perceived coercion, mostly linked to local officials and social benefits (0.47 per cent of respondents).
- About two million children had been taken out of child labour, and half a million adults out of forced labour, since reforms began seven years earlier.

The government's reforms included abolishing the state order system for cotton [28]. The boycott was lifted on 10 March 2022 [29].

This is a combination of market pressure, an exit threat and sustained, independent, on-the-ground monitoring within a structured partnership with the state. It cannot show the effect of the boycott alone, and Uzbekistan's government chose to cooperate. The contrast with Xinjiang, where independent monitoring is not available, is the most important lesson from this case.

### 4.2 Thai seafood: real improvement, persistent abuse

After 2014, pressure from the European Union, the US government and buyers helped push Thailand to adopt a series of fisheries and labour reforms [30]:
- A 2017 royal ordinance on employing foreign workers.
- Ratification in 2018–2019 of the ILO Protocol of 2014 to the Forced Labour Convention and the Work in Fishing Convention (No. 188), making Thailand the first country in Asia to adopt both.

The ILO's 2020 endline survey under the EU-funded Ship to Shore Rights Project interviewed 470 workers in 11 coastal provinces [30]. Pay had risen since 2017, by 28 per cent for fishers and 15 per cent for seafood processing workers. But 44 per cent could not recall signing a work contract, and illegal deductions for food and housing affected 16 per cent of fishers and 18 per cent of seafood workers. The survey found forced labour among 14 per cent of fishers and 7 per cent of seafood processing workers [30]. The report says this indicates "little change" from a 2013 ILO estimate of 17 per cent in fishing, while warning that differences in method make close comparison difficult [30]. It also notes that Thai law bars migrant workers from forming or leading unions [30].

Marschke and Vandergeest argue that slavery and trafficking frames were useful for drawing attention to criminal recruitment, but are a poor basis for tackling root causes. In their view, lasting improvement depends on changing migration management and reducing the vulnerabilities that stop migrant workers from obtaining better conditions [32]. At the global level, Tickler and colleagues find that national modern slavery estimates correlate with fisheries governance among major fishing countries. They also find that countries with documented labour abuse at sea tend to have more subsidised distant-water fishing and poorer catch reporting [31]. Distant-water fleets are hard to reach with trade measures because the work happens far from any port inspector, which is why CBP has started to target individual vessels [16].

### 4.3 Malaysian palm oil and gloves: remediation through WROs

The Malaysian WROs are the clearest US example of an import ban producing a measurable transfer to workers. CBP reports repayments of more than US$85 million in withheld wages and recruitment fees across eight orders and Findings since 2019 [14]. This figure is reported by the enforcing agency. It has not been independently audited, and it does not show whether recruitment-fee debt has reappeared in later hiring. Still, it shows a mechanism: a ban that can be lifted once workers are repaid gives firms a reason to fix the problem rather than leave. By contrast, the September 2026 orders on two Indonesian producers show that the same commodity risk persists elsewhere in the region [15].

### 4.4 Xinjiang cotton and solar polysilicon: enforcement without verification

For Xinjiang, the main measurable effects are on trade. CBP's UFLPA data show that apparel, footwear and textiles account for the largest number of stopped shipments. By value, the main origins of stopped goods are Malaysia, Vietnam and Thailand rather than China [11]. This is consistent with Xinjiang inputs reaching the United States through third-country processing, and with enforcement following that route. It is not, by itself, evidence of how much rerouting occurs. For solar, the dominance of HTS heading 8541 in electronics stops in FY2024 [11] shows how far the presumption reached into photovoltaic supply chains.

What the data cannot show is whether anyone in Xinjiang is better off. Because the forced labour is state-imposed [1], firms cannot remediate it through supplier engagement. Independent audits of the kind used in Uzbekistan are not possible. The EU Regulation recognises this by allowing decisions on "facts available" where a state obstructs [3]. A ban in this setting works mainly as a refusal to be complicit, and perhaps as diplomatic pressure, not as a remedy for workers. Whether this is acceptable depends on whether one sees non-complicity as a sufficient goal in itself (Section 6).

The legal literature also questions the US approach on trade-law grounds. Ru argues that the UFLPA is hard to reconcile with GATT Articles I and XI. The public morals exception in Article XX(a) is the most plausible defence, but the measure would struggle to satisfy the chapeau's non-discrimination test [40].

### 4.5 Cobalt and the conflict minerals precedent: when exit harms the people it aims to protect

No forced labour import ban targets DRC cobalt directly. The closest precedent is Section 1502 of the US Dodd-Frank Act (2010), which pushed buyers away from tin, tantalum, tungsten and gold from the eastern DRC. Three studies find harmful unintended effects:
- **Violence.** Parker and Vadheim, using georeferenced data, find that the law increased looting of civilians and moved militia battles towards unregulated gold-mining areas [33].
- **Longer-run violence.** Stoop, Verpoorten and van der Windt extend the analysis to 2013–2015. In territories with the average number of gold mines, they find the law increased battles by 44 per cent, looting by 51 per cent and violence against civilians by 28 per cent, relative to pre-law averages [34].
- **Infant mortality.** Parker, Foltz and Elsea find that the de facto boycott increased the probability of infant death in villages near regulated mineral deposits by at least 143 per cent, apparently by reducing mothers' spending on infant health care [35].

For cobalt itself, the research is qualitative. Sovacool's fieldwork documents exploitation, gendered harms and child labour in artisanal mining. It also finds cases where mining empowers people, and calls for reforms involving the Congolese state, suppliers and end users rather than exit [37]. Calvão, Mcdonald and Bolay find that corporate formalisation programmes rely on artisanal workers who are not paid wages. They describe this as an "outsourcing of responsibility" by large mining companies [36]. The lesson for forced labour policy is that, where a commodity supports large numbers of poor households, a de facto boycott can harm the people it aims to protect. Engagement that formalises work can also move risk around instead of removing it.

### 4.6 Information and naming

Klymak studies the US government's list of goods produced with child or forced labour and finds that being listed reduced US imports of the goods concerned. The effect was concentrated in goods close to final consumers, with no effect for intermediate goods [38]. This suggests that information-based tools reach consumer-facing products but not the upstream inputs, such as polysilicon, cotton lint and metals, where much of the risk lies. LeBaron's review of research on the business of forced labour reaches a related conclusion. Root causes follow identifiable patterns, workers move in and out of forced labour over short periods, and studies on the ground find serious gaps between corporate social responsibility standards and actual practice on the indicators most relevant to forced labour [39].

### 4.7 What the evidence supports

- **Reasonably supported.** Bans that can be lifted on proof of remediation can produce payments to workers (Malaysia) [14]. Pressure combined with monitored engagement with the state can reduce state-imposed forced labour (Uzbekistan) [28][29]. Pressure can drive legal reform without ending abuse (Thailand) [30].
- **Supported for minerals; plausible elsewhere.** De facto boycotts of poor producing regions can cause serious collateral harm [33][34][35].
- **Not established.** Whether the UFLPA or EU measures have improved conditions for any worker in Xinjiang. How much trade has been rerouted rather than stopped. Whether due diligence laws have changed working conditions anywhere. I found no peer-reviewed counterfactual evaluation of any due diligence statute's effect on workers.

## 5. The view from producer countries

Exporting countries' concerns about mandatory due diligence are on the WTO record. The WTO's trade concerns database shows that the EU Deforestation Regulation, a due diligence measure with the same design as the CSDDD, has been raised at the Committee on Technical Barriers to Trade at every listed meeting from November 2023 to November 2026 [26]. Members raising or supporting the concern include Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, India, Indonesia, Kenya, Malaysia, Pakistan, Paraguay, Peru, South Africa and Tanzania, along with developed members such as Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United States [26]. The issue is not new. Between 2001 and 2002, Malaysia, Egypt, India, Brazil, Thailand and others raised concerns at the same committee about a Belgian draft law "aiming at promoting socially responsible production" [27]. I found no specific trade concern in the database targeting the CSDDD or the Forced Labour Regulation by name as of November 2026, so the deforestation case is used here as the closest parallel, not as direct evidence about the forced labour measures.

UNCTAD's 2025 report *The future of sustainable trade* (UNCTAD/DITC/TAB/2024/5) sets out the development concerns most clearly [25]:
- **Trade effects.** Due diligence measures may cause sourcing divergence, export diversion and export segregation.
- **Exclusion and costs.** They may exclude smaller producers, raise compliance costs and erode price premiums.
- **Fixed costs of audits.** Audits and oversight create fixed costs, such as documentation and facilitating site visits, that "are easier for larger operators to cover than for small and medium enterprises, farmers or smallholders". This becomes exclusionary when the ability to pay does not track actual sustainability performance.
- **Recommendations.** UNCTAD recommends harmonising due diligence measures with voluntary sustainability standards, adopting supporting measures for vulnerable actors in developing countries, providing finance for smallholders and fairer value distribution, and including producer-country stakeholders in designing these regimes [25].

Producer governments have a further, sovereignty-based objection. Unilateral measures let importing states and their companies decide labour conditions abroad without the consent of the producing state or the participation of affected workers. The EU Regulation partly responds to this by requiring third-country consent for inspections [3]. That safeguard also gives obstructive states a veto over verification, which the "facts available" rule is meant to counter [3].

## 6. Remediation versus cut-and-run

The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (2011) do not treat exit as the default. The commentary to Principle 19 says the appropriate response depends on the company's leverage, how crucial the relationship is, how severe the abuse is, and whether ending the relationship would itself have adverse human rights consequences. Where a company lacks leverage, it "should consider ending the relationship, taking into account credible assessments of potential adverse human rights impacts of doing so" [23].

The OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct (2018) goes further. It treats disengagement as a last resort: after failed attempts at mitigation, where impacts are irremediable, where there is no reasonable prospect of change, or where severe impacts are not acted on [24]. It also says "a real possibility of disengagement is necessary in many instances for an enterprise's leverage to be effective" [24]. It lists steps for responsible disengagement: comply with national law and ILO standards, set out the escalation process in advance, inform trade unions, and give sufficient notice where feasible [24].

The 2026 CSDDD amendments move EU law towards the remediation end of this spectrum. Suspension replaces termination, and a company need not suspend if doing so would be manifestly more harmful [8]. On one reading, this follows the UNGPs and the Congo evidence [33][34][35]. On another, it weakens the credible threat of exit that the OECD says leverage depends on [24], and it gives companies a ready reason to keep buying. The Malaysian WROs point to a third option. A public authority, not the buyer, imposes the exclusion, and lifting it depends on verified remediation [14]. This puts the incentive to fix the problem on the producer.

## 7. Policy lessons

1. **Make exclusion conditional and reversible.** Bans with a clear route to modification once workers are repaid seem to deliver more for workers than permanent exclusion [14]. The EU Regulation provides for review of decisions [3]. Its design would benefit from explicit remediation criteria, such as repayment of fees and wages.
2. **Match the tool to the type of forced labour.** Private forced labour can be remediated through leverage. State-imposed forced labour cannot, and only state-level engagement with independent monitoring has worked at scale [28][29]. Treating both the same way leads either to ineffective audits or to bans that are only symbolic.
3. **Fund the burden that falls on suppliers.** If compliance costs fall on smallholders and SMEs, due diligence becomes a barrier to entry [25]. Capacity support, shared traceability infrastructure and cost-sharing by buyers should be part of the design, not an afterthought.
4. **Measure workers, not shipments.** Shipment counts [11][12] measure enforcement activity. Regulators should fund baseline and follow-up surveys of workers, like the Thai endline [30] and the Uzbek monitoring [28], for the sectors they target.
5. **Give workers a voice.** The Thai evidence on union rights [30], the La Poste ruling's requirement to build the alert mechanism with unions [22], and the OECD's emphasis on informing unions [24] all point the same way. Worker organisation is both a monitoring tool and a remedy.
6. **Keep promises on implementation.** The Commission missed the deadline for the EU risk database [5]. Germany suspended enforcement before amending its statute [18][19]. The CSDDD timetable has slipped by two years: Directive 2024/1760 set transposition for 26 July 2026 and first application for 26 July 2027, and Directive 2026/470 now sets 26 July 2028 and 26 July 2029 [6][8]. The Commission also missed both the 14 June 2026 database deadline and the same-day guidelines deadline [3][4][5]. These delays undermine the credibility the laws need.

## 8. Ethical risks

- **Harm to the intended beneficiaries.** Boycotts of poor producing regions can raise violence and infant mortality [33][34][35]. Exclusion can push workers into less visible and worse work.
- **Displacement rather than reduction.** Trade measures can move tainted inputs to less scrutinised markets or through third countries without reducing forced labour.
- **Moral licensing.** Compliance paperwork can satisfy regulators while working conditions stay the same. LeBaron's review describes this gap between standards and practice [39].
- **Protectionism under a moral banner.** Recent US releases explicitly link forced labour enforcement to the competitiveness of US industry [14][16]. This feeds producer-country suspicion that labour rights are being used for trade advantage [26][27].
- **Unequal burden.** Fixed compliance costs fall hardest on small producers who may be fully compliant but cannot document it [25].
- **The burden of proof.** The UFLPA presumption makes importers prove a negative under a "clear and convincing" standard [10]. The EU puts the burden on authorities [3]. The first approach risks excluding legitimate producers. The second may leave the ban unenforced where the evidence is controlled by a hostile state.

## 9. Counterarguments

- **"Non-complicity is enough."** One can argue that consumers and states have a duty not to profit from forced labour regardless of whether a ban helps the victims. If so, the Xinjiang measures are justified even without evidence of benefit to workers. The difficulty is that the same reasoning, applied to the DRC, would have justified a policy shown to harm civilians [33][34][35].
- **"Due diligence is too costly and should be scaled back further."** The German and EU rollbacks have been justified by the administrative burden [8][20]. However, the German bill estimates savings of about €4.1 million a year from abolishing reporting [20]. That is small next to the scale of profits from forced labour [2], which suggests the case for rollback rests on more than measured cost.
- **"Rollback ends accountability."** Others argue that the 2026 Omnibus has gutted the CSDDD. In fact, the core prevention and remediation duties remain for the largest companies, member states must still provide access to justice, and fines of up to 3 per cent of turnover are possible [8]. The more serious loss may be in timing and coverage rather than in the substance of the duties.
- **"Bans are the only tool that works against states."** Where the state is the perpetrator, engagement may be impossible, and import bans are among the few levers available. Uzbekistan suggests pressure works best when paired with a credible route to engagement [28][29].

## 10. Open questions

1. How much of the decline in direct Xinjiang-linked US imports reflects real changes in sourcing, and how much reflects processing in third countries that has escaped detection? The prominence of Malaysia, Vietnam and Thailand in UFLPA stops [11] raises the question but does not answer it.
2. Will the EU database, once published, name state-imposed risk areas explicitly, and how will authorities use "facts available" against obstructive states [3][5]?
3. How will member states transposing the amended CSDDD define access to justice without an EU liability regime [8]?
4. Has the LkSG amendment been enacted, and will the reduced enforcement continue under the planned CSDDD implementing law [17][20]?
5. Will French courts move from ordering better vigilance plans to awarding compensation, and will claims in global supply chains survive the admissibility rules set in 2024 [21][22]?
6. Have the Malaysian repayments [14] lasted, and has recruitment-fee debt come back?
7. Can trade rules accommodate forced labour bans? A WTO dispute over the UFLPA or the EU Regulation could test the Article XX(a) public morals defence [40].
8. What would a counterfactual evaluation of a due diligence statute look like? Without one, the debate between "burden" and "accountability" will continue on assertion rather than evidence.
