# Ethical case study

## Is Texidium "a fraud"? Mandatory digital textbook fees in Ontario colleges, including technical and engineering programs

Status date: 18 November 2026. Each factual claim carries a source tag `[S#]` that points to SOURCES.md, where every source has a public link and a note on how it was checked. Items marked **(unverified)** could not be confirmed from a text opened for this study. Items marked **(our reading)** are interpretations, not statements found in a source. When a working paper and a published article differ, the version used is named. Students, faculty members and staff quoted in news reports are not named here. This case is one of three on Ontario's public colleges. The companion cases cover academic senates and full-time faculty staffing.

## 1. The question and the hypothesis

Texidium is a digital textbook platform used by Ontario colleges. Students in some programs have had the cost of digital textbooks added to their fees, sometimes with no way to opt out. Critics have called the arrangement a fraud. Their argument runs like this. Students pay compulsory fees for books they cannot resell, print in full or always keep. Universities, by contrast, are said to let students buy durable hardcover books. And technical and engineering students, who rely on reference tables and problem sets, are said to be served badly by screen-only books.

This case tests that hypothesis. It asks three questions:

1. **Is it fraud in law?** What does "fraud" mean in Canadian criminal and civil law, and does the public evidence meet that standard?
2. **If not fraud, is it unfair?** Does the model harm students in ways that are ethically wrong, even if lawful?
3. **What should change?**

The answer, set out in section 9, is that **the public evidence does not support a finding of fraud by the platform's owner**. It does support serious concerns about mandatory, no-opt-out billing, loss of choice, uneven savings, restrictive licence terms and weak disclosure. Those are problems of product design and college fee policy, and they can be fixed.

## 2. What Texidium is

Texidium is owned by Kivuto Solutions Inc., an Ottawa company. Its terms of service are Kivuto's [S1]. It started as a joint project with Algonquin College. Algonquin invested $450,000 in development and later sold Kivuto two components it had built (a publisher asset library and book adoption technology). In the end, Kivuto took ownership, and Texidium became "a product line under Kivuto" [S2]. A business news report at launch described Kivuto as majority owner with a publisher as minority owner. It said Texidium earned money by charging publishers a fee and that e-texts would cost about half the price of print [S3]. **(unverified)** We could not confirm the date of that report from the page itself, or whether the ownership has changed since.

The platform's own pages describe how it works:

- **Offline access.** Apps for Windows, Mac, iOS and Android let students read downloaded books offline. Syncing needs an internet connection [S4][S5].
- **Disappearing books.** "If a book has disappeared from your Texidium Library, it means that your license for that book has expired or been revoked." A college may revoke access if a student withdraws before the drop deadline. Some colleges give temporary access that requires students to opt in by a deadline. If a student orders "after the opt-in deadline, you will be considered opted-in by default, and you will be charged accordingly". But "You will not lose access to books for which you have already been charged" [S6].
- **Printing.** "Some eTexts do not allow printing. Others impose a limit on the number of pages", and these restrictions "are set by the publisher". Printing works from the desktop apps, not on mobile [S7].
- **Device limits.** Each account can be used on a limited number of devices [S8].
- **Accessibility.** The student page lists text-to-speech, larger fonts and screen-reader compatibility. It says Texidium "lowers textbook costs to a fraction of the cost of print copies" [S5].
- **Institutional billing.** Kivuto's material for institutions says "Fees can be included within student tuition or posted to student accounts", and it supports hybrid models [S9].

**(our reading)** Whether students pay compulsory fees, or can opt out, is decided by each *college*, not by Texidium. The platform supports both. The ethically important choices sit mostly with the colleges.

## 3. How Ontario colleges have used it

### 3.1 Algonquin College: the institutional pay model, 2013–2019

The fullest public account is a study of Algonquin's program by Weber (2018), published by eCampusOntario [S10]. Algonquin began talks with major publishers in 2012–13. It ran a Winter 2013 pilot with another platform for 750 students. In Fall 2013 it launched an **Institutional Pay Model** (IPM), in which digital texts were charged to students as fees [S10]. The program grew to about 32,000 resources for 9,900 students in Fall 2014. In Fall 2015, Algonquin adopted Texidium, delivering about 38,000 resources to 14,000 students. In Fall 2016, it covered about 140 programs, about 35,000 resources and 12,000 students [S10]. The study calls Algonquin "one of the largest inclusive access model institutions in North America" [S10].

How it worked [S10]:

- Students in designated programs paid the eText fee. There was no general opt-out.
- A student who presented a physical copy of the same edition at the campus bookstore, after the 10-day withdrawal period, could get a refund of the fee. The copy was stamped and could be used for an exemption only once a term.
- Students could print pages themselves, using print credits funded by the information technology fee. The allowance was 700 black-and-white pages a term in 2017–18, falling by 200 pages each later year.
- Because the fee rested on the provincial rule for learning materials that are "the property of the student", the college secured perpetual access to the books.

The study's main findings:

- **No achievement gain.** "We have no evidence that the eText/IPM initiative, as implemented, provides a general improvement to student achievement." The study also questioned the original assumption that students lacked books for financial reasons. Some may have been judging "whether or not a resource would be used" [S10].
- **Unclear affordability.** "We cannot conclude that the eText/IPM initiative is uniformly having a positive contribution to affordability of course materials" [S10]. Algonquin advertised "a goal of 50% savings". Actual discounts ranged from about 65% off list price to "no, or almost no discount", depending on the publisher [S10]. The model removed cheaper routes to the same books: library copies, used copies, older editions, online purchase and resale [S10].
- **Unused materials.** Students said they were paying for resources they did not use. The study found no significant difference in resource-use scores between IPM and non-IPM courses. Because the IPM made the purchase compulsory, the authors called this issue "critical" [S10].
- **Debt to the college.** Financial aid staff warned that adding course materials to compulsory fees "may lead to more student encumbrance", money owed to the college that "would negatively impact their ability to graduate" [S10].
- **Usability in problem-solving courses.** Faculty described students solving problems on laptops while also trying to use the e-text, which meant split screens or flipping between the problem in the book and their own solution. Others described the difficulty of flipping back and forth to "consult reference tables". They noted that bookmarking a physical book "is a natural interaction", while e-text navigation "is less intuitive" [S11].
- **Perpetual access was not what students wanted.** Institutional leaders said the perpetual-access requirement kept prices high, and that a rental model "could be lowered by 25 to 75%". Students "seem to be open to alternatives" [S11].
- **Accessibility gains.** The college's accessible learning centre believed the program improved access for students with disabilities. Publisher agreements included accessible formats, and the college could resolve digital-rights-management problems faster [S11].

Student journalism adds detail. One account from the project's fourth year (undated on the page) describes: access codes that worked only for the next semester; codes reported as already used; students told they would have to pay again for a print copy; device incompatibility; and a three-day outage of an ordering database [S12]. The same article reports problems caused by the campus bookstore and by misunderstanding, including a complaint about a $140 charge that turned out to cover three books, not one [S12]. A 2014 newspaper column described a student's complaints about cut-off pages, page numbers that did not match, battery dependence and no resale. That student received refunds for unusable e-texts [S13].

In December 2018, Algonquin announced that the mandatory fee would end in September 2019. A senior official said "it's primarily the students who we're responding to" [S14]. The report notes that, under the model then in place, students "who want to seek out cheaper options for their e-texts are unable to opt out of the compulsory fees" [S14].

### 3.2 Conestoga College

Conestoga's eTexts page says it uses an Institutional Pay Model, that "Participation in this initiative is mandatory", and that students save about 40% compared with print [S15]. The first 100 courses on its published fee list show fees from $32 to $259, with a median of about $104 **(our arithmetic, first page only)** [S15]. A 2020 college news release reported more than 13,700 students in nearly 50 programs, about 35% savings, an estimated $1.4 million saved from fall 2018 to fall 2019, and expansion to nearly 70 programs from fall 2020 [S16]. The campus store says "Students own their eText for life". Most courses used Texidium before Fall 2024 and VitalSource Bookshelf from Fall 2024 [S17].

### 3.3 Other colleges

Seneca Polytechnic has a compulsory program ancillary fee policy [S18]. Its e-textbook page, which reportedly describes opt-out deadlines in some programs, failed to load for us **(unverified)**. A 2026 news report described Practical Nursing students at another college disputing a "mandatory and undisclosed" software and textbook package fee. The college acknowledged that "the initial communication regarding this fee could have been clearer and more timely", gave one cohort a one-time credit and declined to expand compensation [S19]. **The article does not name a platform, so it is not evidence about Texidium.** It does show the disclosure problem that compulsory bundled fees can create.

We found **no public, system-wide list** of which Ontario college programs, including engineering technology programs, currently use Texidium. Texidium's student page carries a testimonial from a Computer Engineering Technology student at Algonquin [S5]. That shows the platform was used in at least one engineering technology program. It does not show how common that is **(unverified)**.

## 4. The fee rules

Ontario's Minister's Binding Policy Directive on tuition and ancillary fees for colleges (August 2024, covering 2023–24 to 2026–27) sets the rules [S20]:

- "Program-related ancillary fees are outside the scope of the framework and remain allowable compulsory ancillary fees for students registered in applicable programs."
- Other non-essential fees must be optional, with an online opt-out "at the time of billing".
- "Fees for digital learning materials that are the property of the student, and which can include test/assessment tools, may be increased without observing the protocol" that otherwise requires agreement with student representatives.
- Where a course "relies substantially on assessments that are included with a learning resource, such as an online textbook", colleges should have a policy, which "could include a rebate" of part of the fee. Colleges "should be proactive in monitoring conditions attached to the use of learning resources".
- All ancillary fees "are to be adequately explained to students".
- Withdrawal within 10 business days brings a refund of fees less $500.

Algonquin's fee policy similarly lists learning materials "retained by the student" among ancillary fees [S21].

**Do universities simply use hardcovers?** No. The University of Toronto's provostial guidelines (2020) note that the Ministry once prohibited compulsory fees for digital learning materials. In December 2013, it allowed such fees in the university sector too. Use of digital materials "has increased in recent years as an alternative to physical textbooks" [S22]. **(our reading)** The claim that universities use hardcovers while colleges impose digital fees is not accurate as a general statement. The legal permission is similar in both sectors. The difference lies in how widely colleges have adopted *mandatory, program-wide* billing.

**"Property of the student" and licences.** **(our reading)** There is a tension here. The rule allows compulsory fees for digital materials that are the student's *property*. The platform describes what students hold as a *licence* that can expire or be revoked, though it says access is not lost for books already charged [S6]. Algonquin responded by securing perpetual access [S10], and Conestoga's store says students "own their eText for life" [S17]. Whether a lifetime licence counts as "property" under the directive is a question for the Ministry. Students cannot resell or lend these books, as they could a physical book. A college that bills a compulsory fee for a time-limited licence may be outside the spirit of the rule.

## 5. Evidence from elsewhere

### 5.1 United States: "inclusive access" and automatic billing

In the United States, similar programs are called "inclusive access" or automatic textbook billing. The US Public Interest Research Group's 2020 study obtained contracts from 31 of 52 public institutions it asked [S23]. It found that 54% of publisher contracts promised a blanket discount off national list price, which students cannot easily check. It found that 22% limited sales of new print copies, typically to 15% of a course. It also documented high participation quotas, discounts that could disappear if quotas were missed, and restrictions on publicity [S23]. It recommended opt-in billing, capping annual price increases at inflation, eliminating quotas and ending limits on print copies [S23]. This is an advocacy organization's study, but it is based on contracts it obtained and published.

In December 2024, the US Department of Education ended a negotiated rulemaking on cash management. Its notice said the proposal on textbook billing "generated significant discussion". It noted concerns that such programs "may not always deliver costs to students that are below market rates or may not give students a true opportunity to opt out" [S24]. It reminded institutions that existing rules require an opt-out policy that is "clearly communicated and actionable" [S24][S25].

### 5.2 Student outcomes under inclusive access

- A Tennessee pilot across 13 community colleges and 141 courses found "no significant differences in either overall or population-specific academic outcomes" compared with the two prior falls. The author warned about "hidden costs, the need for perpetual access, and the repercussions to student debt from deferring college expenses" (Spica, 2021 online, 2023 issue) [S26].
- A technical college study of 7,110 students across six courses found significant before-and-after differences in five of nine outcome categories (Moore and Piazza, 2023) [S27]. The abstract does not say in which direction each difference ran, so we do not claim it showed improvement.
- Algonquin's own study found no general achievement gain [S10].

### 5.3 Free open textbooks

Research on open educational resources (OER), which are free and openly licensed, gives a benchmark:

- Hilton (2016) reviewed 16 studies. He found that students "generally achieve the same learning outcomes when OER are utilized and simultaneously save significant amounts of money" [S28].
- Hilton (2020 issue; 2019 online) reviewed 16 efficacy studies and 20 perception studies covering 121,168 students or faculty. Students "achieve the same or better learning outcomes" with OER [S29].
- Clinton and Khan (2019) meta-analyzed 22 studies. They found no difference in learning between open and commercial textbooks (g = 0.01), but withdrawal rates were significantly lower with open textbooks (odds ratio 0.71) [S30].
- Fischer, Hilton, Robinson and Wiley (2015) compared 4,909 students using OER with a matched control pool. They found "statistically significant differences between groups, with most favoring students utilizing OER" [S31].
- At Houston Community College, a zero-cost textbook program had "a statistically significant positive effect on pass rates for all students, but no effect on completion" (Smith, Grimaldi and Basu Mallick, 2020) [S32].
- A British Columbia survey of 320 students in courses using an open textbook examined purchasing behaviour, the effects of textbook costs and students' views of open textbooks (Jhangiani and Jhangiani, 2017) [S33].

Ontario has a public open textbook library run by eCampusOntario [S34].

### 5.4 Reading on screens versus paper

This matters for the claim about engineering programs.

- Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman and Salmerón (2018) meta-analyzed 54 studies with 171,055 participants. They found an advantage for paper over digital reading (g = −0.21). The paper advantage was larger under time pressure and held for informational texts but not for narrative texts only [S35].
- Clinton (2019) found that reading from screens had a negative effect on performance relative to paper (g = −0.25). This "may have been limited to expository texts" (g = −0.32), and readers judged their own understanding more accurately on paper [S36].
- Kong, Seo and Zhai (2018) meta-analyzed 17 studies and found better comprehension on paper. They found no significant difference in reading speed **(from the published abstract as summarized by an index; full text not read)** [S37].

**(our reading)** These effects are modest, and most of the studies test short reading tasks, not semester-long use of a technical textbook. They do fit the faculty reports at Algonquin about problem-solving and reference tables [S11]. Engineering and technology students mostly read *expository* text under time pressure, which is where the paper advantage appears. A model that makes print hard to get, through page limits, extra cost or a mandatory digital fee, may therefore cost these students more in learning than it costs students in other fields. This has not been tested in an Ontario college engineering program **(unverified)**.

## 6. Is it fraud? The legal test

### 6.1 Criminal fraud

Section 380 of the Criminal Code makes it an offence to defraud "by deceit, falsehood or other fraudulent means" [S38]. In *R. v. Théroux* (1993), the Supreme Court of Canada set the test [S39]. The prosecution must prove a dishonest act (deceit, a lie, or another fraudulent act that a reasonable person would see as dishonest) and actual deprivation or risk of deprivation. It must also prove that the accused knew what they were doing and knew that deprivation could result. The Court said: "The requirement of intentional fraudulent action excludes mere negligent misrepresentation. It also excludes improvident business conduct or conduct which is sharp in the sense of taking advantage of a business opportunity to the detriment of someone less astute." And "Neither a negligent misstatement, nor a sharp business practice, will suffice" [S39][S40].

### 6.2 Civil fraud

In *Bruno Appliance and Furniture, Inc. v. Hryniak* (2014), the Court summarized the four elements of civil fraud: "(1) a false representation made by the defendant; (2) some level of knowledge of the falsehood of the representation on the part of the defendant (whether through knowledge or recklessness); (3) the false representation caused the plaintiff to act; and (4) the plaintiff's actions resulted in a loss" [S41].

### 6.3 Applying the tests

| Element | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| A false statement or dishonest act | The platform's pages disclose licence expiry and revocation, print limits and device limits [S6][S7][S8]. Its "fraction of the cost of print" claim [S5] is vague but matched by savings in many cases [S10][S16]. The clearest overstatement on record is a *college's* advertised 50% savings goal, when some books had almost no discount [S10]. That is a statement of a goal, not proof of a knowing lie. |
| Knowledge or recklessness | We found no evidence that Kivuto knew any claim it made was false. |
| Inducement | Under mandatory billing, students do not choose to buy at all, so they were not *induced* by a vendor's statement. The college's fee decision drove the purchase. |
| Loss | Some students lost money compared with used or library copies [S10]. Others saved money [S10][S16]. |

**(our reading)** On the public record, neither the criminal nor the civil test is met against the platform's owner. The harms that are documented are the kind the Supreme Court excluded from fraud: business arrangements that favour one side, unclear communication, and technical failures. Calling the model "a fraud" in public could itself expose a speaker to a defamation claim. It is also less useful than the accurate criticism, because it invites a debate about intent instead of a debate about fees, choice and licence terms.

**What would change this conclusion?** Evidence that a vendor or college *knowingly* misstated prices, ownership or access terms to induce payment. Or evidence of charges for books that were never delivered, made knowingly and not corrected. A college that billed a compulsory fee as "property of the student" while knowing that students received only a revocable, time-limited licence could also face a misrepresentation or regulatory complaint. We found no evidence that this happened. It would be worth checking at each college.

## 7. If not fraud, is it unfair?

### 7.1 Arguments that the model is unfair

- **Compulsion without choice.** A mandatory fee with no opt-out takes away the student's choice to borrow, buy used, buy an older edition or share [S10][S14]. The provincial rule for most non-essential fees is an online opt-out [S20]. Digital learning materials avoid that rule by being classed as program-related or the student's property [S20].
- **Savings are uneven and hard to check.** Discounts ran from about 65% to almost nothing [S10]. Blanket "percent off list" claims are hard for students to verify [S23]. A student who would have borrowed a library copy saved nothing.
- **Paying for unused materials.** Students said some required resources were barely used. Under a mandatory model, that is a pure cost to the student [S10].
- **Lost resale value.** A used print book has resale value. A licence does not [S10].
- **Debt and graduation.** Fees on the student account can turn into debt that blocks graduation [S10].
- **Learning costs for technical students.** Reading research favours paper for expository text under time pressure [S35][S36], and faculty reported specific usability problems in problem-solving and reference-heavy work [S11]. Print limits set by publishers [S7] and per-term print allowances [S10] make the problem worse.
- **Conflicts of interest.** The college that set the mandatory fee had also invested in developing the platform [S2]. Whatever the motives, that arrangement needs stronger transparency than an ordinary purchase.

### 7.2 Fair counterarguments

- **Day-one access.** Under mandatory billing, every student has the book on the first day. Students who could not afford the upfront cost no longer go without, and the fee can be deferred like tuition [S10]. This is a real benefit for low-income students.
- **Lower prices for many.** Many students did save money [S10][S16]. Pooled purchasing gives a college more bargaining power than individual students have.
- **Accessibility.** Students with disabilities got accessible formats faster [S11].
- **Convenience.** Students and faculty liked access on several devices and not carrying heavy books [S11].
- **No outcome harm in some studies.** Inclusive access studies have found no significant differences in academic outcomes [S26], and some found changes in several categories [S27].
- **Perpetual access.** Students in licensed professions may want to keep materials for later exams [S11].

### 7.3 Weighing

**(our reading)** The benefits are real, but nearly all of them survive an **opt-out**. A student who wants day-one access, the discount and the accessible format can stay in. A student who has a used copy, uses the library, or needs a print copy for technical work can leave. The case for *mandatory* participation rests mainly on the bulk price: publishers offer lower prices in exchange for guaranteed sales. That is a trade-off between students. Some pay for books they do not need so that others can pay less. Colleges should make that trade-off openly, with student consent, rather than through a fee classification. Algonquin's decision to end its mandatory model after student opposition [S14], and the US regulator's insistence on an actionable opt-out [S24], both point the same way.

## 8. Recommendations

These proposals come from this study. They are not drawn from any cited source.

**For the Ministry**

1. **Opt-out by default for digital materials.** Amend the directive so that fees for commercial digital learning materials are opt-out, with an online opt-out at the time of billing and a full refund, as for other non-essential fees [S20]. Allow a compulsory fee only where the materials are open or free, or where students have approved it through the existing protocol.
2. **Define "property of the student".** State that a licence can qualify only if access is perpetual, cannot be revoked once paid, allows printing of a reasonable share and works offline on common devices. Otherwise the fee is not a property fee.
3. **Disclosure.** Before the offer-acceptance deadline, colleges must publish for each course the fee, the print list price, the platform's terms (printing, devices, duration) and a statement that cheaper legal options may exist [S20].
4. **Assessment rebate.** Enforce the existing expectation of a rebate where bundled assessments are a substantial part of the mark [S20].
5. **Fund open textbooks.** Expand eCampusOntario's open library [S34] in high-enrolment technical courses.

**For colleges**

6. **End no-opt-out programs** and offer a print or print-on-demand option at cost for technical, engineering and reference-heavy courses.
7. **Publish annual results** for each program: participation, opt-out rates, actual savings compared with the cheapest new print copy, and unused-material complaints.
8. **Contract terms.** Refuse contract clauses that limit print sales or set participation quotas, and cap annual price increases [S23]. Require vendors to publish their fee structures.
9. **Governance.** Put textbook fee decisions before a body with student and faculty members, and disclose any financial interest the college has in a platform. The collective agreement already makes "selecting or approving textbooks and learning materials" a professor's duty [S42]. Faculty should be able to choose open or print materials without losing access to a negotiated price.

**For the vendor**

10. **Make terms plain at the point of purchase.** Show the licence duration, print limit, device limit and refund rules before any charge. Do not use opt-in-by-default ordering after deadlines without clear notice [S6].
11. **Remove publisher print limits where the college has paid for perpetual access**, or show those limits clearly before purchase.

**For students and student associations**

12. Ask for the college's per-course fee and savings data. Use the directive's disclosure and opt-out rules. Raise complaints with the college, the student association and the Ministry rather than through public allegations of fraud, which the evidence does not support.

## 9. Verdict

**The public evidence does not support calling Texidium a fraud.** There is no evidence of a knowing falsehood by the platform's owner that induced students to pay and caused them loss. The Supreme Court has said that sharp business practice and careless statements are not fraud. **The evidence does support a strong ethical criticism of mandatory, no-opt-out digital textbook billing** as some Ontario colleges have used it, often on Texidium. It removed student choice, delivered uneven savings, could load students with unused materials and debt, and used licence terms that sit uneasily with a fee rule based on "property of the student". For technical and engineering students, reading research and faculty reports add a plausible learning cost, though no Ontario study has tested it. The remedy is an opt-out by default, clear licence terms, real print options and open textbooks. It is not a fraud allegation.

## 10. Unverified or uncertain items

- **Current use.** We found no public list of which Ontario college programs use Texidium now, including engineering technology programs. One testimonial shows past use in at least one such program [S5].
- **Ownership and pricing model.** The report of a publisher minority ownership and a publisher-fee revenue model is from a launch-era business news report whose date we could not confirm [S3]. Current ownership was not checked.
- **Student newspaper accounts.** One article is undated [S12]. News accounts show individual experiences, not system rates.
- **The Algonquin 2018 announcement date.** The year (2018) is inferred from the article's statement that the model ends in September 2019 [S14].
- **Seneca.** Its e-textbook page did not load, so we do not describe its opt-out terms [S18].
- **Canadore-type disputes.** The 2026 nursing fee dispute does not name a platform [S19].
- **Moore and Piazza (2023).** The abstract does not give the direction of each significant difference [S27].
- **Kong et al. (2018).** We relied on an index summary of the abstract [S37].
- **Engineering-specific learning effects.** We found no study in an Ontario college engineering program comparing print and digital textbooks.
- **Defamation risk.** This is a general caution, not legal advice.

## 11. What this does not replace

This is an ethics case study based on public documents. It is not legal advice, and it is not a finding of fact about any person or company. It does not decide whether any college has complied with the Ministry's directive. That is for the Ministry, the colleges and, if necessary, the courts. It does not replace a review of the actual contracts between colleges, Kivuto and publishers, which are not public. Anyone considering a complaint should obtain legal advice and the relevant college's fee and contract documents.
