# Is populism, used as an election tactic, eroding Western democracy? The evidence for and against a hypothesis

*Ethics case-study source brief 14. Anonymous author. Prepared November 2026. Policy level only: this brief deals with definitions, datasets, published research and institutional reports, not individual cases. It takes no party side and treats left and right populism by the same standard. Every factual claim is tied to a numbered source in the list at the end. Items marked **UNVERIFIED** could not be confirmed against a text read live for this brief.*

**Source labels used throughout:**
- **[PR]** peer-reviewed journal article
- **[BOOK]** scholarly book or book chapter (not journal peer review)
- **[RI]** academic research-institute report using expert-coded data (not peer-reviewed as a report)
- **[NGO]** NGO report; advocacy-oriented, not peer-reviewed
- **[TT]** think-tank paper, not peer-reviewed
- **[INT]** intergovernmental organisation report
- **[WP]** working paper or preprint copy of a later journal article

## The hypothesis

> **H:** In Western democracies (the United States, Canada and Europe), populism used as an electoral strategy, meaning a claim to speak for a virtuous "people" against a corrupt "elite", is eroding democracy: weakening the institutions, norms and rights that make elections meaningful.

This brief treats H as a hypothesis to be tested, not a conclusion. It sets out the strongest evidence-based case for H, then the strongest case against it, and ends with what the evidence says about strengthening democracy without suppressing voters.

## Read this first: four reasons the question is hard to settle

1. **"Populism" is defined in competing ways** (Section 1). Which leaders and parties count as populist changes the results [1][2][3][21c].
2. **Most effect estimates concern populists *in executive office*,** not populism *as a campaign tactic*. The best-known datasets are global and include many non-Western cases [8][9]. Evidence specific to the US, Canada and Western Europe is thinner.
3. **"Democratic erosion" is mostly measured by expert coding** (V-Dem, Freedom House) [10][11]. A peer-reviewed critique finds that objective indicators show little global decline over the past decade, and raises the possibility of coder bias [22].
4. **Selection and reverse causation.** Populists tend to win after crises and institutional failures [8][17]. Separating the effect of populist rule from the conditions that brought it about is difficult. The strongest studies use synthetic controls or shift-share instruments [8][14][15], and even these leave room for doubt.

## 1. Definitions: three competing scholarly conceptions

**(a) Ideational: populism as a "thin-centred ideology" (Mudde).**
- Mudde defines populism as "an ideology that considers society to be ultimately separated into two homogeneous and antagonistic groups, 'the pure people' versus 'the corrupt elite', and which argues that politics should be an expression of the volonté générale (general will) of the people" [1] [PR].
- Because it is only a "thin-centred ideology", it "can be easily combined with very different (thin and full) other ideologies, including communism, ecologism, nationalism or socialism" [1]. That is why left and right populism both exist under this definition.
- Its opposites are "elitism and pluralism" [1].
- Mudde argued that populist discourse had already "become mainstream in the politics of western democracies", a "populist Zeitgeist". He also argued that common explanations of and reactions to it "might actually strengthen rather than weaken it" [1].

**(b) Anti-pluralist: populism as a moral monopoly of representation (Müller).**
- Müller argues that "at populism's core is a rejection of pluralism". Populists "will always claim that they and they alone represent the people and their true interests" [2] [BOOK].
- On this account, criticism of elites is necessary but not sufficient. What makes someone a populist is the claim to *exclusive moral* representation, which treats opponents as illegitimate [2].
- The publisher's summary states the book's thesis that "if populists have enough power, they will end up creating an authoritarian state that excludes all those not considered part of the proper 'people'" [2].
- This definition builds danger to democracy into the concept. Critics of H can therefore argue that it partly assumes what H asserts (see Section 4).

**(c) Discursive and left-populist: populism as legitimate democratic mobilisation (Laclau and Mouffe).**
- Laclau's *On Populist Reason* treats populism as a discourse that *constructs* "the people" through antagonism, equivalence and "empty signifiers". In this theory, constructing a people is central to politics in general [3] [BOOK].
- Mouffe, following Laclau, describes populism as a strategy of building a political frontier that mobilises the "underdog" against "those in power". She argues that a *left* populism "provides the adequate strategy to recover and deepen the ideals of equality and popular sovereignty that are constitutive of a democratic politics" [3] [BOOK]. She openly describes the book as a "political intervention" that "acknowledges its partisan nature" [3].
- The critical literature on this approach raises issues of "formalism and crypto-normativity", the relation between populism and institutions, and "verticality and homogeneity" [3c] [BOOK].

**(d) Populism as an ambivalent force (Canovan; Rovira Kaltwasser).**
- Canovan argues that populism "should not be dismissed as a pathological form of politics". It arises from an "inescapable tension" between democracy's "pragmatic" and "redemptive" faces, which makes it "a perennial possibility" [4] [PR].
- Rovira Kaltwasser argues that populism is best seen as "an ambivalence" that "may constitute a threat to or a corrective for democracy". In Robert Dahl's terms, it "might well represent a democratic corrective in terms of inclusiveness" while becoming "a democratic threat concerning public contestation" [5] [PR].

**Why it matters.** Under (a), populism is morally neutral and its effect is an empirical question. Under (b), populism is anti-pluralist by definition. Under (c), it can be a healthy part of democracy. The strength of the case for H depends heavily on which definition a study uses.

## 2. The strongest evidence-based case FOR the hypothesis

### 2.1 Effects of populists in power on democratic institutions
- **Kyle and Mounk (Tony Blair Institute).** The paper uses a database of 46 populist leaders or parties that held executive office in 33 democracies from 1990 to 2018 [9] [TT]. It reports that:
  - "populist rule—whether from the right or the left—has a highly negative effect on political systems";
  - "23 per cent of populists cause significant democratic backsliding, compared with 6 per cent of non-populist democratically elected leaders";
  - populists hold office on average twice as long as non-populists (six and a half versus three years);
  - "Over 50 per cent of populist leaders amend or rewrite their countries' constitutions";
  - "40 per cent of populist leaders are indicted on corruption charges" [9].
  - The definition used includes "bulldozing over political and civil-society institutions in the name of enacting the popular will" [9]. That partly builds institutional damage into who counts as populist (see Section 4.3).
- **Ruth-Lovell and Grahn.** The study merges data on populist governments in Europe and Latin America from 1995 with V-Dem data. It finds "a rather negative impact of populism on the electoral, liberal and deliberative models of democracy" [6] [PR].
- **Juon and Bochsler.** This study covers 53 established democracies from 1990 to 2016. It finds "a negative impact of populists across the spectrum on institutional safeguards, such as the rule of law and state transparency" [7] [PR]. The same study also finds benefits (Section 3.2).
- **Left and right compared within Europe.** Huber and Schimpf study 30 European countries from 1990 to 2012. They find that left- and right-wing populist parties differ in their association with minority rights, and are more similar in their association with "mutual constraints" (checks and balances). They conclude that "populism should not be considered in isolation from its host ideology" [20] [PR]. The direction of each difference is not given in the abstract and is **UNVERIFIED** here (full text not read).

### 2.2 Economic costs of populist government
- Funke, Schularick and Trebesch identify 51 populist presidents and prime ministers from 1900 to 2020 in 60 countries [8] [PR]. They find:
  - "After 15 years, GDP per capita is 10 percent lower compared to a plausible nonpopulist counterfactual" (synthetic-control comparison);
  - "economic disintegration, decreasing macroeconomic stability, and the erosion of institutions typically go hand in hand with populist rule", and checks and balances "erode, as do judicial and press freedoms" [8];
  - the decline "is primarily driven by left-wing populists that emphasize distributional and social issues, but it is also observable for right-wing populists" [8];
  - populism is "serial": countries with a populist leader in their history are more likely to have another [8].
- **Limit:** the sample is global and long-run, and the authors state that "no perfect strategy exists" for causal estimation [8]. Western cases are a minority of the sample.

### 2.3 Aggregate backsliding trends (expert-coded)
- **V-Dem Democracy Report 2026** [10] [RI]:
  - "The level of democracy for the average citizen in Western Europe and North America is at its lowest level in over 50 years, primarily due to ongoing autocratization in the USA."
  - Italy, the UK and the USA are new additions to the region's autocratisers, alongside Greece. Among the ten new autocratisers in 2025 are five European countries: Croatia, Italy, Slovakia, Slovenia and the UK [10].
  - It reports that the USA's Liberal Democracy Index fell from 0.79 (2023) to 0.57 (2025). It also states that "Electoral components of democracy remain stable" there, with election indicators based on the 2024 elections [10].
  - It attributes Slovakia's decline to "a nationalist-populist coalition" undermining judicial independence and controlling media [10].
- **Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026** [11] [NGO]:
  - "Global freedom declined for the 20th consecutive year in 2025."
  - "Among countries rated Free, the United States, Bulgaria, and Italy have experienced the year's largest declines." The United States' net decline since 2005 is 12 points [11].
  - It names "attacks on democratic institutions by elected leaders" among the main drivers of deterioration over 20 years [11].
- These reports describe trends, not populism's causal effect. Their partial attribution to populist governments is the organisations' own judgement.

### 2.4 Norm erosion and cultural backlash
- **Levitsky and Ziblatt** argue that democracies "work best—and survive longer—where constitutions are reinforced by unwritten democratic norms" [12] [BOOK]. They identify two norms:
  - "mutual toleration" (accepting rivals as legitimate);
  - "forbearance" (restraint in using institutional powers).
- They propose an "essential test": whether parties keep would-be authoritarians "from gaining power in the first place" by refusing to nominate or align with them [12]. They trace US norm erosion to the 1980s–1990s and root it in "extreme partisan polarization" [12]. Their partisan attribution of that erosion is their own argument; this brief does not adjudicate it.
- **Norris and Inglehart** propose a general theory: a "silent revolution in values" triggered "a backlash fuelling support for authoritarian-populist parties and leaders in the US and Europe" [13] [BOOK]. They note that "even small parties can still shift the policy agenda", citing a minor party's role in catalysing Brexit [13]. They warn of "risks to liberal democracy" [13].

### 2.5 Drivers that make populist tactics electorally effective
- **Trade shocks (US).** Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Majlesi find "strong though not definitive evidence of an ideological realignment in trade-exposed local labor markets" [14] [PR]. China-trade-exposed areas showed:
  - "growing ideological polarization", meaning more support for both strong-left and strong-right views;
  - "pure rightward shifts in others";
  - gains at "the expense of moderate Democrats" [14].
- **Trade shocks (Western Europe).** Colantone and Stanig cover 15 Western European countries from 1988 to 2007. They find that a stronger Chinese import shock leads to more support "for nationalist and isolationist parties" and "radical-right parties" [15] [PR].
- **Austerity.** Fetzer finds that exposure to UK welfare reforms after 2010 is associated with rising UKIP support and Leave support. He suggests "the EU referendum could have resulted in a Remain victory had it not been for austerity" [16] [PR].
- **Synthesis.** Guriev and Papaioannou's review covers:
  - trade and automation;
  - the 2008–09 financial crisis and austerity;
  - identity, trust and cultural backlash;
  - immigration, including "the gap between perceptions and reality regarding immigration";
  - the internet and social media [17] [PR, review].
- **Media and social media.** Guriev, Melnikov and Zhuravskaya study 116 countries [18b] [PR]. They find that 3G mobile internet reduces government approval and raises perceived corruption, and that "in Europe, the expansion of mobile internet increased vote shares of anti-establishment populist parties". The working-paper version reports that moving from zero to full 3G coverage raises right-wing populist vote share by 6.9 and left-wing by 5.9 percentage points. These figures were read via a search-index copy of the working paper; that the published figures are identical is **UNVERIFIED**.

## 3. The strongest counterarguments AGAINST the hypothesis

### 3.1 Populism as a democratic corrective
- Canovan argues that populism expresses a real tension inside democracy itself and is "a perennial possibility", not a pathology [4] [PR].
- Rovira Kaltwasser argues that it can be "a democratic corrective in terms of inclusiveness" [5] [PR].
- Mudde, whose definition most empirical work uses, writes that populism "draws its strength from the confused and often opportunistic democratic promises of the political elites" [1]. He also writes that, in its extreme majoritarian form, it rejects limits such as minority protection and independent courts [1].
- Laclau and Mouffe go further and defend left populism as a way to deepen equality and popular sovereignty [3] [BOOK].

### 3.2 Mixed and null empirical findings
- **Participation gains.** Juon and Bochsler find that populist actors "increase participation and representation, especially if they are confined to the opposition and especially if they belong to the political left-wing" [7] [PR]. This bears directly on populism *as an election tactic* (in opposition) versus *in power*. The harm they find concentrates on institutional safeguards when populists govern [7].
- **Host ideology matters.** Huber and Schimpf conclude that populism's association with democratic quality depends on its left or right host ideology [20] [PR]. That cuts against a single "populism erodes democracy" claim.
- **Measurement dispute.** Little and Meng [22] [PR]:
  - find that objective indicators (for example, incumbent performance in elections) show "little evidence of global democratic decline during the past decade";
  - attribute the gap with expert-coded indices partly to possible "coder bias" or to "more subtle undemocratic action";
  - say they "cannot rule out" decline that only subjective judgement can detect, but that the global-decline claim "is not justified by existing evidence".
- **Social media effects are contested.** Allcott and co-authors find that deactivating Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 US midterms "reduced both factual news knowledge and political polarization" [19a] [PR]. Guess and co-authors randomised users to chronological rather than algorithmic feeds during the 2020 US election. The change "did not significantly alter levels of issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes" over three months [19b] [PR]. Zhuravskaya, Petrova and Enikolopov's review lists open questions about how the internet shapes politics in democracies [18a] [PR, review].
- **Resilience.** Freedom House reports that of 87 countries rated Free in 2005, 76 (over 85 percent) "have remained Free throughout the two-decade period". It also reports that democracies remain "capable of course correction" [11] [NGO].

### 3.3 Definitional stretching
- Hunger and Paxton reviewed 884 abstracts (2004–2018) and 50 articles in depth. They find "a common conflation of populism with other ideologies", which leaves populism itself analytically neglected [21b] [PR].
- Rooduijn warns that confusing populism with "nativism" and "Euroscepticism" brings "the risk of sloppy conceptualisation, and, as a result, invalid inferences" [21a] [PR].
- **Implication for H.** Harms attributed to "populism" may come from nativism, authoritarianism or economic policy choices that happen to travel with it.
  - Some datasets define populism partly by institutional "bulldozing" [9].
  - Some definitions build anti-pluralism into the concept [2].
  - Either choice makes a populism–erosion link partly true by definition.
- Müller himself notes that if criticism of elites were all there is to populism, almost any challenger would qualify [2]. That shows how broad definitions can lose discriminating power.

### 3.4 Populism responds to elite and institutional failures
- Funke et al. find that "many populists enter office in the aftermath of a macroeconomic crisis or recession" [8] [PR].
- The trade-shock and austerity studies [14][15][16] show that policy choices by established governments (trade-adjustment and fiscal decisions) preceded populist gains.
- On this view, populism is partly a *symptom* of representational failure, and suppressing it would not fix the cause [1][4][17].

### 3.5 Breadth: the evidence is uneven across the West
- **United States.** The strongest causal driver evidence is for trade shocks [14] and social media [19]. Expert-coded erosion indices report sharp recent decline [10][11]. These indices are disputed on measurement [22].
- **Canada.** Budd argues that a 2018 Ontario provincial populist campaign is better understood within a Canadian tradition of neoliberal, taxpayer-centred populism. He finds it "avoided the nativist and xenophobic rhetoric" of US and Western European right-populists [23] [PR]. This suggests that populist *tactics* need not carry the exclusionary content seen elsewhere. No national-level Canadian estimate of populism's effect on democratic quality was found for this brief; this is a gap.
- **Europe.** This is the richest evidence base [6][7][15][18b][20]. It shows both left and right populist vote gains from common shocks [18b], and different democratic profiles by host ideology [7][20].
- **Left populism in the US and Canada at national executive level.** No study reviewed here estimates its effect. Claims either way are **UNVERIFIED**.

## KEY FINDINGS

1. **Populists *in executive office* are associated with weaker institutional safeguards.** This holds across several independent datasets and methods [6][7][8][9], and for left and right alike, though not identically [8][9][20].
2. **Populist government carries large average economic costs**: about 10% lower GDP per capita after 15 years in a long-run global sample. Left-wing populists drive more of the cost, and right-wing populists show it too [8].
3. **Populism *as an election tactic in opposition* can raise participation and representation**, especially on the left [7]. The evidence for H is therefore much stronger for populism in power than for populism as a campaign tactic.
4. **Economic shocks (trade, austerity) and mobile internet causally raise support for populist and radical parties** [14][15][16][18b]. Social media's effect on polarisation is contested [19a][19b].
5. **Expert-coded indices show recent decline in Western democracy, led by the US** [10][11]. A peer-reviewed critique finds that objective indicators do not show global decline [22], and the same indices also record resilience [11].
6. **Conceptual conflation is a real threat to inference**: much research conflates populism with its host ideologies [21a][21b].

## VERDICT ON THE HYPOTHESIS

- **Partly supported, in a narrower form.** The evidence best supports this version: *populist leaders who gain executive power tend, on average, to weaken checks and balances, the rule of law and press and judicial freedoms, and to impose economic costs* [6][7][8][9].
- **Weakly supported or unsupported:** the broader claim that populism *as an electoral tactic* is in itself eroding Western democracy.
  - In opposition, populists are associated with *higher* participation and representation [7].
  - Effects differ by host ideology [20].
  - Aggregate erosion measures are disputed [22].
  - Much of the effect may reflect nativism, authoritarianism, crises or elite failures rather than the populist element itself [8][17][21].

## WHAT THE EVIDENCE CANNOT SETTLE

- **Whether "populism" or its host ideology does the damage.** Studies that separate them disagree about how much the populist element alone matters [7][20][21b].
- **Whether expert-coded or objective indicators better capture erosion.** Neither side can rule out the other's explanation [22].
- **Western-specific causal effects of populists in power.** Leading datasets are global and include many Latin American and Central/Eastern European cases [8][9]. Western executive cases are few.
- **Long-run net effects of the corrective function.** Whether incorporating neglected voters [4][5][7] outweighs institutional costs [6][7] over decades is a normative weighing as much as an empirical one.
- **Partisan attribution in specific countries.** Who bears responsibility for norm erosion in a given country [12] is contested political ground, and this brief does not adjudicate it.

## 4. What works to strengthen democracy against harmful tactics WITHOUT suppressing voters (ranked by strength of evidence)

**Tier 1: randomised, preregistered evidence (individual level; durability limited)**
1. **Inoculation against manipulation techniques.**
   - Short videos teaching recognition of "emotionally manipulative language, incoherence, false dichotomies, scapegoating, and ad hominem attacks" improved discernment and sharing decisions in six randomised studies (n = 6,464) and a YouTube field study (n = 22,632) [24b] [PR].
   - The effects were "robust across the political spectrum" [24b].
   - This works on tactics, not on parties or voters.
2. **Correcting misperceptions of rival partisans and stressing shared identities.**
   - A 32,059-person US megastudy found that treatments correcting "misperceptions of rival partisans' views" or "highlighting the threat of democratic collapse" reduced support for undemocratic practices [24a] [PR].
   - There was "limited evidence for durable effects for antidemocratic attitudes" after about two weeks [24a] (preprint copy).
   - The authors conclude that "durable effects likely require structural and/or institutional interventions" [24a].

**Tier 2: quasi-experimental and observational evidence on what backfires or fails**
3. **Do not rely on prosecuting populist politicians for speech as an electoral remedy.** In a Dutch panel, an unexpected decision to prosecute a populist MP "substantially enhanced his party's appeal" by 1–5 points among some voters [25a] [PR]. Legal accountability for real crimes is a separate matter; the evidence concerns electoral effects.
4. **Do not expect mainstream adoption of radical positions ("accommodation") to shrink radical-right support.** Krause, Cohen and Abou-Chadi "do not find any evidence that accommodative strategies reduce radical right support". If anything, accommodation increased defections to the radical right [25b] [PR].
5. **Address the economic drivers.** Trade and austerity shocks causally raised populist and radical-right support [14][15][16]. This implies that cushioning concentrated economic losses could reduce the demand that populist tactics feed on. *This is an inference from driver studies; no source reviewed directly tests whether compensation lowers populist vote shares, so it is **UNVERIFIED** as an intervention.*

**Tier 3: theory, comparative history and practice guidance (weaker causal evidence)**
6. **Party gatekeeping and norms of forbearance.** Parties can decline to nominate or ally with would-be authoritarians, and form cross-party coalitions when needed [12] [BOOK]. This works through party choices, not voter exclusion. The supporting evidence is historical comparison, not causal estimation.
7. **Protect institutional safeguards specifically.** The documented harm concentrates on the rule of law, transparency, judicial and press freedoms [7][8][10]. Safeguards that operate regardless of who wins (court independence, transparency rules, legislative constraints) target the harm without restricting who may run or vote. Direct causal evidence on which safeguards hold best is not in the sources reviewed and is **UNVERIFIED**.
8. **Representative deliberative processes (citizens' assemblies).**
   - The OECD gathered close to 300 cases and reports that, done well, these processes "can lead to better policy outcomes… and enhance trust between citizens and government" [26] [INT].
   - The same report says future research is needed for "a better understanding of impact" [26]. The evidence is descriptive.
9. **Platform-level feed changes alone.** A large randomised trial found that a chronological feed did not change polarisation over three months [19b] [PR]. Evidence does not currently support feed redesign alone as a democratic safeguard.

**What to avoid (consistent with the corrective literature):** remedies that shrink participation or exclude voters. These would worsen the representation gaps populism feeds on [4][5][7], and the measures that come closest to doing so have backfired in practice [25a].
