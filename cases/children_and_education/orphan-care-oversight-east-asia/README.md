# Orphans and Children in Alternative Care in East Asia: Gaps in Oversight, What the Evidence Shows, and What Has Been Shown to Work

Date: 2026-11-19

---

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers children without parental care, and children in formal or informal alternative care, in seven East Asian jurisdictions: mainland China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea, Mongolia, and North Korea where any evidence exists. It asks how these children are cared for, how that care is overseen, where oversight has failed, and what has been shown to work. It works at the policy and population level only. It contains no individual stories, no first-person accounts and no identifying details.

- **Empirical claims** rest on peer-reviewed studies and reviews only. Each source is tagged by type: **[PR-study]** for a peer-reviewed empirical study or peer-reviewed policy or ethics analysis, **[SR]** for a peer-reviewed systematic, scoping or narrative review or meta-analysis, **[PR-methods]** for a peer-reviewed paper on measurement or data coverage, **[LAW]** for a statute, treaty record or other official legal source, **[REC]** for the findings of an official commission of inquiry (cited as a record of what the commission found, not as peer-reviewed research), and **[GOV-flag]** for a government statement or a government or agency figure.
- **Government, agency and programme figures** are flagged **[GOV-flag]** right beside the figure, including when a peer-reviewed study is built on them. These figures record *what agencies counted, approved, reported or chose to publish*. They are shaped by reporting incentives, funding rules, legal definitions and enforcement campaigns. **They are not prevalence.**
- **No Canadian data appear in this case, and no paper with a Canadian-affiliated author is cited.** Author affiliations were checked for every cited paper (OpenAlex, Crossref, PubMed or the paper itself). Where a record listed no affiliation, the author's other records or the paper's author notes were checked; the method is noted in the source list.
- **Every source was opened on 19 November 2026.** The source list says for each whether the full text or only the abstract was read. Where only an abstract was read, claims stay within what the abstract says. Sources that could not be opened were dropped and are listed near the end.
- **Labels.** **UNVERIFIED** marks a figure or claim whose original source could not be confirmed on this run. **GAP** marks a topic where no peer-reviewed evidence was found.
- **Evidence strength** is rated for each finding as **Strong**, **Moderate**, **Limited** or **Very limited**, with a reason. "Strong" means consistent findings across several designs, at least one randomised, quasi-experimental or large population-based source, and a plausible mechanism. Very little evidence in this case comes from East Asia itself at that level; where a rating rests on evidence from outside the region, the case says so.
- **Study findings and advocacy claims are kept separate** (Section 12).
- **No political attribution.** Nothing here suggests that one party, government or ideological camp causes the problems described or deserves credit for fixing them. Where a commission found that a state failed in its duties, that finding is reported as the commission's finding, in its own words.
- **Language.** The case uses "parents" rather than gendered parenting terms, except where a source title or legal text is quoted exactly.

### Legal and official records that shape every count

- **Japan.** Article 3-2 of the Child Welfare Act, inserted by the 2016 revision, requires national and local government to support parents so that children are raised in the home. Where that is difficult or inappropriate, it requires measures so that children are raised continuously "in a care environment equivalent to the home", and only where that too is inappropriate, "in as good a family-like environment as possible" [LAW-1]. (Translation from the Japanese statute is this case's own.)
- **South Korea, birth and relinquishment.** The Special Act on Crisis Pregnancy and Protected Childbirth Support and Child Protection (Act No. 19816, 31 October 2023) entered into force on 19 July 2024 [LAW-2]. It defines "protected childbirth" as "the de-identification of a pregnant woman in crisis after completing all counseling under Article 7 and filing an application under Article 9 to give birth" [LAW-2].
- **South Korea, intercountry adoption.** Korea deposited its ratification of the 1993 Hague Adoption Convention on 17 June 2025, and the Convention "entered into force for the Republic of Korea" on 1 October 2025 [LAW-3].
- **China, intercountry adoption.** At a regular press conference on 5 September 2024, the Foreign Ministry spokesperson stated: "apart from the adoption of a child or stepchild from one's collateral relatives by blood of the same generation and up to the third degree of kinship by foreigners coming to China, China will not send children abroad for adoption" [GOV-1]. **[GOV-flag]** This is a government policy statement, not a statute.
- **Consequence for measurement.** Each jurisdiction counts a different population: "orphans" under civil-affairs guardianship (China), "children in care" by placement type (Japan), adoptions reported by licensed agencies (Korea, historically), and in North Korea nothing that outside bodies can check. Counts across jurisdictions are not comparable, and counts within one jurisdiction change when definitions or funding rules change.

## 1. Summary of findings (facts first)

| Topic | Core finding | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional care (global evidence base) | Institutional care is associated with delays in physical growth, brain development, cognition and attention; family placement, especially early, leads to recovery in some but not all domains | **Strong** (global, including a randomised trial); **Limited** (East Asia-specific evidence) |
| Conditions and oversight in the region | Japan's system remains mostly institutional; China's state system holds mostly children with disabilities; small studies from Japan, Hong Kong and Taiwan show poorer quality of life or higher psychiatric morbidity in residential care; North Korean institutions are closed to outside monitoring | **Limited** (small, mostly cross-sectional studies); **Very limited** (North Korea, Mongolia) |
| Left-behind children in China | Meta-analyses find more neglect and more depressive symptoms among left-behind children; one instrumental-variable study finds harm to health and education from cumulative parental absence | **Moderate** (consistent meta-analyses plus one quasi-experimental study; instruments and definitions vary) |
| South Korea's overseas adoption | An official truth commission found that the state failed to legislate, oversee and follow procedure, that orphan records were fabricated and identities substituted, and that emigration permits were approved almost automatically | **Strong as a record** of what the commission found [REC]; peer-reviewed evidence on the scale of fraud is a **GAP** |
| Japan's move toward foster care | The 2016 law sets a family-first order. An older ministry table reproduced in a 2018 peer-reviewed paper (data from before 2014) showed about one child in ten in a foster home [PR-17] [GOV-flag]. The agency's own series shows foster and family-home placements rising from 15.6% (March 2014) to 25.1% (March 2024) of children in foster, family-home, children's-home and infant-home care [GOV-2] [GOV-flag], still far below the national target of 75% for preschool children and 50% for school-age children [GOV-2] | **Moderate** (on the reliance itself); **GAP** (on the effect of the 2016 reform) |
| Disability and abandonment | A 1990s study linked increased abandonment in China to strict birth-control enforcement [PR-9]; children in China's state care are described as mostly disabled [PR-6]; a qualitative study found no formal decision procedure when parents considered withholding treatment from newborns with disabilities [PR-8] | **Limited** |
| Trafficking and sale of children | In China, the sale of children, especially boys, for informal adoption is documented through campaign figures and offender studies; weak birth registration made laundering easier | **Limited** (figures are campaign counts [GOV-flag]; offender sample small) |
| Leaving care | Support after 18 is thin; Japan's law now allows independent-living assistance past 20 without a stated upper age [LAW-1]; qualitative studies in China report gaps in formal support | **Very limited** |
| Inspection and data | No peer-reviewed study opened evaluates an inspection regime in any of the seven jurisdictions; many countries "lack functional administrative systems" for counting children outside family care | **GAP** (inspection effect); **Moderate** (data weakness) |
| What works | Family-based care over institutions (global randomised and meta-analytic evidence); within the region, small studies favour group homes over large institutions, grandparent and supported informal family care over institutions, and some school- or group-based programmes for left-behind children | **Strong** (global, family vs institution); **Limited** (regional interventions) |

## 2. How many children? What the numbers can and cannot say

- **Global data coverage is weak.** A peer-reviewed data-coverage study found that "many countries still lack functional administrative systems for enumerating children living outside of family care". Using country-level figures from some 142 countries, it estimated that "approximately 2.7 million children between the ages of 0 and 17 years could be living in institutional care worldwide" [PR-methods-1]. **[GOV-flag]** The estimate is compiled from national administrative counts and inherits their gaps. **Moderate** (on the weakness of data, which the paper documents directly); **Very limited** (on the precise size).
- **China.** One study states that "In 2022, China had more than 190,000 orphans in different types of care" [PR-5]. **[GOV-flag]** This is an official-type count of children recognised as orphans; the abstract does not give its source (**UNVERIFIED** as to provenance). It excludes children whose parents are alive but absent, which is the far larger group (Section 4).
- **Japan.** "More than 46,000 children are in care in Japan, 88% of them in institutions" (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare figures for 2014, as reported in [PR-17]). **[GOV-flag]**
- **South Korea.** The truth commission records that "Between 1955 and 1999, an estimated 141,778 children were adopted abroad", a total "reported to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission by the four adoption agencies" [REC-1]. **[GOV-flag]** These are agency-reported counts; the commission found agency record-keeping itself unreliable (Section 5).
- **North Korea.** No reliable count exists. The UN Commission of Inquiry recorded that the food situation "caused a very high number of children to become homeless" and that, since the 2009 currency reform, "the number of kotjebi [street children] has reportedly increased" [REC-2]. **GAP** for any count.
- **Mongolia, Taiwan, Hong Kong.** No peer-reviewed count of children in alternative care was opened for this case. **GAP.**

> **Bottom line on size:** every regional count in this case is an administrative or agency count. None measures how many children actually lack parental care, and none is comparable across jurisdictions.

## 3. Conditions in institutional care, and how institutions are overseen

### 3.1 What the global evidence says about institutional care
- A Lancet Group Commission's systematic review and meta-analysis "highlights the associations between institutional care as typically practised and delays in physical growth, brain development, cognition, and attentional competence. Weaker associations were found between institutional care and adverse effects on physical health and socioemotional development" (summary as stated in the Commission's second paper, read in full) [SR-2; SR-1]. **Strong** (large review, consistent with the randomised trial in Section 11).
- The Commission's policy paper adds that projects to transform single institutions in isolation, "even when improved outcomes are secured for the individual children and families supported by these projects", lack "the scale to reach all vulnerable children" and do not tackle the causes of institutionalisation [SR-2].

### 3.2 Mainland China
- **Who is in state care.** An early peer-reviewed policy study described orphaned or abandoned children as "most of them also disabled" and documented a policy movement "from institutional care to foster care", shaped by interaction among the state, civil society and international and domestic NGOs [PR-6]. A later scoping review found that foster care in China still targets "primarily children who are orphans, abandoned, and disabled but not maltreated children", and called for an out-of-home care system for maltreated children [SR-10]. **Limited** (one policy analysis and one scoping review).
- **Nutrition inside institutions.** In a multi-country analysis of records for 2,926 children at 35 institution-based care sites in one NGO nutrition programme (sites in Mongolia, India, Ethiopia, Vietnam, China and the Philippines), "717 (28.7%) children were anaemic, 788 (34.1%) underweight, 1048 (37.3%) stunted" at initial screening, and "Children with disabilities had higher prevalence of malnutrition" [PR-12]. **[GOV-flag]** (programme records from enrolled sites; not representative of all institutions, and the abstract does not separate the Chinese or Mongolian sites). **Limited.**
- **AIDS orphanages.** Interviews with 23 children and 5 workers in two rural counties found children felt living conditions were better than in the relatives' homes they had come from, but reported "administrative restraints, limited psychological guidance, stigma, lack of education on AIDS, and financial burdens of the operation" [PR-10]. **Very limited** (qualitative, two counties, 2006–07).
- **Outside partners inside state institutions.** An ethnographic study described a foreign-funded special-care unit inside a state orphanage, caring for "the institution's most severely ill and disabled children", and conflicts between imported and local caregiving practices [PR-11]. Care standards inside one institution could thus differ by funder, which complicates any single standard of oversight. **Very limited** (one site).
- **Oversight.** No peer-reviewed study opened for this case describes or evaluates the inspection of child welfare institutions in mainland China. **GAP.**

### 3.3 Japan
- See Section 6 for the scale of reliance on institutions.
- **Quality of life.** In a 2016 cross-sectional study, the total quality-of-life score of 47 children living in children's homes was "statistically significantly lower" than that of 157 children living in traditional families [PR-18]. **Limited** (small, one area).
- **Maltreatment history and mental health.** Among 342 children aged 9–18 in residential facilities, maltreatment predicted insecure attachment, which in turn predicted depressive symptoms [PR-19]. **Limited** (cross-sectional).
- **Counter-evidence on development.** A study of 86 children aged 10–33 months in institutional care, compared with 1,897 in biological family care, found they "did not exhibit a delay in productive vocabulary", although comprehension was delayed; time in the institution and "the number of books read to them significantly predicted children's vocabulary scores" [PR-20]. **Limited.** Care quality inside an institution matters, and Japanese institutions are not uniform.
- **Facility type.** A study of children's self-reliance support facilities notes that "in Japan most child protective care is conducted in facilities" and describes the difficult integration of school education into them [PR-22]. **Very limited** (qualitative).

### 3.4 South Korea
- Five-year longitudinal data with propensity score matching found that "children in group homes tend to have more desirable positive outcomes and fewer behavioral problems in the medium term" than children in institutional care facilities, and that group homes were more cost-effective [PR-25]. **Limited** (one cohort; matching cannot rule out unmeasured selection).
- Three further Korean outcome studies comparing foster or kinship care with institutional care were found but could not be opened, and were dropped (end list).

### 3.5 Hong Kong
- **Staff stress and children's behaviour.** A 2-year study of 381 residents and 76 workers in residential care homes found that, over the first 9 months, changes in workers' role stress were positively associated with changes in "work burnout and total behavioral problems" [PR-26]. **Limited** (observational).
- **Small group homes.** A survey of 597 children and adolescents from 107 small group homes, "representing 77% of the target population", identified three psychological health profiles: "optimal" (39.9%), "moderate" (45.4%) and "high‐risk" (14.7%) [PR-27]. **Moderate** for description (near-census of one care type); **Very limited** for causes.

### 3.6 Taiwan
- Among 97 children in four residential institutions in Northern Taiwan (76 in three group homes placed by the social welfare bureau; 21 in one youth home placed by the judiciary for delinquent or conduct behaviour), lifetime prevalence of any psychiatric disorder was 54.6% overall (44.7% group homes; 90.5% youth home) and current prevalence 28.9%, against general-population figures from other studies of 31.6% lifetime and 25% current [PR-29]. **Limited** (small; the comparison figures are from other studies). Peer-reviewed evidence on how Taiwan oversees residential care: **GAP.**

### 3.7 Mongolia
- Mongolian sites are included in the multi-country nutrition analysis [PR-12], but no Mongolia-specific result appears in the abstract. A peer-reviewed evaluation of a community home-visiting model for vulnerable families is covered in Section 11 [PR-30]. Peer-reviewed evidence on conditions in Mongolian residential institutions: **GAP.**

### 3.8 North Korea
- The UN Commission of Inquiry recorded the government's claim "that most orphans are sent to institutions where they can benefit from government protection", and heard that there are "different types of orphanage-like structures": regular orphanages, *gyebumo* institutions and *guhoso* facilities for street children [REC-2].
- The Commission quoted a report that "the custody facilities which serve the purpose of protecting street children resemble in fact a detention facility, rather than a protection facility for children, and children accommodated in the centres are deprived of education and exploited for labour", and recorded that "Humanitarian agencies have never had access to these facilities" [REC-2].
- **Evidence strength:** **Very limited** for conditions (testimony and secondary reports; no access). The absence of independent oversight is itself the Commission's finding. Peer-reviewed studies on North Korean institutional care: **GAP.**

## 4. Left-behind children in China

"Left-behind children" stay in rural home areas, usually with grandparents or other relatives, while one or both parents migrate for work. They are not in formal alternative care, but many are in informal kinship care with no state oversight.

- **Scale.** A meta-analysis states that "approximately 70 million children, nearly 25% of the child population" are left behind [SR-7]. The abstract gives no source for this number (**UNVERIFIED**); such national numbers usually derive from official census samples and should be read as **[GOV-flag]**. A quasi-experimental study notes that "approximately 160 million Chinese rural workers migrated to cities" **[GOV-flag]**, and that "restrictions on migrant access to local health and education systems" are why many children are left behind [PR-2].
- **Neglect.** A meta-analysis of 13 case-control studies (18,688 left-behind children in a sample of 42,003) found higher overall neglect (OR = 1.58, 95% CI [1.50, 1.67]), with the largest gap for emotional neglect (OR = 2.29) [SR-5]. **Moderate** (consistent pooled result; observational studies using China-specific instruments).
- **Depressive symptoms.** A meta-analysis of 39 studies (31,663 left-behind children and 20,049 controls) found pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms of 30.7% versus 22.8% (OR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.4-1.9), and that "the use of different scales was significantly associated with the prevalence" [SR-6]. Another, of 21 studies, gave a pooled estimate of 26.4% and traced heterogeneity to "using an unclear definition of LBC and using invalidated depression instruments" [SR-7]. **Moderate** (direction consistent; levels depend on instrument).
- **Causal evidence.** Using national rural-urban migration survey data, with weather shocks in home villages as an instrument for parental migration, one study found "a sizable adverse effect of exposure to parental migration on the health and education outcomes of children: in particular, boys" [PR-2]. **Moderate** (quasi-experimental, one dataset; the instrument rests on assumptions).
- **Sexual abuse risk.** In a 2015 survey in six middle schools in two provinces, being bullied was associated with child sexual abuse among left-behind children (aOR = 2.52) and much more strongly among left-behind girls (aOR = 7.36) [PR-3]. **Limited** (cross-sectional, two provinces).
- **Oversight.** No peer-reviewed study opened for this case evaluates any formal monitoring or registration system for left-behind children. **GAP.**

## 5. South Korea's overseas adoption record and the truth commission's findings

All findings in this section are those of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of the Republic of Korea, from the English version of its report on intercountry adoption [REC-1]. They are cited as an official record of what the commission found, not as peer-reviewed research. The commission recognised 56 petitioners as victims of serious human rights violations; its system-level findings are reported here without naming individuals.

- **State responsibility.** The commission found that, "in the process of sending hundreds of thousands of children abroad as part of child welfare policy, the Korean government neglected its constitutional and treaty-based duty to protect its citizens' fundamental rights by failing to legislate properly, by neglecting oversight, and by not carrying out administrative procedures" [REC-1].
- **Adoption as a substitute for welfare.** It found that the government "reduced costly domestic measures such as public relief and institutional care, and turned instead to intercountry adoption, passing responsibility onto the private sector" [REC-1].
- **Money and incentives.** It found that "Adoption agencies reinvested adoption fees and compulsory donations from adoptive parents into securing more children for adoption, and by the 1980s, with government turning a blind eye, expanded adoption into an industry" [REC-1].
- **Records and identity.** It found that petitioners "were sent abroad through unlawful means that erased or falsified their identities and family information", including duplicate family registers and identities "laundered under those of other children" [REC-1].
- **Approval without review.** "In 1984, the Ministry authorized 7,964 emigration permits for intercountry adoption: 6,599 cases, or 82.9 percent, were approved on the very day of application, while 1,279 cases, or 16.1 percent, were approved the following day" [REC-1]. **[GOV-flag]** (ministry administrative records; they count approvals, not the quality of checks). Between 1984 and 1986, one major agency's 12,090 applications for emigration permits were all granted but six; the commission concluded that the Ministry's review "was conducted in a merely perfunctory manner" [REC-1].
- **After departure.** "Of the 98 petitioners who gave testimony to the Commission, 31 reported suffering abuse in their adoptive homes" [REC-1]. This is a self-selected group of petitioners, not a rate for all adoptees. On adoptees who never acquired citizenship abroad, the commission noted that "No systematic survey has ever been conducted by either the Korean or U.S. government" [REC-1].
- **Recommendations (record).** The commission recommended a formal state apology; prioritising domestic solutions and prompt ratification of the Hague Convention; investigation of unlawful adoptions and of adoptees without citizenship; correction of public records; collection and preservation of records from care facilities, counselling centres, medical providers and local governments; and "a dedicated organization to provide practical support for restoring relationships between adoptees and their original families, including DNA testing" [REC-1]. It also recommended that agencies transfer all their records to the national adoption archives [REC-1].
- **Since then.** In preparation for ratification, Korea's Special Act on Domestic Adoption and Act on Intercountry Adoption entered into force on 19 July 2025, placing adoption procedures under public authorities, with the Ministry of Health and Welfare as central authority [LAW-4; GOV-3] **[GOV-flag]** (the ministry describing its own reform); Korea's Hague ratification took effect on 1 October 2025 [LAW-3]. No evaluation of either change was found. **GAP.**
- **Evidence strength:** **Strong as a record** (a statutory commission working from national, diplomatic and city archives, agency files, interviews and testimony); **Very limited** for the share of all overseas adoptions affected, which the commission did not estimate. Peer-reviewed quantitative evidence on the scale of fraud: **GAP.**

## 6. Japan's reliance on institutional care and its move toward foster care

- **The reliance.** A peer-reviewed overview by Japanese residential-care researchers states that "Japan still relies especially heavily on the residential care system; this is due to the past process of development more than to traditional Japanese culture" [PR-17]. Its table of Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare figures (2017) shows, of 46,038 children: 4,578 (9.9%) in foster homes, 829 (1.8%) in family homes, 3,069 (6.7%) in infant care institutions and 28,831 (62.6%) in children's homes [PR-17]. **[GOV-flag]** **Moderate** (official counts, consistent across the years reported, interpreted by researchers). The table is cited to a 2017 ministry publication, but its counts are lower than the agency's own figure for March 2014, so they describe the situation before 2014. **Later official series:** 19.7% at end-March 2018 and 25.1% at end-March 2024 (foster plus family homes, as a share of foster, family-home, children's-home and infant-home placements) [GOV-2] **[GOV-flag]** (administrative counts; the denominator excludes other facility types, so the rate is not comparable with the 9.9% above).
- **Not simply culture.** The same paper reports that the "cultural explanation" is "not well supported by research", that foster family care "was not foreign to Japanese families in the past", and that "a big difference in the foster care to residential care ratio among prefectures" fits path dependency, though it "may still be explained by cultural differences among the prefectures" [PR-17]. **Limited** (interpretive; one paper).
- **Reform.** The 2016 revision of the Child Welfare Act wrote a family-first order of preference into law [LAW-1; PR-17]. Some institutions introduced small-unit care of at most 8 children and group homes of at most 6 [PR-17]. Between 2008 and 2012, the number of institutions that had introduced small-group-care homes rose from 212 of 489 (43.4%) to 323 of 561 (57.6%) [PR-17]. **[GOV-flag]**
- **A relayed advocacy figure, not used.** A peer-reviewed paper relays an advocacy organisation's 2014 figure for the share of children in alternative care who were in foster care as of 2011 [PR-17]. The underlying government source was not opened, so the figure is **UNVERIFIED** and is not used as a finding.
- **How foster care is understood.** Interviews with seven Japanese foster parents suggest they "implicitly recognize the temporariness of their role", unlike prevailing Norwegian discourse [PR-23]. **Very limited** (seven interviews).
- **Effect of the reform.** No peer-reviewed study opened for this case tests whether the 2016 revision *caused* the rise in family-based placements shown in official series [GOV-2], or changed child outcomes. **GAP** (causal effect).

## 7. Disability and abandonment

- **China, sex of the child.** A study of 629 families (392 adopting and 237 abandoning) in the mid-1990s linked increased abandonment to strict birth-control enforcement [PR-9]. Only an indexing summary of the article was opened, so no further figures from that paper are used. **Limited** (non-random sample, concentrated in one province; full text not opened).
- **China, disability.** A policy study described children in state care as mostly disabled [PR-6]. A qualitative child-rights study of three cases found that, when parents considered not seeking treatment for a newborn with a disability, "the decision was made privately by parents—no formal decision procedure was available, the role of professionals was minimal, and the state did not intervene", and concluded that "China has not yet established a system to support parents and protect the lives of young children with disabilities" [PR-8]. **Very limited** (three cases), but directly about oversight.
- **Disability inside institutions.** In the multi-country nutrition records, 739 children (25.3%) had disabilities, and children with disabilities had more malnutrition [PR-12]. **[GOV-flag]** (programme records). **Limited.**
- **Leaving care with a disability (China).** Care leavers reported "societal discrimination, particularly against those with physical impairments" in finding work [PR-16]. **Very limited** (19 interviews).
- **Japan, anonymous relinquishment.** A baby hatch has operated at a hospital in Kumamoto City since 10 May 2007. A peer-reviewed ethics analysis lists the main objections (the child's right to know their origins, inducing abandonment, medical risk, no clear legal basis) and argues that there are "many plausible refutations for each objection" [PR-24]. This is a normative analysis, not an outcome study. Whether the hatch reduced harm: **GAP.**
- **South Korea, protected birth.** Since 19 July 2024, Korean law has provided a counselled, de-identified "protected childbirth" route [LAW-2]. No outcome evaluation was found. **GAP.**
- **Mainland China baby hatches.** No peer-reviewed study was found. **GAP.**
- **Abandonment of children with disabilities in Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Mongolia and North Korea.** No peer-reviewed quantitative study was found. **GAP.**

## 8. Trafficking and the sale of children

- **China, demand for sons and informal adoption.** A peer-reviewed review (full text read) describes how preference for sons and the cost of formal adoption sustain a market: "Adopting a child from an orphanage requires the payment of a sponsoring fee of tens of thousands of Yuan. Because of this high expense, some purchasers prefer to purchase a child privately" [PR-13]. Citing Chinese-language sources, it reports that poor registration of newborns made it "too easy for the children who are adopted after they have been abducted and trafficked to have their data accepted for entry into the household registrations system" [PR-13]. **Limited** (review relying heavily on media and official sources).
- **Campaign figures.** The same review reports that a national campaign from April 2009 to December 2011 "solved 16,137 cases relating to the abduction and trafficking of women and 11,777 cases relating to the abduction and trafficking of children", and that "18,518 children and 34,813 women were rescued" [PR-13]. **[GOV-flag]** These are enforcement-campaign outputs; they rise and fall with campaign intensity and are not prevalence. The review also reports that reliance on "dedicated task operations" makes it "difficult to sustain actions against such a crime" [PR-13].
- **Who the offenders are.** Interviews with 10 of the 44 women imprisoned for child trafficking in one Chinese prison led the author to conclude that female child traffickers are "offenders as well as victims of social and gender inequalities" [PR-14]. **Very limited** (ten interviews, one prison).
- **Korea, false orphan status and identity substitution.** See Section 5. The commission found fabricated foundling reports and identity substitution [REC-1]. This is the strongest official record in the region of children being moved through an adoption system under false identities. **Strong as a record.**
- **China and intercountry adoption.** China stated in September 2024 that it will no longer send children abroad for adoption, except within close kin [GOV-1]. Peer-reviewed evidence on trafficking of children *into* Chinese institutions for intercountry adoption was not found among sources that could be opened on this run. **GAP.**
- **North Korea.** Peer-reviewed evidence on the trafficking of North Korean children was not found among sources opened for this case. **GAP.**
- **Mongolia, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Japan.** No peer-reviewed study of child sale or trafficking linked to alternative care was found. **GAP.**

## 9. Children leaving care

- **Japan.** Residential care is "regulated by the Child Welfare Act for children under 18 years", and support for care leavers had been provided "only with limited capacity, and without the benefits of a funded system". The revised Act "expands the possibility for care and support of young adults up to the age of 22", which the authors call "still limited" compared with countries where support continues to 26 [PR-17]. The Act as currently in force extends independent-living assistance to former care recipients aged 20 and over whom the prefecture recognises as needing it, for example while in high school or university, without a stated upper age [LAW-1, Art. 6-3]. No outcome evaluation was found. **GAP.** An anthropological study argues that the system "disproportionately depends on chance" and demands that youth leaving care "care for themselves with minimal or no state assistance" [PR-21]. **Limited** (policy description plus a qualitative study).
- **China.** Interviews with 22 care leavers identified personal qualities and supportive relationships as protective, but found "substantial gaps in formal support structures" [PR-15]. Interviews with 19 care leavers found "educational setbacks, insufficient work skills, lack of support, and societal discrimination" in finding work [PR-16]. **Very limited** (qualitative, small samples).
- **Hong Kong.** A trauma-informed programme in residential care reported improvements in "readiness, and perceived strength in terms of independent living" (Section 11) [PR-28]. **Very limited.**
- **South Korea, Taiwan, Mongolia, North Korea.** No peer-reviewed English-language outcome study of care leavers was opened. Korean-language studies were found but dropped because their author affiliations could not be confirmed (end list). **GAP.**

## 10. Gaps in inspection and data

- **Counting.** Globally, "many countries still lack functional administrative systems for enumerating children living outside of family care" [PR-methods-1]. The Lancet Commission called for "the assemblage of data relevant for monitoring the situation of children in all forms of care" [SR-2].
- **Korea: oversight on paper only.** Same-day or next-day approval of 99% of 1984 emigration permits (82.9% same day, 16.1% next day) [REC-1] **[GOV-flag]** shows an oversight step that existed on paper but not in practice.
- **Korea: records.** The commission found agencies responsible for "fabricating identity papers, neglecting recordkeeping, and failing to preserve accurate information" [REC-1].
- **Japan: variation without explanation.** Large differences between prefectures in the foster-to-residential ratio [PR-17] mean national averages hide very different local systems. No peer-reviewed study opened explains them. **GAP.**
- **China: definitions.** Foster care targets orphaned, abandoned and disabled children rather than maltreated children [SR-10], so counts of children in care miss children who need protection from their own families. Left-behind children sit outside formal care counts altogether (Section 4).
- **North Korea: no access.** Humanitarian agencies "have never had access" to custody facilities for street children [REC-2].
- **Inspection regimes.** No peer-reviewed study opened for this case describes or evaluates how residential care is inspected in any of the seven jurisdictions, or whether inspection improves outcomes. **GAP.**
- **Research quality.** Reviews on left-behind children and on foster care in China both report small samples and weak designs [SR-8; SR-9; SR-10]. **Moderate** (on the weakness of the evidence base).

## 11. What has been shown to work

### 11.1 Global evidence (not from East Asia)
- **Family care versus institutional care: randomised evidence.** In the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, young children living in institutions were randomly assigned to continued institutional care or to foster care. "The cognitive outcome of children who remained in the institution was markedly below that of never-institutionalized children and children taken out of the institution and placed into foster care", and gains were "most marked for the youngest children placed in foster care" [PR-1]. A later review describes it as "the first and only randomized controlled trial of foster care as an alternative to institutional care", reports that "foster care remediates some but not all compromises", and that "higher quality of care provided and more stable placements" enhanced outcomes [SR-4]. **Strong** for the direction of effect; the trial was in Romania, so transfer to East Asian systems is an assumption.
- **Meta-analysis.** Across 23 studies and 13,630 children, "children in foster care had consistently better experiences and less problems across the three outcomes as compared to children in residential care" [SR-3]. **Moderate** (observational studies; children are not randomly assigned to placement types).
- **Policy consensus.** The Lancet Commission's policy paper set out 14 recommendations prioritising family strengthening, high-quality family-based alternatives and "careful and appropriate gatekeeping (the process for ensuring alternative care is used only when necessary and that the child receives the most suitable support to meet their individual needs)" [SR-2]. This is expert consensus built on evidence, not itself an outcome study.

### 11.2 Evidence from the region
- **China, foster care.** "Most studies indicated that foster children exhibited better well-being outcomes ... than children who stayed in child welfare institutions", but the review found "small sample sizes, less rigorous study designs, limited data analyses" [SR-10]. **Limited.**
- **China, grandparent care and supported informal adoption.** Among 320 orphans in one city, "grandparent care has a significant positive impact on the overall development and psychological status of orphans", moderated by guardians' social participation [PR-5]. **Limited** (cross-sectional). In some locations, institutions accepted guardianship of children already being raised informally by community families and supported those families; among 12 young people studied, "the family environment was more conducive to the young people's wellbeing", and formal guardianship gave families "state resources for support" [PR-7]. **Very limited** (12 people).
- **South Korea, group homes.** Group homes produced better medium-term outcomes and were more cost-effective than institutional facilities [PR-25]. **Limited.**
- **Hong Kong, trauma-informed care.** A quasi-experimental evaluation (23 intervention and 11 comparison residents) found the intervention group "reported fewer experiences of child abuse and family violence; more reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress" [PR-28]. **Very limited** (very small, non-random).
- **Hong Kong, staff support.** Because changes in staff role stress tracked residents' behaviour problems [PR-26], reducing role stress is a plausible target. **Limited** (association, not a tested intervention).
- **Japan, care quality inside institutions.** Books read to young children predicted their vocabulary scores [PR-20]. **Limited** (association).
- **Left-behind children.** A review of 12 randomised trials found that "various intervention formats ... improved LBC's mental health", but "The evidence base, however, is relatively poor in quality" and "Social workers were not involved in any intervention" [SR-8]. A scoping review of 33 studies (20 randomised) found "only two studies adopted validated interventions" [SR-9]. A pilot randomised trial of a six-session education programme for 56 migrant parents found no difference in parental identity but "significant differences did emerge in parent-child relationships and parenting practices" [PR-4]. **Limited.**
- **Mongolia, sustaining community home visiting.** In a follow-up of a volunteer home-visiting model in nine sites after the original funding ended, "Only two of the home visiting sites were found to have continued operations", both by moving the work to schools with salaried teachers; the principal barriers were "inadequate and untimely sustainability planning and lack of financial resources to support program coordination" [PR-30]. **Limited** (one programme, ex-post design).
- **Not shown to work, or not evaluated:** inspection regimes (**GAP**); Hague ratification (**GAP**); Japan's 2016 reform (**GAP**); protected or anonymous birth schemes (**GAP**); extended leaving-care support (**GAP**).

## Fair counterarguments

| Common claim | What the opened evidence shows |
|---|---|
| "Orphanages are full of orphans." | Japanese residential institutions were designated after the mid-1990s "as the last resort for maltreated young people" [PR-17], and China's state-care population is described as mostly children with disabilities [PR-6]. "Orphan" counts and "children in care" counts measure different populations. |
| "Japan's reliance on institutions is cultural." | One peer-reviewed account finds the cultural explanation "not well supported by research" and points to path dependency and prefectural variation [PR-17]. Evidence is limited. |
| "All institutional care damages development." | Global evidence shows strong average associations [SR-1; SR-2], but one Japanese study found no productive-vocabulary delay in institutional care [PR-20]. Age at entry and quality of care matter [SR-4]. |
| "Korean adoption fraud was a few isolated cases." | The commission found systemic failure by the state to legislate, oversee and follow procedure [REC-1]. It did not estimate what share of all overseas adoptions was affected. |
| "Rescue counts show trafficking is falling (or rising)." | Campaign rescue counts are enforcement outputs [GOV-flag] that track campaign intensity, not prevalence [PR-13]. |
| "Left-behind children are fine because relatives care for them." | Meta-analyses find more neglect and depressive symptoms [SR-5; SR-6; SR-7], and a quasi-experimental study finds harm [PR-2]. One study finds grandparent care protective for orphans [PR-5], so the picture is mixed. |

## Proposed solutions

Each solution below names the failures it addresses (with a cross-reference to the section where they are documented), the source that backs it, and an evidence-strength rating with a reason. **UNTESTED** marks a proposal for which no outcome evaluation of that specific measure was found; for those, the cited source documents the failure or recommends the measure, but does not show that it works.

**S1. Gatekeeping and family support before any admission to care.** Require that every admission to residential care pass a documented, reviewable assessment of whether the child's own or extended family could care for the child with support (cash, disability services, counselling).
- *Failures addressed:* institutions used as the default rather than the last resort in Japan (Section 6); private, unsupported decisions about newborns with disabilities in China (Section 7); Korea's use of overseas adoption in place of domestic welfare (Section 5).
- *Evidence:* recommended by the Lancet Commission, which defines gatekeeping and links it to preventing separation [SR-2]; Japan's 2016 law already sets a family-first order [LAW-1].
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because it rests on expert recommendation and no outcome study of gatekeeping in East Asia was found. **UNTESTED.**

**S2. Move young children from institutions to family-based care first, and early.** Set placement targets that give priority to children under three, with funded recruitment, training and support of foster and kinship carers.
- *Failures addressed:* Japan's heavy reliance on institutions (Section 6); China's institution-centred state care (Section 3.2); developmental harm associated with institutional care (Section 3.1).
- *Evidence:* the randomised Bucharest trial, with larger gains for earlier placement [PR-1; SR-4]; meta-analytic evidence [SR-1; SR-3]; consistent but weaker Chinese evidence [SR-10].
- *Strength:* **Strong** for the direction of effect (a randomised trial plus meta-analyses); **Limited** for East Asian implementation, because regional studies are small and observational.

**S3. Where residential care remains, make it small, stable and well staffed.** Convert large institutions into group homes of six or fewer children; fund staffing and supervision that reduce staff role stress; train staff in trauma-informed care; build reading and language routines into daily care for young children.
- *Failures addressed:* lower quality of life and high psychiatric morbidity in residential care in Japan and Taiwan (Sections 3.3, 3.6); staff stress tracking children's behaviour problems in Hong Kong (Section 3.5).
- *Evidence:* better outcomes and cost-effectiveness for Korean group homes [PR-25]; staff role stress linked to residents' problems [PR-26]; a small trauma-informed care evaluation [PR-28]; books read predicting vocabulary [PR-20].
- *Strength:* **Limited**, because the studies are observational or small quasi-experiments, though all point the same way.

**S4. Formalise and resource kinship and informal family care.** Give grandparents and community families already raising children without parental care formal guardianship status, with cash support, health and education access and periodic home visits, instead of admitting the children to institutions.
- *Failures addressed:* orphans and left-behind children in informal care with no oversight (Sections 3.2, 4); institution-centred state guardianship (Section 3.2).
- *Evidence:* better development under grandparent care among orphans [PR-5]; better outcomes when institutions took guardianship of informally adopted children and supported their families [PR-7].
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because it rests on one cross-sectional study and one follow-up of 12 young people.

**S5. Adoption safeguards: verified consent, real review, separated money, preserved records.** In every jurisdiction that still arranges adoptions, require independently verified parental consent; separate adoption fees and donations from any body that finds or admits children; require substantive review of each case before approval; and preserve all records, including those of care facilities, hospitals and local governments, in a national archive that adoptees can access.
- *Failures addressed:* fabricated orphan records, identity substitution, same-day approvals, fee-driven expansion and lost records in Korea (Sections 5, 10); a fee incentive pushing private purchase of children in China (Section 8).
- *Evidence:* the Korean commission's findings and recommendations [REC-1]; the sponsoring-fee incentive described in [PR-13]; Korea's Hague ratification [LAW-3]. Korea's 2025 adoption laws put parts of this into effect [LAW-4; GOV-3]; outcome not yet evaluated. **UNTESTED.**
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because the failures are well documented as a record but no evaluation of these safeguards was found. **UNTESTED.**

**S6. Support for parents of newborns with disabilities, and disability-aware monitoring in institutions.** Create a formal, professional-led decision and support procedure when a child is born with a disability (treatment, financial help, respite, early intervention), and require routine nutrition and growth screening in every institution, with results reported by site.
- *Failures addressed:* no formal decision procedure and no state role in treatment decisions for newborns with disabilities in China (Section 7); more malnutrition among children with disabilities in institutions (Sections 3.2, 7).
- *Evidence:* [PR-8] documents the missing procedure; [PR-12] shows that site-level control charts can flag sites with malnutrition outside expected limits, but does not show that flagging improved outcomes.
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because it combines documentation of a failure with a monitoring method and no outcome evaluation. **UNTESTED.**

**S7. Counselled, record-keeping alternatives to anonymous abandonment.** Prefer counselled protected or confidential birth schemes that keep sealed records of origin over anonymous drop-off, and evaluate them.
- *Failures addressed:* infant abandonment and children's loss of their origins (Sections 5, 7); baby hatches operating without a clear legal basis (Section 7).
- *Evidence:* Korea's 2024 protected-birth law keeps de-identified records after counselling [LAW-2]; the Japanese ethics debate sets out the competing rights [PR-24].
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because only legal design and ethical argument exist; there are no outcome data. **UNTESTED.**

**S8. Validated, rigorously evaluated programmes for left-behind children, plus service access so children can move with their parents.** Scale only school- and community-based mental-health programmes that have been validated, involve social workers, and are tested in adequately powered trials; add education programmes for migrant parents; remove the barriers that stop migrants' children from using health and education services in cities.
- *Failures addressed:* higher neglect and depressive symptoms (Section 4); harm from cumulative parental absence (Section 4); mostly self-designed, poor-quality interventions (Sections 10, 11.2).
- *Evidence:* 12 randomised trials suggesting benefit but of poor quality [SR-8]; only two of 33 interventions validated [SR-9]; a pilot trial improving parent-child relationships [PR-4]; service-access restrictions identified as the reason children are left behind [PR-2].
- *Strength:* **Limited** for the programmes, because the trials are randomised but low in quality. The service-access element has not itself been evaluated for child outcomes in the sources opened; it is counted under this solution because the programme component has trial evidence.

**S9. Funded support after leaving care, without a fixed upper age, and track outcomes.** Guarantee housing, education and employment support, and a named adult contact, for all care leavers, and track their outcomes.
- *Failures addressed:* support ending at 18 and dependence on institutions' goodwill in Japan (Section 9); gaps in formal support, and discrimination against care leavers with impairments, in China (Sections 7, 9).
- *Evidence:* Japan's extension of possible support is documented as a policy change [PR-17], and the current Act sets no upper age [LAW-1, Art. 6-3], but no outcomes were found; qualitative evidence of need [PR-15; PR-16; PR-21]; improved independent-living readiness in one small Hong Kong programme [PR-28].
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because the evidence is a documented policy change and qualitative need, with no regional outcome evaluation of extended support. **UNTESTED.**

**S10. A counting and inspection system that can be checked.** Keep a register of every child in every form of alternative care, including kinship and informal care known to authorities, recording disability and reason for entry; publish annual figures with definitions; and subject every residential setting to independent inspection with published reports.
- *Failures addressed:* missing or non-comparable counts (Sections 2, 10); unexplained prefectural variation in Japan (Sections 6, 10); left-behind children outside any count (Section 4); oversight on paper only in Korea (Sections 5, 10).
- *Evidence:* the data-coverage gap is documented [PR-methods-1]; the Lancet Commission recommends monitoring data [SR-2]; the Korean commission documents the results of perfunctory oversight [REC-1].
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because no study opened shows that inspection improves outcomes (Section 10, GAP). **UNTESTED.**

**S11. Family tracing and identity restoration.** Fund DNA-assisted family tracing, record correction and reunion support for people adopted or placed under falsified identities, and correct civil registration where a child was registered under false data.
- *Failures addressed:* identity substitution and fabricated records in Korea (Sections 5, 8); trafficked children entered into household registration in China (Section 8).
- *Evidence:* recommended by the Korean commission [REC-1]; registration weaknesses described in [PR-13].
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because it rests on a commission recommendation and a documented failure, with no outcome evaluation. **UNTESTED.**

**S12. Plan for sustainability before donor-funded family-support programmes end.** Build transition plans, including salaried local staff such as teachers and public funding, into community home-visiting programmes from the start.
- *Failures addressed:* thin and fragile community support for vulnerable families in Mongolia (Sections 3.7, 11.2).
- *Evidence:* only two of nine sites continued, both by moving to salaried teachers; untimely sustainability planning and lack of funding were the main barriers [PR-30].
- *Strength:* **Limited**, because it rests on one ex-post evaluation with interviews and surveys: a documented outcome, but not a comparison of planned and unplanned transitions.

**S13. Independent access to North Korean institutions as a condition of support to them.** Tie humanitarian support for orphanages and custody facilities to regular independent access and monitoring.
- *Failures addressed:* no humanitarian access to custody facilities for street children, and reported detention-like conditions and labour exploitation (Sections 3.8, 10).
- *Evidence:* the Commission of Inquiry's record of no access [REC-2].
- *Strength:* **Very limited**, because only the failure is documented; there is no evidence on whether conditional access improves conditions. **UNTESTED.**

*Solutions proposed: 13. Marked UNTESTED: 8 (S1, S5, S6, S7, S9, S10, S11, S13).*
## What this does not replace

This is an ethics case study based on public sources. It is not legal advice, a finding about any named child, parent or worker, or a substitute for a court, inquiry, regulator, inspector or caseworker. It is not a ranking of countries, states or agencies, and it is not a current compliance score for any system. Proposed solutions are options to be tested, not evaluated results. Anyone acting on them should read the cited records and consult the people and institutions affected.
