# Who is watching? Oversight failures for children in foster care and legal orphans in the United States: a case study

Date: 2026-11-19

**How to read this case.** Facts come first. Each finding carries an evidence-strength label: **Strong** (consistent findings from several rigorous studies, causal designs, or a statistically projected audit finding), **Moderate** (consistent observational or administrative findings with known biases), **Limited** (single studies, small, clinical or non-generalisable samples, qualitative or descriptive work) or **GAP** (no adequate evidence was found). Anything that could not be verified by opening the source is marked **UNVERIFIED** and is not relied on.

Every government figure carries a **[GOV-flag]** with a short note on its reporting incentives. Government counts measure what agencies *detected, recorded and reported*. They are not true prevalence. They are shaped by self-reporting by states, by funding rules (for example, Title IV-E reimbursement and Chafee allotments tied to eligibility and reporting), by penalties for non-compliance, and by the reputational cost to an agency of high counts of runaways, maltreatment in care or deaths. Peer-reviewed analyses of government administrative data (for example AFCARS, NCANDS or Medicaid claims) inherit the source's reporting limits, even where no separate flag is shown.

Normative material appears only in the values section (labelled) and in Proposed solutions (each tied to a documented failure).

## 2. Scope, terms and the population

- **Foster care** here means all out-of-home care under a state child-welfare agency: non-relative family homes, kinship homes, group homes, residential treatment facilities and shelters.
- **Orphans.** In the US, very few children in care have no living parent. The relevant group is **legal orphans**: children whose parents' rights have been terminated by a court, many of whom wait in care for adoption. Life-table estimates from federal case-level data show that about **1 in 100 US children** will experience termination of parental rights by age 18. The risk is highest in the first years of life and highest for Native American (nearly 3.0%) and African American (about 1.5%) children, with dramatic variation between states [P14]. **Moderate** (the estimate depends on the quality of federal AFCARS reporting; see Section 9). **[GOV-flag: AFCARS input data are state self-reports.]**
- **No Canadian data, and no paper with a Canadian-affiliated co-author, is used.**
- **Adopted children after adoption** are a second, largely unmonitored group. Once an adoption is final, the child-welfare agency usually has no ongoing role. Section 3.4 covers what happens when adoptive placements break down outside the courts.

## 3. Children going missing from care

### 3.1 What the audits found

- **Scale and reporting.** An HHS Office of Inspector General (OIG) national audit counted **110,446 missing-from-care episodes** from mid-2018 to the end of 2020. State shares of children missing ranged from 0 to 7%. The average time missing ranged from 7 to 96 days by state. **6,619 children were still missing** on 31 December 2020. About 65% of missing children were aged 15 to 17 [A14]. **[GOV-flag: state-reported counts. The 0% to 7% range across states is as likely to reflect differences in definitions and recording as differences in true runaway rates. Agencies face reputational pressure to keep missing counts low, and one state's case-management system could not tell a missing child from a child in a known but unapproved placement (A12).]**
- **Federal reporting duty unmet.** Under the 2014 Act, with the duty in force from September 2016, states must report a missing child in care to law enforcement for entry into the FBI's National Crime Information Center and to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) "immediately, and in no case later than 24 hours" [L1]. A follow-up OIG audit sampled 100 of 74,353 episodes lasting two calendar days or longer (July 2018 to December 2020): **33 were reported on time, 22 late, and 45 never**. Projected, **51,115 episodes were not reported as required and an estimated 34,869 were never reported** [A13]. State agencies "generally lacked adequate systems to readily identify whether or not they had reported" episodes [A13]. **Strong** (statistically projected audit finding). **[GOV-flag: the denominator is itself the states' own count of episodes, so it understates the problem if episodes go unrecorded.]**
- **Barriers.** The national snapshot identified limitations in state data systems, lack of oversight of reporting timeliness, and poor information exchange with federal agencies and law enforcement [A14].
- **A state case study.** In a review of 59 cases from one state where 978 children went missing at some point in 2019, OIG found: 49 of the 59 children had known risk factors for going missing, but only 7 of those 49 case files showed services to reduce that risk. Nearly half of the files showed no evidence that the child was reported missing to law enforcement or to NCMEC. For **one in three children there was no documentation of any health-and-safety check on return**, so no assessment of whether the child had been trafficked while missing. The state's case-management system could not reliably identify missing children without reading each case file [A12]. **Moderate** (case study, not projectable, as OIG states). OIG's recommendations were recorded as implemented in January 2025 [A12]. **[GOV-flag: "implemented" is a status recorded on the basis of the agency's reported actions, not a re-audit of outcomes.]**

### 3.2 What the peer-reviewed research adds

- **Who runs.** National AFCARS data on children aged 12 to 17 (2009) show that the likelihood of a runaway episode **varies by county** even after accounting for child factors. Age, gender, clinical diagnoses, family structure, number of removals, number of placements, manner of removal and case goal predicted running [P22]. Data from 17 states show higher running among older youth, girls and youth with any congregate-care history, and larger racial disparities in counties that use congregate care more. The author specifically highlights **the need for better federal reporting of running away** [P21]. **Moderate.**
- **Why place matters.** County-level variation [P21; P22] means running is partly a property of how local systems operate, not only of the child. That is an oversight question.
- **Reporting gap in the research itself.** No peer-reviewed study was found that measures, independently of agency records, how many children in US care go missing and are never recorded as missing. **GAP.**
- **What youth say.** In a survey of 121 girls and young women in the child-welfare or juvenile-justice systems, of whom 60% had experienced commercial sexual exploitation, youth preferred **unlocked, smaller, more home-like placements closer to their communities**. Youth with exploitation histories were more likely to run [P23]. **Limited** (convenience sample).
- **Intervention evidence.** No randomised or quasi-experimental US evaluation of a missing-from-care prevention programme was opened for this case. One review of running from foster care (Crosland and Dunlap, 2015) was located, but its abstract and text could not be read; it is **UNVERIFIED** and not relied on. **GAP** for tested prevention.

### 3.3 Interpretation

The core oversight failure is not only that children run. It is that **the system often does not know, does not tell the national clearinghouse, and does not check what happened to the child when they return.** Federal law has required all three since the 2014 Act's phased deadlines (2015–2016) [L1]. The audit evidence shows the requirements are widely unmet [A12; A13; A14].

### 3.4 Children who disappear from oversight through adoption breakdown

GAO examined "unregulated child custody transfers" ("rehoming"), in which adoptive parents pass a child to another family outside the courts and the child-welfare system. GAO found that **no federal agency keeps statistics** on these transfers and that "little is known about the prevalence." Over 15 months of observing social-media forums, GAO identified **23 posts** from parents seeking a new family for an adopted child, but could not determine whether transfers occurred. At least 15 states had legislative or other activity on the issue by July 2015; 7 had enacted laws, most commonly criminalising unregulated transfers or restricting advertising of children. GAO noted that federal rules required only 10 hours of pre-adoption training for international adoptions, against at least 27 hours for foster-care adoptions in about half of states [A5]. **GAP** for prevalence; **Limited** for mechanisms (interviews). **[GOV-flag: the 23 posts are an illustrative observation count, not a prevalence estimate.]**

## 4. Abuse and unsafe conditions in foster placements, group homes and residential facilities

### 4.1 Health-and-safety audits of group homes

HHS OIG ran a series of unannounced site-visit audits of group homes that received Title IV-E foster-care funds. In every state reviewed, **the state had completed its required inspections, yet the homes still broke health-and-safety rules**:

| State (audit, date) | Homes visited | Homes not compliant with ≥1 requirement | Background-check failures | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Massachusetts (Dec 2017) | 30 | At least 27 (living-unit rules); 26 buildings and grounds; 20 bathing and toilet; 11 toxic substances; 15 care of residents | 18 homes | [A6] |
| Ohio (Sep 2017) | 30 | 19 | 5 homes (criminal records checks) | [A7] |
| Oklahoma (Sep 2017) | 22 | 17 | not among the summary findings; violations included 12 homes on transportation, 12 on buildings and grounds, 10 on fire safety | [A8] |
| Washington (Mar 2018) | 20 | 20 | 16 facilities | [A9] |

**Strong** for the audited homes (direct observation by auditors). **Limited** for national generalisation, because homes were selected by risk and location factors, not as national samples. **[GOV-flag: these are audit observations, which avoid the self-reporting bias of state data. The underlying problem they expose is that routine state inspection results, which are what federal oversight normally relies on, overstated compliance.]**

Most violations were physical-plant and safety issues, not abuse findings. Their significance for oversight is that **a completed inspection did not mean a safe home**, and that background checks on staff with unsupervised access to children were missing in a large share of homes in three of the four states [A6; A7; A9].

### 4.2 Investigations of abuse allegations involving children in care

- **One large state:** in 36 of 100 sampled foster-care abuse and neglect cases (October 2014 to June 2015), the state did not record and investigate the allegation in line with state requirements, mainly through late notices to the people named and late determinations. Projected: **171 of 474** cases [A10]. **Strong** (projected audit finding). **[GOV-flag: the audit tests process timeliness against the state's own rules; it does not re-investigate whether the abuse findings were correct.]**
- **A second large state:** 46 of 100 sampled cases did not meet requirements, mainly late supervisory approval and undocumented interim investigator–supervisor meetings [A11]. **Strong** for process failures. **[GOV-flag: tests timeliness against the state's own rules; the state agreed the conditions were accurate but disputed that they put children at risk.]**
- **Court findings on tracking.** In the 2015 trial of a class action against one state's foster-care system, the federal district court found that the state's licensing division "does not track child-on-child abuse" in licensed foster-care placements, so there was "no centralized data that shows which foster children have a history of physical or sexual abuse" [C1]. On appeal, the Fifth Circuit **affirmed liability on the general class's claim and on the licensed-foster-care subclass's oversight claim**, but **reversed** the district court's liability findings on placement array and on foster group homes, and vacated and remanded the injunction for modification [C2]. Only the affirmed findings are relied on here.

### 4.3 Residential treatment programmes and restraint deaths

- **Restraint fatalities.** A 26-year study compiled **79 restraint-related deaths** of children and youth in the US (1993 to 2018) across child-welfare, corrections, mental-health and disability services, including community schools. The authors concluded that the deaths resulted from medical, psychological and organisational causes, including "cultures prioritizing control, ignoring risk, using dangerous techniques," and agencies lacking structures to learn and ensure safety [P19]. **Limited** (the cases were found through internet searches of journalism, advocacy and agency sources, so the count is a floor, not a census). There is **no national mandatory registry** of restraint deaths of children in out-of-home care that this case could locate. **GAP.**
- **Private residential programmes.** A 2007 GAO testimony on private residential treatment programmes (wilderness programmes, boot camps, academies) examined thousands of allegations of abuse and closed death cases dating from 1990. GAO did not verify the allegations or evaluate programme benefits [A19]. These programmes include but are not limited to children placed by child-welfare agencies. **Limited** for the foster-care population. **[GOV-flag: allegation counts; unverified by the auditor.]**
- **Is congregate care itself the harm?** Earlier studies linked group-home placement to delinquency. A 2025 study of all youth aged 11 to 17 entering out-of-home care in one state (2015–2018) used pre-placement child-welfare, juvenile-justice and Medicaid records. Accounting for pre-existing behavioural risk **modestly attenuated** the association. The association was strongest for low-risk youth and disappeared for youth with prior delinquency. Delinquency referrals were highest after leaving placement, regardless of placement type [P18]. **Moderate.** This supports two readings at once: low-risk youth should not be placed in congregate care, and, for high-risk youth with few alternatives, raising the *quality and oversight* of congregate care may matter more than eliminating it.

### 4.4 What is still missing

- No nationally representative, independently measured (non-agency) estimate of abuse in US foster homes or residential facilities was found. The federal "maltreatment in foster care" indicator is built from state-reported substantiations. **GAP.**
- A 2024 national OIG survey found nearly one-third of states could not identify patterns of maltreatment in residential facilities, states had limited visibility of maltreatment across multi-state chains and in out-of-state placements, and 13 states did not consistently record in NCANDS whether a maltreated child lived in a residential facility [A21]. **Strong** as a census survey of state agencies. **[GOV-flag: state self-reported capacity; recommendations closed on the basis of ACF actions, not a re-survey.]**
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of whether unannounced inspections, as opposed to scheduled ones, reduce harm in US group homes was found. **GAP.**

## 5. Psychotropic over-medication

### 5.1 Prescribing patterns

- **National rate.** In the first nationally representative survey of children in contact with child welfare (2001–2002; n = 3,114; 87% living at home), **13.5%** were taking psychotropic medication, an estimated **2 to 3 times** the rate in community samples [P2]. **Moderate.**
- **Multiple drugs at once.** In a random sample of 472 medicated youth in foster care in one southwestern state's Medicaid data (July 2004), **41.3%** received three or more psychotropic classes in a single month and **15.9%** received four or more. Antidepressants (56.8%), ADHD drugs (55.9%) and antipsychotics (53.2%) were most common. Use of specific drug classes "varied little by diagnostic grouping." The authors concluded that concomitant treatment "lacks substantive evidence as to its effectiveness and safety" [P1]. **Moderate.**
- **Trends across states.** Medicaid data for 47 states and the District of Columbia (2002–2007; about 686,000 children in foster care annually) show **any antipsychotic use rising from 8.9% to 11.8%**, with increases in 45 states. Polypharmacy (three or more classes for 30+ days) peaked at 5.9% in 2004 and was 5.3% in 2007 [P3]. **Strong** (near-census claims data; trend, not appropriateness).
- **Quality indicators.** In 10 states' 2008 Medicaid data on 144,200 youth receiving antipsychotics, children in foster care were about **2.5 times as likely to receive higher-than-recommended doses** (AOR 2.5) and **2.2 times as likely to receive multiple concurrent antipsychotics** than children in income-based Medicaid. But they were *more* likely to receive psychosocial care before starting and ongoing metabolic monitoring [P5]. **Moderate.** This is an important counterweight: over-use indicators and better follow-up coexist.
- **Recent state data.** One state's data (2012–2017) show high-level polypharmacy (four or more classes including an antipsychotic) rose to 2014 and then stabilised. Neither children in foster care nor other Medicaid children received metabolic monitoring "at an acceptable rate" [P4]. **Limited** (single state).
- **Federal audit evidence.** GAO analysed 2008 Medicaid data in five states and found children in foster care were prescribed psychotropics at higher rates than other Medicaid children, with more indicators of potential health risk. Hundreds of children in foster care and outside it were on **five or more psychotropic drugs at once**, which GAO's experts said no evidence supports. Thousands received doses above guideline maximums, and some children **under 1 year old** were prescribed psychotropics. GAO stressed that higher rates "do not necessarily indicate inappropriate prescribing," because children in care have greater needs and trauma exposure [A1]. **Strong** as an audit analysis of claims data. **[GOV-flag: Medicaid fee-for-service claims only; one state's foster-care data were "unreliable and could not be analyzed," itself a data-quality finding.]**

### 5.2 Oversight of prescribing

- **Law.** Since 2011, each state's health-oversight plan for children in care must include "protocols for the appropriate use and monitoring of psychotropic medications" [L3]. The law does not set the content of those protocols.
- **State policies.** By the time of a 2011 national study, just over half of states (52.7%) had policies or guidelines for psychotropic oversight, with "substantial variation in populations and medications covered" and in mechanisms such as consent and monitoring [P6]. A 2022 legislative review found 23 states had enacted relevant informed-consent laws, but **only two specified all five procedural elements** (history-taking, prescribing, consent and youth assent, notification, and periodic review) [P7]. **Moderate.**
- **Compliance.** OIG sampled 625 children in care in the five states with the highest psychotropic use. **One in three did not receive treatment planning or medication monitoring as their own state required**, and state requirements did not always incorporate professional guidelines [A3]. **Strong** (audit of case files and claims). Both OIG recommendations were later recorded as implemented (December 2023 and January 2026) [A3]. **[GOV-flag: "implemented" reflects the agency's reported actions, not a re-audit of children's care.]**
- **Data barriers.** In seven states reviewed in 2017, four had reduced medication use from 2011 to 2015, two were steady and one lacked data. GAO said these data "cannot be compared across states because states use different methodologies." Officials cited disparate data systems and privacy concerns between child-welfare and Medicaid agencies and managed-care organisations [A2]. **Moderate.** **[GOV-flag: state-reported reductions using non-comparable methods; officials also told GAO that reducing use "may not be appropriate for every child."]**

### 5.3 Has oversight changed prescribing?

- A supportive state oversight programme that reviewed prescriptions (any psychotropic at age 6 or under; two or more at age 7 or older) **without prior authorisation** was followed by a fall in antipsychotic prescription rates among reviewed youth from **52.8% to 39.1% in males and 43.3% to 38.2% in females** (2019–2022), with no increase in the level of care needed [P8]. **Limited** (single state, pre–post, no comparison group, authors run the programme).
- No multi-state causal study of foster-care-specific psychotropic oversight was located and opened. **GAP** for strong causal evidence.

## 6. Caseworker caseloads and turnover

### 6.1 Size of the problem

- **National workforce data.** Using caseworker identifiers in federal data for 46 states (139,921 caseworkers) and supervisors in 43 states (31,124), 2003–2015, the **median caseworker handled 55 cases a year and stayed about 1.8 years**. Annual caseworker turnover in the median state was **14–22%**, and supervisor turnover about 20% [P17]. **Moderate** (the identifiers come from state submissions; "cases" are not standardised across states). **[GOV-flag: derived from state-reported administrative data.]**
- **Court findings in one state.** The 2015 district-court opinion in a class action found that, as of July 2014, 43% of conservatorship caseworkers with at least one child in permanent state custody carried caseloads above the most lenient professional standard before the court (more than 20 children). The court found the agency's count included people "not there," such as workers on leave or in training. It found caseloads "excessive to the point that foster children are placed at an unreasonable risk of harm" and cited the agency's own admission that "high caseloads lead to high worker turnover, further exacerbating high caseloads," with annual caseworker turnover of 26.7% [C1] **[GOV-flag: agency caseload data, which the court found overstated staffing; turnover from a consultant's report]**. The appeals court **affirmed liability on this general-class claim** [C2]. **Strong** as a judicial finding of fact for that state; **Limited** for generalisation.
- **State audit.** A 2018 state audit found "chronic understaffing, overwhelming workloads, high turnover, and a large proportion of inexperienced staff." Caseworker turnover was 23% in 2016, and resignations (13%) were double the state-government average (6%). The audit estimated the agency needed about 35% more field staff (about 769 positions). It reported a union survey in which 57% of child-welfare staff said their caseloads were above the recommended level. It also found children were housed in hotels when no placement was available, and that the agency's informal tracking of this was incomplete: over 60% of hotelled children had no completed needs assessment [A18]. **Moderate** (audit; the 57% figure is a union survey cited by the auditor). **[GOV-flag: state workload-model figures are produced by the agency that benefits from showing need for more staff; the hotel-tracking data were found incomplete by the auditor.]**
- **A court-monitored state.** Independent monitors under a consent decree in one metropolitan area reported that, after caps took effect, 98% of case managers were at or below caps in December 2010 [C3]. By mid-2014, compliance had fallen to 84%, after a rise in referrals and turnover in which **19% of the workforce resigned in nine months** [C4] **[GOV-flag: monitor-verified samples of state data]**. **Moderate** (monitor-verified, but limited to one jurisdiction).

### 6.2 Does it affect children?

- **Caseworker visits.** In one state's administrative data on 3,510 children reunified with their families (2016–2020), **re-entry to care was lower when children received caseworker visits after reunification** [P25]. **Moderate** (observational; visits are not randomly assigned).
- **Continuity.** Court monitors noted a real tension between enforcing caseload caps by redistributing cases and keeping the same worker with a child, and stated that worker continuity affects timely permanency [C3]. One peer-reviewed study on the number of caseworkers per child and permanency (Ryan, Garnier, Zyphur and Zhai, 2006) was located, but no abstract or text could be read; it is **UNVERIFIED** and not relied on.
- **Causal evidence on caseload caps.** No peer-reviewed study using a causal design was found that measures the effect of caseload size on child safety outcomes in US child welfare. **GAP.** The strongest evidence that excessive caseloads harm children is currently **judicial** (a finding of unreasonable risk of harm after a full trial [C1; C2]), not experimental.

## 7. Aging out of care

### 7.1 Outcomes

- **Homelessness.** In a longitudinal study of youth aging out in three Midwestern states, **between 31% and 46%** had been homeless at least once by age 26. Running away while in care, placement instability, being male, physical-abuse history, delinquent behaviour and mental-health symptoms raised the risk [P9]. **Moderate.**
- **Arrest.** In the same study, matched to official records, **46%** were arrested between ages 17–18 and 24, spread evenly across drug, non-violent and violent offences. Bonds to employment and education were associated with lower arrest risk [P13]. **Moderate.**
- **Education.** In the same study and a later California study, 77–85% finished a secondary credential by age 21 and 31–52% enrolled in college, but only about **8% had earned a college degree by age 25** [P12]. **Moderate.**

### 7.2 Extended foster care to 21

- Since 2008, federal law has allowed states to claim Title IV-E reimbursement for youth who stay in care to 19, 20 or 21, if they are in school, training, work or meet other conditions [L2].
- **Education.** Using between-state policy differences in the age youth must leave care (n = 732), **each additional year in care was associated with 46% higher odds of reaching the next level of education** by age 26 [P11]. Across two studies, extended care promotes secondary completion and college entry, but **not** college persistence or degree completion [P12]. **Moderate** (natural-experiment design, but states differ in other ways too).
- **Arrest.** Extended care was associated with a lower risk of a first adult arrest in the first year, with the effect declining over time [P10]. **Moderate.**

### 7.3 Chafee services and money left unspent

- The Chafee programme, created in 1999 [L4] and extended in 2018, funds independent-living services for youth aged 14 to 21, or to 23 in states that have elected the extension [A16]. GAO found that **states returned an average of about 5% of Chafee and education-voucher funds** in fiscal years 2018–2022, about **$8.4 million a year**. In fiscal year 2022, 12 of 51 states returned Chafee funds and 28 returned voucher funds (about $8.9 million in total), and about $670,000 went back to the Treasury even after reallocation [A16]. **Strong** as an audit of financial data. **[GOV-flag: federal financial data; low risk of manipulation, but states may under-report services. GAO heard that caseworkers prioritise serving youth over entering data, so services "were likely underreported" (A16).]**

## 8. Trafficking of youth in care

### 8.1 What the law requires

The Preventing Sex Trafficking and Strengthening Families Act of 2014 [L1] requires states to:
- identify, document and determine services for children in care who are, or are at risk of becoming, sex-trafficking victims;
- report identified victims to law enforcement "immediately, and in no case later than 24 hours";
- report missing or abducted children in care to law enforcement for NCIC entry and to NCMEC within 24 hours;
- screen children who return from a missing episode; and
- report annually the number of identified victims.

### 8.2 Is it implemented?

- **Missing-child reporting:** an estimated 34,869 episodes never reported to NCMEC (Section 3.1) [A13] **[GOV-flag: see Section 3.1]**.
- **Post-return screening:** for one in three children in a state case study, there was no documentation of any return check, and therefore no determination of whether the child had been trafficked while missing [A12] **[GOV-flag: see Section 3.1]**.
- **State reporting models.** A policy analysis of one state that made commercial sexual exploitation of children a reportable form of child abuse described the consequences and implementation challenges of treating exploitation as a child-welfare reporting matter, and recommended ways to improve identification of victims [P24]. **Limited** (policy analysis).
- **Law-enforcement response.** A study of trafficking investigations and interviews in three US communities found that trafficking victims "often do not trust the police and rarely seek their assistance" [P27]. **Limited**. This matters because the 2014 Act relies on law-enforcement reporting as its main protective step.
- **"Normalcy" provisions.** The same Act requires a "reasonable and prudent parent" standard so that children in care can take part in ordinary activities. A state-by-state legal review found significant variation in how states implemented this, and called for research on whether the protections reach youth [P26]. **Limited.**

### 8.3 Does it work?

No peer-reviewed study was found that measures whether the 2014 Act changed rates of trafficking, missing episodes or recovery among youth in care. **GAP.** Measurement is complicated because the Act's screening duties themselves increase recorded counts among children in care. **[GOV-flag: any rise in identified victims after 2014 reflects, at least in part, new screening, not new victimisation.]**

## 9. Gaps in federal reviews, Inspector General audits and state audits

### 9.1 Child and Family Services Reviews (CFSRs)

- CFSRs are the federal government's main oversight tool for state child-welfare systems. In Round 3 (fiscal years 2015–2018), all 51 states were reviewed against seven outcomes using 4,067 case reviews. **Four states met the standard for Safety Outcome 1 (children protected from abuse and neglect); six met the educational well-being outcome; no state met the standard on the other five outcomes.** Systemic-factor results were better, but only 14 states met the standard on foster and adoptive parent licensing, recruitment and retention, and 27 on the statewide information system (so **24 did not**) [A17]. The bar is deliberately high: an outcome is met only if 95% of reviewed cases achieve it, and the Children's Bureau cautions against generalising about national performance from these results. Case-level achievement was higher; for example, 55% of applicable cases achieved Safety Outcome 2 [A17]. **Strong** as review findings. **[GOV-flag: the reviews combine federal and state reviewers, statewide self-assessments and state-submitted data; states that fail must enter improvement plans with potential financial penalties, which creates incentives to manage how performance is presented.]**
- **What the reviews do not check.** OIG found that nearly a third of children in care enrolled in Medicaid in four large states missed at least one required health screening, and just over a quarter received one late. It found that "ACF's reviews do not ensure that children in foster care receive the required screenings," and recommended expanding CFSR scope; ACF initially said it "would consider" this; OIG recorded the recommendation as implemented in November 2023 [A4] **[GOV-flag: 'implemented' reflects reported agency action, not a re-audit of whether screenings now happen on time]**. **Strong** (random samples of 100 children per state). **[GOV-flag: Medicaid claims may miss screenings paid outside Medicaid, which could overstate gaps.]**

### 9.2 The pattern in Inspector General work

Across the audits opened for this case, a common pattern appears:
1. **A required process exists on paper** (inspection, investigation, reporting, treatment plan).
2. **The state reports or believes it is complying** (inspections done; investigations completed).
3. **Independent testing finds a large share of failures** (group homes non-compliant [A6–A9]; investigations late [A10; A11]; missing children not reported [A13]; medication not monitored [A3]; screenings missed [A4]).
4. **Recommendations are closed as "implemented"** on the basis of guidance, webinars or policy changes [A3; A12; A13], **without a published re-audit of outcomes** for the same children. Whether implementation changed outcomes is therefore unknown. **GAP.**

### 9.3 State audits

The state audit opened for this case [A18] shows the same features at state level: management failures, missing data on placement capacity, incomplete tracking of hotel placements, and high caseloads and turnover. A follow-up audit was located but could not be opened (server error); it is **UNVERIFIED** and not relied on. No systematic review of state audit findings on child welfare across all states was found. **GAP.**

## 10. Data-reporting failures

### 10.1 Missing children (NCMEC and NCIC)

See Section 3.1. The audit found states could not readily tell whether they had reported episodes [A13], and one state's system could not identify missing children without reading case files [A12]. The national count of missing-from-care episodes therefore depends on systems that the audits found unreliable. **[GOV-flag.]**

### 10.2 Child maltreatment deaths (NCANDS)

- GAO found that "more children have likely died from maltreatment than are counted" in NCANDS, which is based on **voluntary** state reports. Nearly half of states used only child-welfare agency data, missing deaths of children never known to the agency. GAO's 2011 estimate base was 1,770 deaths in fiscal year 2009 [A15]. **Strong** (audit). **[GOV-flag: NCANDS is voluntary and self-reported; counts depend on which agencies each state draws on.]**
- Follow-up: Congress then required states to describe their data sources for fatality reporting [L3; A15]. One GAO recommendation on routinely sharing analyses was closed **not implemented**, and HHS decided not to collect national near-fatality data after states cited cost and burden [A15].
- A peer-reviewed medical-examiner study in one state (1985–1994) found that vital records under-recorded abuse homicides of children under 11 by **58.7%**, and estimated nationally that about **9,467** child homicides (1985–1996) were due to abuse rather than the 2,973 reported (about 61.6% under-ascertainment) [P20]. **Moderate** (older data; single-state base). No recent peer-reviewed national estimate of under-counting specific to children *in foster care* was found. **GAP.**

### 10.3 Aging-out outcomes (NYTD)

States survey youth at about 17, 19 and 21. For youth who have left care, the federal standard is a **60% participation rate**; for youth still in care, 80% [A20]. Up to 40% of youth who have left care can therefore be missing from outcome data without a penalty. Youth who are homeless, incarcerated or have moved are plausibly harder to reach, which would bias outcomes upward. This is an inference from the design, not a measured finding. **[GOV-flag: penalties of 1–5% of the Chafee allotment for non-compliance create an incentive to meet the participation floor, not to reach the hardest-to-find youth.]** No peer-reviewed study of NYTD non-response bias was located. **GAP.**

### 10.4 Running away and placement data (AFCARS)

AFCARS is the federal case-level foster-care dataset. Peer-reviewed analyses depend on it [P14; P22], and one author calls for "better reporting of running away by the federal government" [P21]. GAO could not analyse one state's foster-care data because they were "unreliable" [A1]. No independent validation study of AFCARS runaway or placement-setting fields was opened for this case. **GAP.**

### 10.5 Statewide information systems

24 of 51 states did not meet the federal standard for their statewide information system in Round 3 [A17] **[GOV-flag: partly self-assessed review]**. GAO found data-sharing barriers between child-welfare and Medicaid systems for psychotropic oversight [A2]. **Strong** as review and audit findings.

## Fair counterarguments

1. **Over-medication or under-treatment?** Higher prescribing among children in care may reflect greater need and trauma [A1; P2]. Children in care also received better follow-up on some quality measures than other Medicaid children [P5]. Others point to doses above guidelines, multiple antipsychotics and infants receiving psychotropics [A1; P5]. Both positions are supported by data. The evidence does not show that most prescribing is inappropriate. It does show a meaningful minority of high-risk regimens and weak monitoring.
2. **Abolish or improve congregate care?** Family-based care is preferred by youth [P23] and is the usual professional preference. But the association between congregate care and delinquency weakens after accounting for prior risk [P18], and some high-need youth have few alternatives. Reasonable people disagree on whether resources should go to closing facilities or to raising their quality and oversight.
3. **Do lawsuits fix systems?** Supporters cite monitored improvements [C3] and accountability models [P15; P16]. Critics note gains can reverse [C4], and the appeals court in one case cut back an injunction it saw as overreaching [C2]. The dissent in that case would have upheld the remedy in full [C2]. No peer-reviewed causal evaluation settles the question.
4. **Is "missing" a child-protection failure or adolescent behaviour?** Most missing children are 15 to 17 [A14] and many return. But the duty to report and check on return applies regardless of reason, and audits show it is often unmet [A12; A13].
5. **Are government counts too high or too low?** Missing, maltreatment and fatality counts are probably too low because they depend on agency detection and voluntary or self-reported data [A13; A15; P20]. Trafficking counts among children in care may be inflated *relative to other children* by targeted screening. Both biases can operate at once.

## 13. Values and judgements (normative, labelled)

*This section states values. It is not a factual finding.*

- **The state as parent.** When the state removes a child from parents, it takes on the duties of a parent. The values question this case raises is whether a parent who did not know where a child was, did not report the child missing, and did not ask what happened on return would be considered adequate. The audits show those failures occur at scale [A12; A13].
- **Paper compliance is not protection.** A recurring pattern is that processes are completed on paper while independent checks find failures (Section 9.2). A reasonable value position is that oversight should be judged by verified outcomes for children, not by completed forms.
- **Proportionate caution about blame.** Failures documented here are findings against systems and, where cited, specific agencies as found by auditors or courts. They are not findings against individual caseworkers, who work under caseloads a court found harmful [C1].
- **Both parents.** Policies affecting children in care should support both parents equally and should not frame family need in gendered terms.
- **Honesty about uncertainty.** Many of the most important questions (how many children in care are abused, how many go missing unrecorded, whether reporting laws work) are **GAP**s. Claims beyond the evidence, in either direction, should be avoided.

## 15. Proposed solutions (normative; each tied to a documented failure)

*Each proposal names the failure it answers (by section), the evidence behind it, and an evidence-strength rating: **High** (causal or consistent multi-study evidence of improved outcomes), **Medium** (observational or quasi-experimental evidence, or a documented outcome in a court-monitored or audited setting), **Low** (single pre–post study, or evidence only that the failure exists). Proposals with no evaluation of their effect are marked **UNTESTED**. Where a proposal is UNTESTED, the cited evidence documents the failure, not the fix.*

**S1. Automated missing-child reporting with monthly reconciliation.** *Failure: Section 3.1 and 10.1.* States should flag a child as missing in the case-management system, which then generates the law-enforcement (NCIC) and NCMEC reports, and should reconcile their missing-episode lists against NCMEC intake records each month, with results reported to the federal agency. *Evidence:* States "generally lacked adequate systems" to know whether they had reported [A13]; one state's system could not identify missing children without reading case files [A12]. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED.**

**S2. Documented return interview and trafficking screen within a fixed period, verified by sample audit.** *Failure: Sections 3.1 and 8.2.* Every return from a missing episode should trigger a recorded health-and-safety check and trafficking screen, with a random sample audited each year. *Evidence:* Already required by law [L1]; one in three children in a case study had no documented return check [A12]. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED.**

**S3. Risk-based runaway prevention using known predictors, with priority for family-like placements.** *Failure: Section 3.2.* Youth with prior running, many placements, congregate-care history or mental-health needs should receive a prevention plan, and placement decisions should favour unlocked, smaller, home-like settings closer to the youth's community. *Evidence:* Predictors are well documented [P9; P21; P22]; youth preferences [P23]; in the case study, only 7 of 49 at-risk children received risk-reduction services [A12]. No evaluated US prevention programme was found (Section 3.2). *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED.**

**S4. Independent, unannounced inspections of group homes and residential facilities, with published results.** *Failure: Section 4.1.* Inspection should be done by a body independent of the placing agency, without notice, and should include verification of staff background checks. *Evidence:* Unannounced auditor visits found violations in homes that had passed routine state inspection in all four audited states, including missing background checks [A6–A9]. That is a documented detection outcome. Whether this reduces harm to children has not been measured. *Strength:* **Medium** for detection; harm reduction **UNTESTED**. Counted below as tested (detection documented).

**S5. Central tracking of child-on-child abuse and restraint incidents, and a national report of restraint deaths in out-of-home care.** *Failure: Sections 4.2 and 4.3.* *Evidence:* A federal court found one state kept no central data on child-on-child abuse in licensed care, a finding left standing on appeal [C1; C2]; restraint deaths are known only from searches of public sources [P19], whose authors recommend greater transparency. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED.**

**S6. Threshold-triggered psychotropic review with metabolic-monitoring checks.** *Failure: Section 5.* Review by a child psychiatrist should be triggered automatically for any psychotropic at age 6 or under, two or more psychotropics at age 7 or over, more than one antipsychotic, or a dose above guideline maximums, and baseline and ongoing metabolic monitoring should be tracked. *Evidence:* A state programme using these age and count triggers was followed by lower antipsychotic use without higher levels of care [P8]; quality measures for over-dosing, multiple antipsychotics and metabolic monitoring already exist and show gaps [P5; P4]; audit found one in three children lacked required monitoring [A3]. *Strength:* **Low** (single state, pre–post, no comparison group). Tested.

**S7. A uniform informed-consent procedure covering all five procedural elements.** *Failure: Section 5.2.* States should set in law or policy how history is gathered, how prescribing decisions are made, who consents and how youth assent, who is notified, and when consent is reviewed. *Evidence:* Only 2 states specify all five elements [P7]; 2011 policies varied widely [P6]. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED** for effects on children.

**S8. Enforceable caseload standards counted only on active, trained workers, with independent verification and a retention plan.** *Failure: Section 6.1.* *Evidence:* A court found caseload counts included people "not there" and that excessive caseloads created an unreasonable risk of harm [C1; C2]. Under a monitored consent decree, 98% of case managers reached caps **[GOV-flag]**, alongside the lowest maltreatment-in-care rate on record in that jurisdiction [C3]; compliance fell to 84% when turnover rose, leading to a corrective plan including retention measures [C4]. *Strength:* **Medium** for achievability under court monitoring; **Low** for effect on child safety (single jurisdiction, no comparison group). Tested.

**S9. Caseworker visits after reunification as a funded standard.** *Failure: Section 6.2.* *Evidence:* Re-entry to care was lower when children received post-reunification visits [P25]. *Strength:* **Medium** (large administrative cohort; observational). Tested.

**S10. Extended foster care to 21 in every state, with the option to return.** *Failure: Section 7.* *Evidence:* Each extra year in care was associated with 46% higher odds of progressing in education [P11]; extended care promotes secondary completion and college entry [P12] and was associated with lower first-year arrest risk [P10]. Federal law already allows reimbursement [L2]. *Strength:* **Medium** (natural experiment; the strongest outcome evidence among these proposals). Tested.

**S11. Federal follow-up with states that return Chafee funds.** *Failure: Section 7.3.* *Evidence:* About 5% of Chafee and voucher funds were returned on average in 2018–2022, despite unmet need [A16] **[GOV-flag]**; GAO recommended a plan to address state barriers, which HHS accepted [A16]. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED.**

**S12. Strengthen aging-out data: higher NYTD participation for youth who have left care, required non-response analysis, and linkage to administrative records.** *Failure: Section 10.3.* *Evidence:* The current standard allows up to 40% non-response among youth who have left care [A20]. Linking youth to official arrest records is feasible, as a peer-reviewed study did [P13]. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED** as a national reform.

**S13. Multi-source child-fatality reporting that records whether the child was in care.** *Failure: Section 10.2.* All states should draw on medical-examiner, vital-records, law-enforcement and child-death-review data, not only child-welfare records. *Evidence:* Medical-examiner data revealed large under-ascertainment of abuse homicides [P20]; GAO found nearly half of states used only child-welfare data [A15]. *Strength:* **Medium** that multi-source data find more deaths [P20]; effect on prevention **UNTESTED**. Counted below as UNTESTED.

**S14. Outcome re-audits before audit recommendations are closed.** *Failure: Section 9.2.* Inspector General recommendations on children's safety should be closed only after a follow-up sample shows changed outcomes, not on the basis of guidance or webinars. *Evidence:* Recommendations were closed on the basis of guidance, webinars or policy changes [A3; A12; A13]. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED.**

**S15. Verify, by sample re-audit, that CFSR Round 4 actually detects late or missed health screenings, and add missing-episode handling.** *Failure: Section 9.1.* *Evidence:* OIG found the reviews did not ensure required screenings; its recommendation to expand them was recorded as implemented in November 2023 on the basis of agency action, not a re-audit [A4]; no state met the standard on five of seven outcomes in Round 3 [A17] **[GOV-flag]**. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED.**

**S16. Regulate custody transfers of adopted children and fund post-adoption support.** *Failure: Section 3.4.* *Evidence:* No federal agency tracks unregulated transfers; 7 states had enacted laws by mid-2015, and officials told GAO that post-adoption services were a key need [A5]. The effect of these laws has not been evaluated. *Strength:* **Low. UNTESTED.**

**Count:** 16 proposed solutions. **11 are UNTESTED** (S1, S2, S3, S5, S7, S11, S12, S13, S14, S15, S16). **5 have some tested evidence**: S4 (detection only), S6, S8, S9 and S10. None reaches **High**. The best-evidenced proposal is S10 (extended care to 21).
