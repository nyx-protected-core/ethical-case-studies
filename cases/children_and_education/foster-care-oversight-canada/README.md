# Oversight of children in care in Canada: deaths, disappearances, Indigenous over-representation, unlicensed placements, aging out, trafficking and data gaps

Date: 2026-11-19

---

## Scope and ground rules

This case covers how well Canadian systems watch over children and youth in the care of child-welfare authorities ("in care"), including children with no living or available parents who are permanent wards. It works at the policy and population level only. It contains no individual stories, no first-person accounts and no identifying details of any child.

- **Source tags.** **[PR]** peer-reviewed study or review. **[REC]** official record: a statute, court or tribunal decision, coroner report, child-advocate or ombudsperson report, commission report or auditor-general report. **[GOV]** official government statistics or programme pages, cited as records, each with a **[GOV-flag]**.
- **[GOV-flag].** Every figure produced by a federal, provincial or territorial government, or by a body that is funded by and reports to one, carries this flag beside it. It means: *this number counts what an agency recorded, under that agency's own definitions, at a time when the agency had reasons (funding formulas, political scrutiny, liability, changes in tracking rules) that could push the count up or down.* Where a peer-reviewed audit of the data exists, it is cited next to the figure. Advocates, ombudspersons, coroners and auditors general are independent officers of their legislatures, but their numbers usually come from ministry files, so they are flagged too.
- **Evidence strength.** **Strong**: consistent findings across several designs, including population-level data. **Moderate**: consistent observational or administrative findings with known biases. **Limited**: single studies, small or selected samples, or records of a few cases. **GAP**: no adequate evidence found. Nothing here is "Strong" for a *causal* claim unless a causal design is named.
- **UNVERIFIED** marks a claim or source that could not be opened or confirmed during this review. Such items are not relied on.
- **Fault.** A named government body or agency is described as having failed only where a court, tribunal, coroner, advocate, ombudsperson or auditor made that finding, and the finding is cited to the primary document.
- **Language.** "Parents" and "families" are used throughout. Historical terms ("Aboriginal") are kept only when quoting a source's own category.

## 1. Children who die or go missing in care

### 1.1 Facts

**Ontario: the Chief Coroner's Expert Panel (2018) [GOV-flag on counts].** The Office of the Chief Coroner identified 12 young people who died between 1 January 2014 and 31 July 2017 while in residential placements and in the care of a Children's Aid Society or Indigenous Child Wellbeing Society. All 12 had a history of mental-health problems, and 8 of the 12 were Indigenous [REC-1]. The panel's findings, which are findings of a coroner-appointed expert panel, include:
- On average the young people had about 12 placements over their lives, and many were placed far from their home communities [REC-1].
- "There is currently no way to monitor and track the length of young people's placements or the number of placement transfers they have at the systemic level" [REC-1].
- Many licensed and unlicensed residences were never inspected by the ministry, because an operator with several homes could be licensed on an inspection of 10% of them. Licensing checked operational standards, and "there is no process to monitor the quality of care" [REC-1].
- Serious-occurrence reports were "often not substantial and, in some cases, inaccurate", and their trends had not been monitored at provincial level. Case files were so disjointed that the panel judged the ministry "was equally unable to understand the pathways through the various systems" [REC-1].
- The total cost of supporting young people in the child-protection system "is not recorded centrally" [REC-1].

**Strength: Moderate** as a documented record of oversight gaps in one province. It is a review of 12 deaths, chosen because they occurred in residential placements, so it cannot give rates and was not designed to compare with young people who did not die.

**Manitoba: Advocate for Children and Youth roll-up, 2019–20 [GOV-flag].** The Advocate received 198 child-death notifications from the Chief Medical Examiner in the year: 170 for children aged 0–17, 28 for young adults aged 18–20, and 13 for children in the care of child and family services [REC-2]. Of 71 reviews completed that year, the manner of death was suicide in 25 (35%), undetermined in 18, accidental in 13, natural in 8 and homicide in 4 (6%). The Advocate notes that natural deaths are under-represented because most are routed elsewhere [REC-2]. Service-delivery concerns were very common in the reviewed files: assessment concerns in 66 of 71 reviews (93%), planning concerns in 59 of 71 (83%) and service-provision concerns in 62 of 71 (86%) [REC-2]. The Advocate also reports that suicide had become the leading manner of death for young people aged 10–17 in Manitoba [REC-2].
- **Flag.** These are counts of deaths the Advocate was notified of and chose to review under its statutory scope. The "concern" rates describe files of children who died. They are not rates of failure across all child-welfare files, and they have no comparison group.

**Alberta: Child and Youth Advocate [GOV-flag].** The Advocate is required to review the death or serious injury of any young person who was receiving child-intervention services at the time or within the previous two years. For April 2023 to March 2024 the office received 83 notifications (68 deaths, 15 serious injuries) and completed and published reviews for 47 deaths and one serious injury; 35 of the 47 young people who died were Indigenous, and reviews of 15 more were on hold because of police investigations and court proceedings [REC-3]. Of the 47 deaths, 20 were recorded as accidental (10 of them drug- or alcohol-related), 6 as suicide, 8 as medical, 4 as undetermined, 3 as victims of violence and 6 pending [REC-3]. Only 12 of the 48 young people were actually in care at the time; 20 were receiving other child-intervention services and 16 had received services in the prior two years [REC-3].
- **Flag.** The count depends on the two-year look-back window and the definition of "receiving services". Alberta's figures are therefore not comparable with Manitoba's or Ontario's. The office's 2024–25 annual report could not be read on this run (it timed out); figures from that year are not used here (**UNVERIFIED**).

**Population-level risk (Manitoba linked data).** A population study linked child-welfare, health and vital-statistics records for every child aged 5–17 entering care in Manitoba for the first time between 1997 and 2006 (n = 8,279) and compared them with 353,050 children never in care. Children in care had higher rates of suicide (adjusted relative risk 3.54, 95% CI 2.11–5.95) and of attempted suicide (aRR 2.11, 1.84–2.43). Within the in-care group, suicide attempts and hospital admissions were highest *before* entry into care and fell afterwards (aRR for attempts after entry 0.27, 0.21–0.34) [PR-1]. **Strength: Moderate.** It is population-level and linked, but observational. The pre/post pattern is important: it means the higher risk is at least partly present before the state takes custody, and it does not show that care itself causes suicide.

**Missing from care.** No province publishes a peer-reviewed or audited count of children reported missing from care over time. The Quebec special commission (Section 4) treated running away (*fugue*) from rehabilitation centres as a major risk, including for sexual exploitation [REC-12]. **GAP:** a Canadian rate of missing-from-care episodes, their length, and what happened during them. No Canadian equivalent was found of a national audit of whether missing children in care are reported to police or a central registry.

**GAP: British Columbia.** The British Columbia Representative for Children and Youth publishes reviews of critical injuries and deaths, but no aggregate figures from those reports were opened during this review, so none are used.

### 1.2 What these records show and do not show
- **Consistent across three provinces:** the young people who die are disproportionately Indigenous, often adolescents with mental-health or substance-use needs, and the reviewing bodies repeatedly find gaps in assessment, planning, placement tracking and inspection. **Moderate.**
- **Not shown:** that the death rate of children in care is rising or falling, or how it compares between provinces. Each office counts a different population with a different window, and none of these counts has been audited by independent researchers. **GAP.**

## 2. Over-representation of Indigenous children

### 2.1 Facts

**Census share [GOV-flag].** According to Statistics Canada's release of the 2021 Census, Indigenous children accounted for 53.8% of all children in foster care, while nationally they accounted for 7.7% of all children 14 years of age and younger. Of children in private households, Indigenous children were more likely to be in foster care than non-Indigenous children (3.2% versus 0.2%) [GOV-1]. **Flag.** The census relies on self-identification and on how a household reports a foster child. It is a one-day count of foster children in private households, not a count of everyone who passes through care, and it does not include children in group or residential (treatment) care. It has not been independently audited against provincial records in any source found here.

**Peer-reviewed findings on the same question.**
- The national CIS-2019, published in a peer-reviewed journal, estimated 299,171 maltreatment-related investigations in Canada in 2019 (48.22 per 1,000 children). 34% were substantiated, 20% were transferred to ongoing services, and an out-of-home placement was made in 5% [PR-5] **[GOV-flag: federally funded data collection]**. **Strong** as a national estimate of *investigations*. Ontario data were collected in 2018 and Quebec used separate procedures, so it is not a single uniform national sample.
- **Ontario (OIS-2013).** First Nations children were 2.5% of Ontario's child population but 7.4% of maltreatment-related investigations, with an investigation rate about three times that for White children. The over-representation was largest for neglect and exposure to intimate-partner violence, and rates of substantiation, court and placement were also higher [PR-4]. **Moderate** (one province, bivariate analysis).
- **Why the gap exists, statistically.** An analysis of the 1998 national incidence study found Indigenous ("Aboriginal") children twice as likely to be placed in foster care, and that this placement gap was *explained statistically* by socioeconomic, child, parent and maltreatment characteristics [PR-3]. The authors note that these factors "may reflect the multiple disadvantages experienced by Aboriginal families" [PR-3]. In plain terms, once household and case characteristics are accounted for, Indigenous identity on its own did not explain the placement gap in that data. **Moderate.** The data are from 1998, the measures are worker-reported, and controlling for poverty-linked factors does not show that those factors, rather than bias in how they are judged, drive decisions.

**Tribunal finding: the federal funding design (2016 CHRT 2).** The Canadian Human Rights Tribunal found that the federal government's design, management and control of the First Nations Child and Family Services program and its funding formulas discriminated against First Nations children and families on reserve and in the Yukon. Among the adverse effects it found: funding formulas based on "flawed assumptions", fixed and inadequate funding for operations and prevention, and an "incentive to bring children into care because eligible maintenance expenditures are reimbursable at cost". It also found funding levels unadjusted for inflation since 1995, a failure to coordinate with other federal services, and a "narrow definition and inadequate implementation of Jordan's Principle" [REC-7, para. 458]. **This is a tribunal finding of fact, the strongest record-level evidence in this case that a government's own funding rules pushed the count of children in care upward.**

**Compensation orders.** In 2019 CHRT 39 the tribunal found compensation warranted for First Nations children unnecessarily removed from their homes, and for children affected by the narrow application of Jordan's Principle, under both the pain-and-suffering head and the "wilful and reckless" head of the Canadian Human Rights Act, each capped by statute at $20,000 [REC-8]. *The orders section of the ruling could not be fully read on this run, so the exact per-person total (commonly reported as $40,000) is* **UNVERIFIED** *here.* The Federal Court approved a class settlement of those claims on 24 October 2023; the written reasons, *Moushoom v. Canada*, 2023 FC 1533 (20 November 2023), record that Canada will pay $23,343,940,000 under the final settlement agreement [REC-15].

**Federal statute (Bill C-92).** *An Act respecting First Nations, Inuit and Métis children, youth and families*, S.C. 2019, c. 24, received Royal Assent on 21 June 2019 [REC-9]. It sets national standards. Among them:
- Preventive services are to be given priority where consistent with the child's best interests (s. 14).
- A child "must not be apprehended solely on the basis of his or her socio-economic conditions, including poverty, lack of adequate housing or infrastructure or the state of health of his or her parent or the care provider" (s. 15).
- Placements follow an order of priority: a parent, then another adult family member, then an adult of the same Indigenous group, then another Indigenous adult, then any other adult (s. 16(1)).
- On data, the Minister "may" enter into agreements with provinces and Indigenous governing bodies on the collection and sharing of information (s. 28). This is discretionary, not a duty.
- A review of the Act's operation is required every five years (s. 31) [REC-9].

**Supreme Court reference (2024 SCC 5).** On 9 February 2024 the Supreme Court of Canada unanimously held that the Act as a whole is constitutionally valid under Parliament's jurisdiction over "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians" (s. 91(24), Constitution Act, 1867). It held that s. 21 validly incorporates Indigenous laws by reference and that s. 22(3), under which those laws prevail over conflicting provincial laws, simply restates federal paramountcy [REC-10].

**Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Calls to Action 1–5 (2015).** Under the "Child welfare" heading, the Commission called on governments to [REC-11]:
1. Reduce the number of Indigenous children in care by monitoring neglect investigations, resourcing communities to keep families together where safe, training investigators on residential-school history, and requiring decision-makers to consider that history.
2. Prepare and publish annual reports on the number of Indigenous children in care compared with non-Indigenous children, the reasons for apprehension, spending on prevention and care, and the effectiveness of interventions.
3. Fully implement Jordan's Principle.
4. Enact federal Indigenous child-welfare legislation with national standards.
5. Develop culturally appropriate parenting programs for Indigenous families.

**Status check.** Call 4 was answered in form by S.C. 2019, c. 24 [REC-9]. Call 2 asks for exactly the national annual data that Section 7 shows still do not exist in comparable form. No peer-reviewed evaluation was found that measures whether the 2019 Act has reduced the number of Indigenous children in care. **GAP.**

### 2.2 Evidence strength
- That Indigenous, and especially First Nations, children are heavily over-represented at every stage from investigation to placement: **Strong** (census, two cycles of incidence studies, provincial studies and tribunal findings all agree, though each has its own measurement weaknesses).
- That the gap is driven mainly by poverty and household-level disadvantage, and by funding rules that rewarded placement over prevention on reserve: **Moderate** (statistical explanation in [PR-3]; tribunal fact-finding in [REC-7]).
- That the 2019 Act or the tribunal orders have reduced over-representation: **GAP** (not yet measured).

## 3. Hotels, offices and unlicensed placements

### 3.1 Facts, province by province

**British Columbia.**
- A 2016 joint report of the Representative for Children and Youth and the ministry found that 117 children and youth in care were placed in hotels between November 2014 and October 2015, in 131 placements. The average stay was 2.7 days, 55% lasted a single night, and 52% of the children were Indigenous ("Aboriginal" in the report), compared with about 60% of all children in care [REC-4; GOV-flag].
- **The tracking problem is in the record itself.** The ministry could record hotel stays electronically from 1996, but "in the absence of any policy or procedures on the recording of such stays, data gathered prior to November 2014 is unreliable". A 2014 survey and internal data search had suggested "fewer than 20" hotel placements a year, which the later manual tracking showed to be a large undercount. The report adds that "every request made by a social worker for a hotel placement has been approved", and that full compliance with the new reporting directive could not be verified [REC-4].
- **Current ministry figures [GOV-flag].** The ministry's public data page reports 6 children placed in hotels in 2024/25, 5 of them Indigenous, or about one day in every 51,116 days of care [GOV-2]. **Flag, with two specific problems.** First, from 1 January 2024 the ministry changed the rule so that hotel stays "are now recorded only when no other appropriate resources are available". That narrows what counts, so figures before and after 2024 are not comparable. Second, the same page reports 6 children and, under reasons for stay, "No resource available (10 children/youth)". It later calls these "10 hotel placements" and notes that some children had multiple stays, and that reason-counts may not add to the child total. The numbers are consistent with 6 children and 10 placements; the remaining fault is the reason line's label "children/youth" for what the duration breakdown treats as placements [GOV-2]. No independent audit of these figures was found. **GAP.**

**Manitoba.**
- A 2009 report of the Office of the Children's Advocate reviewed progress on its earlier Hotel Review (2000) and Shelter Review (2004), which together made 90 recommendations [REC-5]. It found:
  - A hotel-reduction strategy launched in November 2006 removed all children from hotels by 31 July 2007. However, sibling groups were exempt, and high-needs youth were "once again" appearing in hotel statistics.
  - The rush out of hotels shifted strain to emergency shelters. Winnipeg shelter facilities rose from 42 (2003/04) to 60 (May 2008), and shelter cost rose from $271 to $377 per child per day.
  - There were no standards specifically regulating short-term emergency facilities.
  - There was no province-wide system for sharing vacant foster-bed information across the 25 agencies.
  - 81% of children in care were Aboriginal [REC-5; GOV-flag on all counts, which came from departmental data].
- **Strength: Moderate** as a record. The report is from 2009. No more recent Manitoba advocate or auditor report on hotel use was opened during this review. **GAP** for current Manitoba hotel figures.

**Ontario.**
- The Ontario Ombudsman opened a systemic investigation on 5 September 2024, on his own initiative, into children's aid societies placing children and youth in "hotels, motels, offices and trailers", including spaces in agencies' own offices. Societies described these placements as a last resort, used for lack of appropriate housing, especially for young people with special needs [REC-6]. When checked for this review, the Ombudsman's site still listed the investigation as ongoing, and no final report was found. **GAP** for the Ombudsman's findings.
- The coroner's expert panel (Section 1) found that many licensed and unlicensed residences were not inspected, because multi-home operators could be licensed on a 10% sample of their homes [REC-1].

**Nova Scotia (Auditor General, May 2024).** The audit found that [REC-13]:
- The number of children in "temporary emergency arrangements" rose from 36 in 2017–18 to 138 in 2022–23, and their annual cost from $4.6 million to $27.9 million.
- These arrangements "are not licensed or inspected by the Department".
- The average stay was over eight months, although they are meant to last no more than four days.
- More than 1,900 critical incidents or serious occurrences in care homes had been reported to the department with "no analysis completed to determine causes, trends, or patterns".
- The department "does not know how many allegations of child abuse or neglect were reported by children living in child and youth care homes".
- Agreements with third-party providers contained no performance standards for the care of children.

**This is an audit finding, and the strongest record in this section. Strength: Strong** for the existence of the oversight failures in that province in that period. The audit's counts come from departmental files [GOV-flag].

**Quebec, Alberta, Saskatchewan and the other provinces and territories.** No auditor-general or advocate report on hotel or unlicensed placements was opened for these jurisdictions during this review. **GAP** (this does not mean the practice is absent).

### 3.2 Pattern across provinces
- **The same cycle appears in every province with records.** A shortage of foster homes and treatment beds for sibling groups and high-needs adolescents leads to emergency use of hotels, offices or unlicensed "arrangements". Those settings are outside licensing and inspection, and counting them depends on ad hoc manual tracking or on definitions the ministry can change [REC-4; REC-5; REC-6; GOV-2; REC-13]. **Moderate** (consistent records across BC, Manitoba, Ontario and Nova Scotia, but no peer-reviewed cross-provincial study).
- **No peer-reviewed study was found that measures outcomes for children placed in Canadian hotels or unlicensed settings, compared with similar children in licensed care. GAP.**

## 4. What advocates, ombudspersons, coroners and auditors general found

This section collects oversight findings that are not specific to hotels. All are records, not peer-reviewed research. Their case counts come from ministry or agency files [GOV-flag].

**Ontario Auditor General, value-for-money audit of Children's Aid Societies (2015).** The audit sampled files at 7 of Ontario's 47 societies [REC-14]. Its findings:
- None of the investigations reviewed was completed within the required 30 days. On average they were completed more than seven months after the report was received.
- In more than one-third of investigations, safety assessments were not done or not done on time.
- In more than half the reviewed files where abuse was alleged, societies did not check the Ontario Child Abuse Register.
- In more than half the reopened cases, the risk factors behind the reopening were present when the case was first closed, and reopening came on average within 68 days.
- For almost one-third of children in care reviewed, the reason for the specific placement was not clearly documented or not documented at all. In about one-quarter, plans of care were not completed within 30 days of placement.
- Private visits with children in care missed the legislated time frames in about 10% of cases.
- Caseloads ranged from 8 to 32 family-protection cases per worker per month across societies.

**Strength: Strong** as an audit finding of non-compliance in the sampled societies, and **Limited** for generalising to all 47.

*Follow-up status.* No later Ontario Auditor General value-for-money audit of children's aid societies was found. Reports of later non-auditor reviews were not opened and are not used.

**Nova Scotia Auditor General (2024).** See Section 3 [REC-13]. In addition, the audit found:
- Social workers were not meeting children within seven days of placement, or every 30 days, as policy requires.
- Plans of care were missing or late.
- Department policy "does not require documentation to explain placement decisions".
- Quality assurance looked only at closed or transferred files.
- Inspections of care homes did not verify that staff background checks were renewed [REC-13].

**Ontario coroner's expert panel (2018).** Placement histories could not be tracked at system level, serious-occurrence reports were unreliable, and costs were not recorded centrally [REC-1]. See Section 1.

**Manitoba Advocate (2019–20).** Assessment, planning and service-provision concerns were found in 83–93% of the 71 death reviews completed [REC-2]. See Section 1.

**Alberta Child and Youth Advocate.** Death and injury reviews are published individually, and consolidated reports make recommendations to government [REC-3]. See Section 1.

**Quebec: Special Commission on the Rights of the Child and Youth Protection (Laurent Commission), final report, April 2021** [REC-12]:
- Reports to youth protection more than doubled between 1995–96 and 2018–19, from 49,388 to 105,644, or from about 29.6 to 67.3 per 1,000 children. In 2018–19, 10.5% of reports led to the protection service taking charge [REC-12; GOV-flag: these are counts of reports, which rise with mandatory-reporting rules and public awareness as well as with need].
- The commission found a shortage of foster families leading to instability, and recruitment criteria that seemed too strict [REC-12].
- It recommended an independent Commissioner for the Well-being and Rights of Children, supported by a deputy commissioner responsible for Indigenous children, and a children's rights charter [REC-12].
- It agreed with an earlier Quebec special commission on the sexual exploitation of minors that rehabilitation centres "can become recruitment places" for pimps, and that conditions there may be experienced as detention, which leads youth to run away (*fugues*), "a major risk for them" [REC-12].
- It found a lack of service planning "based on evidence" [REC-12].

### 4.1 What the oversight bodies agree on
- **Common findings:** missed contact standards, late or absent plans of care, undocumented placement reasons, serious-incident data collected but not analysed, and placement settings outside licensing and inspection. **Strong** as a pattern in records from at least four provinces.
- **Limits of the oversight system itself:**
  - Ontario's Ombudsman investigation of unlicensed placements had no published findings when checked.
  - The Ontario Auditor General's only child-protection audit found here is from 2015.
  - Advocates in different provinces review different populations, with different look-back windows, so their counts cannot be pooled.
  - **No source was found that tracks whether recommendations from these bodies were implemented and with what effect, across provinces. GAP.**

## 5. Youth aging out of care

### 5.1 Facts

**Quebec: the EDJeP longitudinal study.** This is described by its authors as the first longitudinal and representative cohort study of youth leaving care in Quebec. They found that youth leaving care had significant educational delays compared with peers in the general population. Nearly half experienced residential instability in the months after leaving placement, and 20% had at least one episode of homelessness [PR-11]. **Moderate** (representative cohort in one province; the abstract does not give the comparison group's homelessness rate).

**British Columbia: transitional services and later outcomes.** Among 217 people who use drugs in Vancouver and reported having aged out of care, those who had used 4–7 types of transitional service while aging out had lower odds of current homelessness (adjusted OR 0.29) and of daily drug use (AOR 0.35) than those who used one or none. Unmet demand for each service type ranged from 64.8% to 78.4% [PR-12]. **Limited.** The sample is a drug-using cohort, the design is cross-sectional with recalled service use, and youth who were already doing better may have used more services. It shows association, not effect.

**Manitoba: outcomes of being placed in care, linked data.** A linked-data study of children served by child-protection agencies in Manitoba from 2007 to 2018 compared those placed in care with those who had protection concerns but stayed home. It used an instrumental-variable design, with each agency's placement rate as the instrument. Children placed in care had higher odds of teen pregnancy, teen birth and incarceration (First Nations agencies: OR 3.64 for incarceration; other agencies: OR 1.19, with a reported CI of 1.19–8.04 that is inconsistent with its own point estimate). Results for First Nations agencies were often not significant [PR-2]. **Limited to Moderate.** The design is stronger than most in this field, but the source opened is a conference abstract. The full paper was not found, and the confidence interval reported for one estimate is internally inconsistent. **Partly UNVERIFIED** pending a full paper.

**Ontario Auditor General (2015) on the extended-care program then in force.** In almost half the files reviewed there was no evidence that youth in the Continued Care and Support for Youth program were actively preparing for independent living. In some cases the required agreements were missing or unsigned. In 2013 the ministry had removed the requirement that youth work toward agreed goals in order to keep receiving support [REC-14]. **Strong** as an audit finding for the sampled files.

**Systematic evidence (mostly non-Canadian).**
- A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized and quasi-experimental studies of supports for young people leaving care found 14 studies. All but one were from the United States, and two were of extended care. It rated certainty of evidence "very low" in every meta-analysis and found a significant risk of bias in every included study. It concluded that "while some interventions showed promise, particularly extended care, the scope and strength of included evidence is insufficient to recommend any included approach" [PR-13]. **This is the best available summary, and it says the effect of aging-out programs is not established.**
- A 2022 systematic review of independent-living programs and services in Canada and the United States exists [PR-10]. Its abstract and full text could not be opened on this run, so its findings are not used. **UNVERIFIED.**

### 5.2 Policy changes since 2023 (government program pages) [GOV-flag on all program descriptions]
- **Ontario:** the "Ready, Set, Go" program, launched 1 April 2023, lets youth remain in care until age 23 (previously 21). It raises monthly support from $850 to $1,800 at 18, $1,500 at 19, $1,000 at 20 and 21, and $500 at 22. It also begins life-skills planning at age 13 [GOV-3].
- **British Columbia:** the SAJE program provides supports and services to eligible young adults with care experience until their 27th birthday. The province states that these supports are now enshrined in legislation [GOV-4].
- **Alberta:** the Transition to Adulthood Program serves eligible young adults aged 18 to 23, with financial supports ending the day before age 22. Separate education funding is available on application up to age 25 [GOV-5].
- **Quebec:** the Laurent Commission repeated an earlier recommendation to widen the criteria of the youth-qualification program (*programme qualification des jeunes*) [REC-12]. The current Quebec age limits were not verified from a primary source. **UNVERIFIED.**
- **Evaluation status: GAP.** No peer-reviewed evaluation of the outcomes of Ontario's Ready, Set, Go, British Columbia's SAJE or Alberta's program was found. The government pages describe what is offered, not what it achieved.

### 5.3 Evidence strength
- That youth leaving care in Canada face high rates of housing instability and educational delay: **Moderate** (EDJeP; consistent with audits).
- That extending care or transitional supports improves Canadian outcomes: **Limited** (one cross-sectional BC study). Internationally the effect is **promising but very low certainty** [PR-13].
- National outcome data on Canadian care leavers: **GAP** (no national cohort).

## 6. Trafficking of youth in care (aggregate level)

### 6.1 Facts
- **Ontario police-file study.** Researchers worked with a regional police service and two child-welfare agencies to review files of women and girls known to police as sex-trafficking victims. The project page and a research summary report 223 cases, of which 52 had child-welfare involvement and 102 did not (52 + 102 = 154, so 69 cases are unaccounted for in those two sources); the average age at recruitment is given as 16.7 [PR-8]. The journal full text was not opened, so the reason for the shortfall is unknown. Compared with those without child-welfare involvement, child-welfare-involved victims were more often living in group or foster homes, were recruited as minors, and had higher recorded rates of maltreatment and substance use. They were also more often recruited by strangers in public places, and more often subjected to inducements such as gifts, drugs, money or housing [PR-8]. *Source note: findings were read from the research team's project page and an independent research summary; the journal full text was not opened.* **Limited to Moderate.** It is one region and based on police files, so it covers only identified cases. The summary also reports that child-welfare-involved victims came to police attention earlier, for example because a missing-person report is required when a youth does not return to placement [PR-8]. That pattern is consistent with a **detection effect**: youth in care are more visible to police, so their share of identified cases may overstate their share of all cases.
- **Quebec record.** The Laurent Commission endorsed an earlier Quebec legislative commission's concern that youth rehabilitation centres "can become recruitment places" for pimps, and that running away from them is a major risk [REC-12]. This is an inquiry finding, not a measured rate.
- **What counts as trafficking in Canada.** A peer-reviewed legal analysis argues that the breadth of the Criminal Code offence and its overlap with procuring offences make trafficking hard to distinguish from other offences [PR-14]. An analysis of 127 prosecuted, mostly domestic, sex-trafficking cases found patterns consistent with racially selective identification and prosecution. It notes that Canada does not publish race-disaggregated police and court data, which makes bias hard to test [PR-15]. Both were re-checked for this case. Together they mean that any count of "trafficked youth in care" depends heavily on police practice.
- **Data on trafficking generally.** A 2025 analysis of Canadian government data sources and Access to Information requests found that available data are predominantly crime statistics and that responses have been "largely rhetorical and enforcement-centric" [PR-16].
- **Exploitation beyond girls and beyond care.** British Columbia surveys of homeless and street-involved youth (2006 and 2014) found just over one in four boys reported sexual exploitation, with a median age at first exploitation of 14–15. Most had run away or were homeless first, but 25.5% (2006) and 41% (2014) were living with family at the time [PR-17]. This is a reminder that care status is one risk marker among several.

### 6.2 What is not known
- **The share of youth in care who are trafficked, in any province. GAP.** No Canadian population-based or audited estimate exists in the sources found.
- **Whether being in care raises risk, or whether youth in care are simply more visible to police. GAP.** The Ontario study cannot separate the two [PR-8].
- **Court records at aggregate level.** No peer-reviewed analysis of Canadian trafficking judgments that reports the care status of complainants was found. The 127-case study [PR-15] does not report care status in its abstract. **GAP.**
- **Missing-from-care as a pathway.** Running away from placement is named as a risk in Ontario police files [PR-8] and in the Quebec record [REC-12], but no province publishes audited missing-from-care counts (Section 1). **GAP.**

## 7. Data gaps: Canada has no national child-welfare dataset

### 7.1 Facts
- **No central system.** Child welfare falls under provinces, territories and First Nations. A peer-reviewed analysis of the federal public-health agency's national child-welfare information system shows that the data are assembled from a mix of public, custom and record-level sources that differ by jurisdiction and year, and that because the data are aggregate the authors could not assess quality, missing data or double-counting [PR-6]. Most Canadian jurisdictions "are unable to report on basic information such as the stability and duration of out-of-home placements, academic achievement, rates of recurrence", and the national incidence study is "actually the only national source of data on child welfare services" [PR-7].
- **The national count depends on the method.**
  - A peer-reviewed analysis of the federal public-health agency's national child-welfare information system estimated 61,104 children in out-of-home care on 31 March 2022 (8.24 per 1,000). Adding federal on-reserve data from Indigenous Services Canada raised the estimate to 70,434 (9.50 per 1,000), a 14.2% difference [PR-6].
  - **Flag on [PR-6].** The article is peer-reviewed, but it was written mainly by staff of the Public Health Agency of Canada, published in that agency's journal, and built on government data. It is therefore both a peer-reviewed source and a government figure.
- **The data sources disagree on direction.** The same article notes that its rate including federal on-reserve data "suggests that the rate may have increased or had been previously underestimated". That contrasts with census data showing a *decline* in the rate of children in foster care, from 4.93 per 1,000 in 2016 to 4.45 in 2021 [PR-6]. The two sources cannot both describe the same trend, and the authors say the factors behind the differences "are not evident" [PR-6]. **This is the clearest peer-reviewed evidence that Canada cannot currently say whether the number of children in care is rising or falling.**
- **Coverage gaps in the national system:**
  - The system draws on a mix of public, custom and record-level data that differs by jurisdiction and year.
  - It has gaps for First Nations children on reserve or served by First Nations agencies, for years before 2013, and for sex, age and placement type.
  - Sex and age breakdowns cover jurisdictions holding only 18.8% and 26.3% of children in care.
  - Because the data are aggregate, the authors could not assess data quality, missing data or double-counting.
  - They did not calculate Indigenous-specific rates, because they lacked permission and distinctions-based data for most jurisdictions [PR-6].
- **What jurisdictions cannot report.** Most Canadian jurisdictions "are unable to report on basic information such as the stability and duration of out-of-home placements, academic achievement, rates of recurrence", and the national incidence study (CIS) "is actually the only national source of data on child welfare services". The CIS is cross-sectional and repeats only every few years [PR-7]. The same authors note that Quebec has the best-developed client information system in the country, yet agencies lacked the research capacity to use it beyond standard counts [PR-7].
- **Records that confirm the gap at provincial level:**
  - Ontario's coroner's panel found no system-level tracking of placement moves and no central record of costs [REC-1].
  - British Columbia's hotel data before November 2014 were "unreliable" [REC-4], and the 2024 definition change breaks the series [GOV-2].
  - Nova Scotia's department did not analyse more than 1,900 serious-occurrence reports and did not know how many abuse allegations children in care homes had made [REC-13].
  - Manitoba had no province-wide record of vacant foster beds in 2009 [REC-5].
- **The law does not require national data.** The 2019 federal Act says the Minister "may" enter into data agreements [REC-9]. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Call to Action 2, which asks for annual national reports on Indigenous children in care, the reasons for apprehension, prevention spending and effectiveness of interventions [REC-11], remains unmet in comparable form by any source found here.

### 7.2 Why this matters for the rest of the case
Every count in Sections 1–6 comes from an agency with a stake in the number:
- Funding formulas can reward placements. The Human Rights Tribunal found that the federal on-reserve formula did exactly this [REC-7].
- Definitions can be narrowed, as with British Columbia's 2024 hotel rule [GOV-2].
- Look-back windows differ between advocates (Section 1).

Without a national, audited, record-level dataset, these incentives cannot be checked against an independent baseline. **Strength of the finding that the data gap exists: Strong** (peer-reviewed and record sources agree).

## 8. What has been shown to work (measured effects only)

| Measure | Measured effect | Where measured | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Placing children with relatives (kinship care) rather than with unrelated foster carers** | A Cochrane review of 102 quasi-experimental studies (666,615 children) found that children in kinship care had fewer behavioural problems (SMD −0.33), fewer mental-health disorders (OR 0.51), better well-being (OR 0.50) and less placement disruption (OR 0.52). Reunification rates did not differ [PR-18] | Mostly outside Canada | **Moderate.** The review itself flags pronounced design weaknesses, mainly selection bias |
| **Not placing a child in care when the child can safely stay home** | A Manitoba instrumental-variable study found that placement in care raised the odds of teen pregnancy, teen birth and incarceration compared with similar children who stayed home with protection concerns [PR-2]. The suicide-attempt rate was highest *before* entry into care and fell afterwards [PR-1] | Manitoba | **Limited to Moderate.** The two findings point in different directions on different outcomes, and the main study is a conference abstract |
| **Extended care and transitional supports after 18** | A meta-analysis found extended care "promising", but rated certainty very low and recommended no approach [PR-13]. In Vancouver, using more transitional services was associated with lower homelessness and daily drug use [PR-12] | Mostly United States; one BC study | **Limited** |
| **Housing First for Youth** (immediate housing plus supports) | A randomized trial is under way in Ottawa and Toronto. The Toronto site focuses on youth exiting child welfare. Its published protocol reports no outcomes [PR-19]. No peer-reviewed outcome paper was found | Ontario | **GAP** (results not yet verified) |
| **Ending hotel placements by directive** | Manitoba's 2006 hotel-reduction strategy removed all children from hotels by July 2007. However, it displaced children to emergency shelters, whose number and cost rose, and sibling groups and high-needs youth later reappeared in hotels [REC-5]. British Columbia's 2014 approval-and-reporting directive created the first reliable counts [REC-4]. The later fall to 6 children a year coincides with a narrower definition [GOV-2] | Manitoba, BC | **Limited** (documented outcomes in records, no controlled comparison) |
| **Equitable on-reserve funding, Jordan's Principle, and Indigenous jurisdiction under the 2019 Act** | Ordered or enacted [REC-7; REC-8; REC-9; REC-10]. No peer-reviewed measurement of the effect on the number of children in care, on deaths, or on reunification was found | National | **GAP** |
| **Trafficking-specific models of care for youth in child welfare** | A systematic review plus interviews identified "wraparound supports" and "trained caregivers and supported foster homes" as core components [PR-9]. It describes components; it does not measure effects | Canada and elsewhere | **GAP** (effect untested) |
| **Independent advocates, ombudspersons and coroner panels** | They produce the findings in Sections 1–4. No study was found that measures whether their recommendations changed outcomes | All provinces | **GAP** |

**Bottom line.** The strongest measured evidence supports two things: placing with kin where a child must leave home, and treating removal itself as an intervention with possible harms. Extended care is promising, with very low certainty. For most of the reforms Canada has actually adopted since 2016, including the tribunal orders, the 2019 Act, extended-care programs in Ontario, BC and Alberta, and hotel bans, **the effect has not been measured.**

## Fair counterarguments

These are live disputes among researchers, courts, governments and Indigenous organisations. The case does not resolve them.

1. **Removal as protection or as harm.** One view holds that removal saves children from dangerous homes, and points to the fall in suicide attempts after entry into care [PR-1]. The other holds that removal itself causes harm, and points to worse later outcomes for children placed rather than kept home [PR-2] and to researchers' view that foster care "has not yet proven to improve children's lives" [PR-7]. Both readings rest on observational data.
2. **Why Indigenous children are over-represented.** Statistical work attributes the placement gap mainly to poverty and household disadvantage [PR-3]. The tribunal found that federal funding rules themselves created an incentive to place children [REC-7]. Others stress bias in decision-making, which those statistical controls cannot rule out (Section 2). These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and their relative weight is unmeasured.
3. **Who should run child welfare for Indigenous children.** Quebec challenged the 2019 Act as beyond Parliament's power, and the Supreme Court upheld it [REC-10]. Disagreement continues over how provincial standards, Indigenous laws and federal paramountcy interact in practice. There is no outcome data yet to settle it.
4. **National data versus Indigenous data governance.** Researchers and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission call for national data [PR-7; REC-11]. However, the national analysis could not produce Indigenous-specific rates without permission from Indigenous and provincial partners [PR-6]. A national dataset must be reconciled with community control of data.
5. **Hotel bans.** Bans end an unsafe practice. Manitoba's experience shows a ban without new capacity can shift children into crowded emergency shelters [REC-5].
6. **Trafficking of youth in care: rising, or more visible?** Police-file studies show over-representation of child-welfare-involved victims [PR-8]. Legal and enforcement scholars warn that definitions, police practice and selective identification shape the counts [PR-14; PR-15; PR-16].
7. **Extended care.** Supporters cite promising effects. Sceptics cite very-low-certainty evidence and cost [PR-13]. An earlier audit also noted that Ontario had removed goal requirements for continued support [REC-14].

## 10. Values (separate from the facts above)

This section states value positions. They are not findings.

- When the state takes a child from their parents, it takes on at least the same duty of care that it would demand of those parents, and it should be held to account in the same way.
- A system that cannot count its own children, placements, moves, disappearances and deaths cannot show that it is meeting that duty.
- Where a tribunal has found that funding rules rewarded removal, many people hold that fixing the incentive is a matter of fairness to children and parents, regardless of the cost.
- Some weigh family and cultural continuity very highly, and others weigh immediate physical safety very highly. Most reforms try to honour both; the evidence above does not decide how to trade them off.

## 11. Limits of this case

- **Search limits.** This review opened about 40 sources under a fixed budget. It did not systematically search every province and territory. Saskatchewan, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Prince Edward Island and the three territories are covered only through national data. **GAP** for province-specific oversight records in those jurisdictions.
- **"Orphans."** Canadian child-welfare data do not report how many children in care have no living parents. Permanent wards are children whose parents' rights were ended or transferred by a court, not necessarily orphans. **GAP.**
- **Records are not research.** Coroner, advocate, ombudsperson and auditor reports are authoritative on what they examined, but they examine selected cases or samples, and their counts come from ministry files.
- **Government figures.** Every government-sourced number here is flagged. Few have been audited by independent researchers, and the main peer-reviewed national analysis was itself written largely by federal staff [PR-6].
- **Partial reads.** Several sources were read only as abstracts, summaries or extracts: [PR-2] (conference abstract), [PR-8] (research summary), [PR-10] (not readable), [REC-8] (orders section truncated), and the 2024–25 Alberta annual report (timed out). These are marked in the text.
- **Time.** Policy is changing quickly. The Ontario Ombudsman's investigation into unlicensed placements was still ongoing when checked, and Canada-wide settlement and reform of on-reserve funding is continuing.
- **No individual cases.** By design, the case uses aggregate data only. Some of the most detailed oversight reports in Canada concern single deaths and are not used.

## 12. Proposed solutions

Each proposal names the failure it answers, the evidence behind it, and how strong that evidence is (**strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **untested**). **UNTESTED** means no measured outcome was found for that specific measure. Proposals are policy options, not findings.

**S1. A national, record-level, independently audited child-welfare dataset, built under Indigenous data-governance agreements.**
- *Failure addressed:*
  - No central count exists, and federal and census sources disagree on whether the number in care is rising or falling [PR-6].
  - Jurisdictions cannot report placement stability or recurrence [PR-7].
  - TRC Call to Action 2 is unmet [REC-11], and the 2019 Act makes data agreements optional (s. 28) [REC-9].
- *Concrete form:* Amend s. 28 so that data agreements become a duty. Set common definitions (age, placement type, missing episodes, deaths). Release annual figures with each province's raw counts beside them, and have a legislative auditor or a university team audit them.
- *Evidence:* The peer-reviewed national analysis shows that aggregate data cannot test quality or double-counting, and that record-level data would allow it [PR-6]. Quebec's record-level system shows the data can exist, but that it goes unused without research capacity [PR-7].
- *Strength:* **untested** as a national measure. **UNTESTED.**

**S2. A statutory ban on placements in hotels, offices, trailers and other unlicensed settings, paired with funded emergency capacity for sibling groups and high-needs adolescents.**
- *Failure addressed:*
  - Hotel and unlicensed placements in BC, Manitoba and Ontario [REC-4; REC-5; REC-6].
  - Unlicensed, uninspected emergency arrangements in Nova Scotia averaging over eight months [REC-13].
- *Concrete form:* Make any unlicensed placement a reportable event to the provincial advocate within 24 hours. Cap its length in law. Publish counts monthly under a definition that cannot be changed without the advocate's agreement, which guards against redefinition like BC's 2024 change [GOV-2]. Fund specialised foster and treatment beds *before* the ban takes effect.
- *Evidence:* Manitoba's 2006 strategy did empty hotels within nine months, but without new capacity it pushed children into crowded shelters [REC-5]. BC's 2014 approval-and-reporting directive produced the first reliable counts [REC-4].
- *Strength:* **weak** (documented outcomes in records, no controlled comparison).

**S3. Inspect every residence, and monitor quality of care, not only compliance.**
- *Failure addressed:* Ontario licensed multi-home operators on a 10% inspection sample, and had "no process to monitor the quality of care" [REC-1]. Nova Scotia inspections did not verify that staff background checks were renewed [REC-13].
- *Concrete form:* Require an annual unannounced inspection of every home where a child in care lives, including private-operator homes. Check background-check renewals. Include a private interview with each child.
- *Evidence:* Inspection gaps are documented [REC-1; REC-13]. No Canadian study measures the effect of full inspection on child safety.
- *Strength:* **untested. UNTESTED.**

**S4. Independent analysis of serious-occurrence and critical-incident reports.**
- *Failure addressed:* More than 1,900 incident reports were never analysed in Nova Scotia, and the department did not know how many abuse allegations children had made [REC-13]. Ontario's serious-occurrence reports were often thin or inaccurate and were not trend-monitored [REC-1].
- *Concrete form:* Route every serious-occurrence report to the provincial advocate as well as the ministry. Require a published annual trend analysis, broken down by placement type.
- *Evidence:* The failure is documented by audit and coroner records. The effect of the remedy has not been measured.
- *Strength:* **untested. UNTESTED.**

**S5. Enforce the kin-first placement order and measure compliance.**
- *Failure addressed:* Children placed far from family and community, about 12 placements on average among the young people whose deaths the Ontario panel reviewed [REC-1]. Placement instability more generally (Sections 1 and 4).
- *Concrete form:* Report, by province, the share of placements made at each level of the s. 16 priority order of the 2019 Act [REC-9], extended by provincial law to all children. Fund kinship carers on the same basis as unrelated foster carers.
- *Evidence:* A Cochrane review of 102 studies found kinship care linked to fewer behavioural and mental-health problems and less placement disruption [PR-18].
- *Strength:* **moderate** (consistent but quasi-experimental and mostly non-Canadian).

**S6. Remove funding incentives that reward placement, and fund prevention on a needs basis.**
- *Failure addressed:* The tribunal found that federal on-reserve funding reimbursed maintenance at cost while capping prevention, "providing an incentive to bring children into care" [REC-7]. The same logic should be checked in every provincial formula.
- *Concrete form:*
  - Have each provincial auditor general audit whether provincial child-welfare funding formulas pay more for placement than for in-home support.
  - Publish the ratio of prevention to placement spending, as TRC Call 2 already asks [REC-11].
  - Apply s. 15 of the 2019 Act (no apprehension solely for poverty or housing) [REC-9] with a tracked count of removals in which housing or income was a recorded factor.
- *Evidence:* The incentive is a tribunal finding (**strong** as fact). Its removal has not been evaluated. Removal can carry its own harms [PR-2].
- *Strength:* **untested** for the remedy. **UNTESTED.**

**S7. Extend care and supports to age 25 everywhere, with evaluation built in.**
- *Failure addressed:*
  - Housing instability and homelessness after leaving care [PR-11].
  - High unmet demand for transitional services [PR-12].
  - An unevaluated patchwork of provincial programs ending at 21, 22, 23 or 27 [GOV-3; GOV-4; GOV-5].
  - Weak engagement in Ontario's earlier program [REC-14].
- *Concrete form:* Set a national minimum of supports to 25. Require each province to stagger or randomise the order in which new supports roll out, so effects can be measured, and to publish outcomes for housing, education, income and justice contact.
- *Evidence:* The best systematic review found extended care "promising" but with very low certainty [PR-13]. One BC study found more service use associated with lower homelessness [PR-12].
- *Strength:* **weak.**

**S8. Mandatory recording, return interviews and publication for every missing-from-care episode.**
- *Failure addressed:* No audited Canadian count of missing-from-care episodes (Section 1). Running away is named as a pathway to exploitation in Ontario police files [PR-8] and in the Quebec record [REC-12].
- *Concrete form:* Report every episode to police and the provincial advocate. Interview the child on return about where they were and who they were with. Publish annual counts, durations and repeat rates.
- *Evidence:* The need is documented. No Canadian evaluation of return interviews or mandatory reporting was found.
- *Strength:* **untested. UNTESTED.**

**S9. A specialised, evaluated model of care for youth in care at risk of sexual exploitation.**
- *Failure addressed:* Over-representation of child-welfare-involved youth among identified sex-trafficking victims [PR-8]. Rehabilitation centres described as recruitment sites [REC-12].
- *Concrete form:* Wraparound supports and specially trained, supported foster carers, as the components identified by systematic review and expert interviews [PR-9]. Avoid mixing high-risk youth with recruiters in congregate settings, and pilot the model with a comparison group.
- *Evidence:* Components are identified; effects are not measured [PR-9].
- *Strength:* **untested. UNTESTED.**

**S10. A common national standard for reviewing deaths and serious injuries of children in care or recently served.**
- *Failure addressed:* Ontario, Manitoba and Alberta review different populations with different windows, so deaths cannot be compared or trended [REC-1; REC-2; REC-3].
- *Concrete form:* Agree a shared definition (in care, receiving services, or served within 24 months). Require every province to publish deaths by manner and placement type, with the number of children in each category as the denominator.
- *Evidence:* Records show the incompatibility. The effect of standardisation is unmeasured.
- *Strength:* **untested. UNTESTED.**

**S11. Public tracking of oversight recommendations.**
- *Failure addressed:*
  - No source tracks whether advocate, coroner, ombudsperson and auditor recommendations were implemented (Section 4).
  - Manitoba's 2009 review found only about 63% of 90 earlier recommendations completed, in progress or ongoing [REC-5].
  - Ontario's only auditor-general child-protection audit found here dates from 2015 [REC-14].
- *Concrete form:* Each oversight body publishes a live status table for every recommendation. Legislative committees hear any recommendation still open after two years. Auditors general repeat child-protection audits at least every five years.
- *Evidence:* The gap is documented. The effect of tracking is unmeasured in Canadian child welfare.
- *Strength:* **untested. UNTESTED.**

**S12. Complete and publish the Housing First for Youth trial results for youth leaving care.**
- *Failure addressed:* Homelessness after care [PR-11]. No peer-reviewed Canadian housing intervention with measured outcomes for care leavers was found (Section 8).
- *Concrete form:* Publish the Toronto site's results, which focus on youth exiting child welfare, in peer-reviewed form. Make any scale-up conditional on the results.
- *Evidence:* A randomised trial is registered and under way [PR-19]. Outcomes have not been verified here.
- *Strength:* **untested** pending results. **UNTESTED.**

**Count:** 12 proposals. One has moderate evidence (S5), two have weak evidence (S2, S7), and nine are untested (S1, S3, S4, S6, S8, S9, S10, S11, S12).

## What this does not replace

This is an ethics case study based on public sources. It is not legal advice, a finding about any named child, parent or worker, or a substitute for a court, inquiry, regulator, inspector or caseworker. It is not a ranking of countries, states or agencies, and it is not a current compliance score for any system. Proposed solutions are options to be tested, not evaluated results. Anyone acting on them should read the cited records and consult the people and institutions affected.
