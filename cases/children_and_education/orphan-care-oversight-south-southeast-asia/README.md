# Weak oversight of orphans and children in alternative care in South, Southeast and Central Asia

Date: 2026-11-19

This case examines how far orphans and other children in alternative care in South, Southeast and Central Asia are subject to effective oversight, and what the opened evidence says about harm, protection and reform. The countries in scope are India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Cambodia, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Myanmar and the Central Asian states. The themes covered are orphanage trafficking and “paper orphans”; orphanage voluntourism; unregistered institutions; abuse inquiries; intercountry adoption fraud, including suspensions and official findings for Cambodia, Nepal and India; young people leaving care; gaps in registration and inspection; deinstitutionalisation efforts, including Cambodia’s action plans, Nepal, Indonesia and Kazakhstan; and what has been shown to work. No Canadian data, and no paper with a Canadian-affiliated co-author, is used.

**Source rule.** Every analytical or causal claim rests on a peer-reviewed journal study that was opened for this case, either in full text or as the official abstract. Where only an official abstract was opened, claims stay within that abstract. Statutes, court judgments, official inquiries and government statistics may be cited and are labelled **official data/law/inquiry, not peer-reviewed**. Every official count of institutions, children, inspections, closures, reintegrations or identified abuses is a product of registration, detection, legal definitions, institutional capacity and public attention. It is not a count of how many children are actually in alternative care, nor of how much trafficking, fraud or abuse exists. Every government or agency figure is tagged **[GOV-flag]** beside the figure, including figures relayed inside peer-reviewed papers. Such figures record what agencies counted or chose to report, and may be shaped by reporting incentives, for example a ministry reporting progress against its own target, or institutions self-reporting headcounts. Co-author affiliations listed on the opened sources were checked; sources with a Canadian-affiliated co-author were not used, and sources whose co-author affiliations could not be checked were not used. News appears only as a labelled secondary source and is not used as evidence of scale. Nothing unverified is used as evidence.

**Evidence strength** is rated Strong, Moderate, Limited or Very limited, with a reason. “Strong” would require consistent findings across designs and a direct measure of the outcome (for example actual trafficking, abuse or post-care wellbeing), not only official counts or mechanisms inferred from qualitative and legal research. Almost nothing in this field meets that standard. Measures that have not been tested against child outcomes in the opened sources are marked **UNTESTED**.

## Summary

- In the better-documented settings, a large share of children in residential institutions are not without parents. Peer-reviewed legal analysis describes recruitment from families into orphanages, the construction of false orphan status, and the use of children to attract donations and volunteer fees as “paper orphaning,” and argues that this process can meet the international definition of child trafficking [S1][S2]. That is a legal and qualitative account of a mechanism, not a prevalence estimate.
- Widely cited global figures such as “eight million” children in orphanages, or “four out of five” not being orphans, appear in the opened legal literature as estimates [S1]. A peer-reviewed compilation of official administrative sources, covering residential-care data from 140 countries, produced a much lower global estimate of about 2.7 million children aged 0–17 in institutional care, and stressed that many countries lack functional enumeration systems and that unregistered facilities are missed [S5]. Neither figure is a true prevalence count.
- Official mappings show large registration and inspection gaps. Cambodia’s national mapping (fieldwork 2014–2015; full report published 2017) recorded 406 residential care institutions housing 16,579 children under 18, compared with 254 institutions in the 2015 inspection report, suggesting that as many as 38 per cent had been outside the ministry’s previous regulatory frame; 21 per cent had no memorandum of understanding with the government and 12 per cent were not registered with any government agency. The figures were largely self-reported by institution staff and were not independently verified by the data collectors [S17] **[GOV-flag]**. In India, the Supreme Court found in 2017 that registration of child-care institutions under section 41 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, was still incomplete, held that minimum standards cannot be ensured if institutions are not identified, and directed the Union and all states and union territories to complete registration, with validated data, by 31 December 2017, and to set up inspection committees to visit all facilities at least once every three months [S16].
- Orphanage voluntourism — short visits and volunteering in institutions as part of a holiday — is identified in peer-reviewed research as a demand driver that can keep children in residential care and shape how they are presented to visitors [S2][S7][S11]. In Nepal, one peer-reviewed abstract argues that after receiving states restricted intercountry adoption in 2010, operators shifted toward using children to attract foreign volunteers and donors [S7].
- Official inquiries and receiving-state decisions document systematic failures in intercountry adoption. A 2010 Hague Conference technical mission to Nepal found regular falsification of documents and regular false statements about abandonment, origins, age and status in order to declare a child adoptable, the absence of gatekeeping, and weak regulation of children’s homes; it advised a temporary suspension until a proper legal framework was in place [S13]. The United States then suspended new petitions for children presented as abandoned, stating that documents were unreliable, investigations were hindered, and in one reviewed case the child’s parents were actively searching for a child already matched with a prospective adoptive family [S15]. The United Kingdom placed statutory special restrictions on adoptions from Cambodia (2008) and Nepal (2010) on similar official grounds [S14]. A 2021 official Dutch inquiry into intercountry adoption in 1967–1998, covering Bangladesh, Indonesia and Sri Lanka among five country studies, found large-scale, systematic abuses, including “baby farms,” child trafficking, forced relinquishment and forged documents in the Sri Lankan system, and found that receiving-state authorities often knew of abuses and did not act [S12].
- Cambodia’s Action Plan for Improving Child Care (2016–2018, later extended) set a target of safely returning 30 per cent of children in residential care in five provinces — around 3,500 children — to their families [S18]. An official progress report found that by 2019 the nationwide official counts had fallen by 43 per cent for institutions (406 to 232) and by 59 per cent for children (16,579 to 6,778) compared with the 2014–15 mapping **[GOV-flag: reported by the ministry against its own 30 per cent target]**. In the five target provinces, 7,579 children had left institutions, but only 1,419 had received reintegration support from the social-affairs department or non-state partners; 6,160 had left without that department’s involvement, and authorities were still trying to trace them [S19] **[GOV-flag: reported by the ministry against its own 30 per cent target]**. A fall in official headcount is not evidence that children returned safely to parents.
- A global systematic review and meta-analysis, not specific to this region, finds substantial developmental delays associated with institutional care, especially in physical growth, cognition and attention, a dose–response pattern with longer stays, and significant recovery in some domains after a move to foster or family care, though not in attention. Those findings converge with the only experimental trial in the field, which was conducted outside this region [S3][S4]. Regional evidence on what happens to young people leaving care, and on oversight in Pakistan, the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Myanmar and most Central Asian states, is thin in the opened set.


## 1. Facts by theme

### 1.1 What official numbers can and cannot show

Petrowski, Cappa and Gross compiled official and administrative sources on children in formal alternative care. Data on children in alternative care were identified for 142 of the 197 countries and areas in the agency’s global reporting set. Residential-care figures were found for 140 countries, corresponding to 84 per cent of the world’s children, and from those country-level official figures the authors estimated that approximately 2.7 million children aged 0–17 could be living in institutional care worldwide. They present this as a snapshot of available official data, not as a complete enumeration. Coverage of foster care was much weaker than coverage of residential care. In South Asia, residential-care data were identified for all eight countries in the agency’s regional grouping, but only Sri Lanka had data on children in foster care, and population coverage for foster care in South Asia and in East Asia and the Pacific fell below 10 per cent. The authors stress that many countries still lack functional administrative systems for counting children living outside family care, and that unregistered facilities are not captured [S5] **[GOV-flag]** (compiled from official counts). The linked Lancet policy paper gives a third estimate, 5–6 million children aged 0–18 in institutions, and notes that it rests on scarce data and might be an underestimate [S4]. The spread from about 2.7 to 8 million reflects different definitions and sources, not measured change **[GOV-flag: all three derive partly from official counts]**. Those limitations apply to every official figure used below. A commonly cited estimate of eight million children in orphanages, and a claim that four out of five of them are not orphans, appears in the opened legal literature [S1]. It should not be treated as a measured global stock, and it is not consistent with the 2.7 million estimate derived from the compiled official sources [S5].

### 1.2 Orphanage trafficking and “paper orphans”

Van Doore’s 2016 legal analysis, opened as the official abstract, states that many children are taken from their families by recruiters and sold into orphanages for profit, and that these children are known as “paper orphans.” The article examines whether that movement can be classed as child trafficking under the Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons, Especially Women and Children, and argues that the process of paper orphaning meets the current interpretation of the definition, the main point of contention being whether the documented harms amount to “exploitation” under the Protocol [S1]. The abstract does not offer a new prevalence survey.

A 2023 peer-reviewed review, opened in full, treats orphanage tourism and volunteering as a consumer product at the intersection of operators’ need for international funding and visitors’ wish to “give back.” It concludes that the limited benefits for children are outweighed by child-protection concerns and by negative effects on children’s agency, rights and development, and it links the practice to unnecessary institutionalisation, child trafficking and exploitation in residential care [S2]. That conclusion is a synthesis of a decade of research, not a new causal estimate.

The mechanism described in these papers is: recruit a child from a family, often with a promise of education or support; admit the child to an institution, sometimes by manipulating gatekeeping; construct documents that present the child as an orphan; and keep the child institutionalised so that donations, sponsorships and volunteer fees continue. Official adoption records discussed later show a closely related pattern in intercountry adoption: false abandonment papers, falsified civil documents, and parents who had not consented or were still searching [S12][S13][S15]. Those official findings are about adoption processes, not a measured count of “paper orphans” now in institutions.

### 1.3 Orphanage voluntourism

Guiney’s 2012 peer-reviewed paper on Cambodia, opened in full, describes “orphanage tourism” as visiting, volunteering and watching performances at residential centres. At the time of that fieldwork she reported 21 state-run orphanages and 248 other centres (with the true number potentially higher), many of them dependent on overseas donations and encouraging tourist visits. She noted that, according to the figures then in use, the number of orphans in Cambodia was at its lowest point in decades, while the number of orphanages had risen by 76 per cent in five years, coinciding with a 76 per cent rise in tourist numbers. The paper investigates the forms of the practice and its effects on the institutions and children who are said to benefit, and it treats the “darker side” of the practice — including risks to a vulnerable group — as already becoming visible [S11] **[GOV-flag]**. Those stock and growth figures are not a new census; they should be read as reported contemporaneous counts, subject to the same official-data limits set out above.

Guiney and Mostafanezhad, in an official abstract, analyse Cambodia’s orphanage-tourism industry as a “double movement” between the commodification of orphanages in a newly liberalised economy and campaigns that challenge the industry’s morality and legitimacy. They argue that resistance to that commodification is itself constrained by the broader political economy in which it operates [S6]. The abstract does not measure trafficking incidence.

Punaks and Feit, in an official abstract on Nepal, argue that orphanage voluntourism fuels the displacement of children from their families and their unnecessary institutionalisation. They describe an earlier displacement that began during the conflict, later driven by rural families’ wish for education, and then a shift after an international embargo on intercountry adoptions in 2010: operators who had lost adoption income, the abstract says, adapted by using children as “poverty commodities” to solicit donations from foreign volunteers and donors. The article advises against orphanage voluntourism and discusses “ethical voluntourism” as an alternative; it does not report a controlled outcome evaluation [S7].

Van Doore and Nhep reach a consistent conclusion from the wider literature: orphanage tourism has become a popular altruistic activity, but a growing body of research finds that it can harm more than it assists, including by creating a demand for children to be available in institutions [S2]. No opened study in this set measures a change in trafficking rates after a voluntourism ban.

### 1.4 Unregistered institutions and inspection gaps

Cambodia’s official 2014–15 mapping (full report published 2017), published by the Ministry of Social Affairs, Veterans and Youth Rehabilitation, is the clearest opened enumeration of a registration gap in the region. It identified 639 residential facilities of five types, with 35,374 children and young people in them on staff self-reports: 406 residential care institutions, 25 transit or temporary facilities, 71 group homes, 65 pagoda or other faith-based settings, and 72 boarding schools. Of these, 16,579 children under 18 (47 per cent of them female) were reported in the 406 residential care institutions; 26,187 children under 18 were reported across all 639 facilities, and a further 9,187 people older than 18 were reported to be still living in them. The previous known inspection figure was 254 institutions in 2015, against 406 in the mapping, “suggesting as many as 38 per cent were out of the ministry’s regulatory framework.” The report also states that 21 per cent of the residential care institutions had no memorandum of understanding with the government and 12 per cent were not registered with any government agency, with unregulated institutions concentrated in the provinces that already had the most children in residential care. Only institutions with a ministry memorandum had previously been inspected. The ministry’s own foreword notes that the data were not verified by the data collectors [S17] **[GOV-flag]**. All of these figures are official and self-reported. They show a gap in oversight; they do not show how many children in unregistered facilities were abused or trafficked.

In India, section 41 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, requires child-care institutions to be registered. In *Re: Exploitation of Children in Orphanages in the State of Tamil Nadu v. Union of India* (5 May 2017), the Supreme Court recorded that the Act had been in force since 15 January 2016 but that registration was not yet complete. It directed the Union and all states and union territories to finish registering all child-care institutions by 31 December 2017, with the data confirmed and validated, and to maintain a monthly-updated database of children in need of care and protection, subject to confidentiality. It held that the minimum standards required of institutions cannot be ensured if the institution is not identified, and that it is not hard to imagine “what possibly can go wrong in unregistered institutions.” It read the registration duty together with section 54, which requires inspection of registered institutions, and directed that inspection committees visit all facilities housing children in their area at least once every three months, in a team of at least three members, including at least one woman and one medical officer. It also directed a social audit and stressed rehabilitation and social reintegration, including through skill development and vocational training schemes [S16]. Those are judicial findings and directions about the legal framework, not a measured national stock of unregistered homes after 2017. No later official national stocktake was opened for this case.

The 2010 Hague mission to Nepal likewise found a lack of regulation of children’s homes, a lack of information and statistics on children in those homes, and no gatekeeping to prevent admission when the main aim was free education. It found that children proposed for intercountry adoption were concentrated in homes in the Kathmandu Valley with direct links to adoption agencies, and that there was evidence of many abuses of the rights of children and their parents [S13].

### 1.5 Abuse inquiries, adoption fraud and suspensions

The opened official record is strongest on intercountry adoption, not on a complete set of domestic abuse inquiries in every country in scope.

**Nepal.** The Hague Conference mission of 23–27 November 2009, reported on 4 February 2010, listed as current abuses: regular falsification of documents in order to declare a child adoptable, continuing under the 2008 Terms and Conditions; regular false statements about the child’s abandonment, origins, age and status; lack of transparency and accountability for money coming into Nepal from intercountry adoptions; the absence of a policy that placed intercountry adoption within an integrated alternative-care system; and a lack of alternative care for children without parental care. It found that the best-interests principle was “completely absent” from the framework it reviewed, and that there was no functioning gatekeeping. Its priority recommendations included developing family preservation and alternative care, better regulation of children’s homes, and a new law. “To undertake the necessary reform of the intercountry adoption system, a temporary suspension of adoptions will be necessary.” It also recommended suspending application of the 2008 Terms and Conditions until a proper legal framework was in place [S13].

On 6 August 2010 the United States Department of State and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services jointly suspended adjudication of new petitions for children described as having been abandoned in Nepal. The official statement said that documents presented to prove abandonment were unreliable; that civil documents such as birth certificates often included data that had been changed or fabricated; that investigations were routinely hindered by the unavailability of officials named in abandonment reports; and that police and orphanage officials often refused to cooperate with efforts to compare information with official records. In one case, “the birth parents were actively searching for a child who had been matched with an American family for adoption.” The statement said the United States could no longer reasonably determine whether a child documented as abandoned qualified as an orphan, and that, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, all other countries that had been processing Nepal cases had stopped accepting new ones for the same reason [S15]. The United Kingdom’s Special Restrictions on Adoptions from Abroad (Nepal) Order 2010, in force from 3 May 2010, cited the Hague Bureau report’s findings of insufficient procedures to establish whether a child is adoptable, inadequate support for parents about the legal effects of relinquishment, and no procedures for finding a permanent family in Nepal, and it placed those concerns on a statutory footing [S14].

**Cambodia.** The United Kingdom’s Special Restrictions on Adoptions from Abroad (Cambodia) Order 2008 placed on a statutory footing a suspension announced in 2004 and confirmed after a 2007–2008 review. The official reasons included evidence of systematic falsification of Cambodian official adoption documents; extensive use of adoption facilitators even though Cambodian law forbade their participation; procurement of children for intercountry adoption by facilitators, including by coercion and by paying parents to give up their children; and concern about child trafficking and corruption. The 2008 review found that legislation, practice and procedure remained insufficient to protect children and their families, and that lifting the suspension would expose them to an increased risk of practices contrary to the Hague Adoption Convention and the Convention on the Rights of the Child [S14].

**Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and Indonesia.** The official Dutch Committee on Intercountry Adoption (report of February 2021) was established to examine the role and responsibility of the Dutch government in intercountry adoptions, at least for 1967–1998, starting with Bangladesh, Brazil, Colombia, Indonesia and Sri Lanka. In its English summary, the committee found that intercountry adoptions were characterised by “large-scale, systematic abuses,” including inaccurate documentation, child trafficking and “baby farms”; that reports of abuse were known to several Dutch government bodies and to politicians and were for a long time not acted on; that papers were not subjected to strict controls; and that intermediaries were subject to only minimal oversight. It found that factors of “supply and demand” created an international “adoption market” with a corrupting financial effect, and that a positive image of intercountry adoption as “doing good” persisted despite growing evidence to the contrary. It screened 23 countries in all and reported that abuses were or are reported in all of them, and that abuses continued after the Hague Adoption Convention entered into force in the Netherlands in 1998. It also found that, although adopted people were often presented as orphans, many of them still had parents [S12].

In its country findings, which this case uses as official inquiry findings, the committee concluded as follows. In Sri Lanka, almost all imaginable forms of abuse occurred in the adoption practice with the Netherlands: “baby farms,” child trafficking, theft of children, forced relinquishment, forgery of documents, legalisation of incorrect documents, embezzlement of status, corruption, bribery and improper payments, in breach of the Dutch and Sri Lankan law then in force. Dutch ministries were repeatedly and in detail informed of these abuses from the early 1980s, including reports of baby farming and of outright child theft; in general they did not intervene, even when diplomats on the ground raised the alarm, and they continued to assign solutions to the Sri Lankan authorities. The committee found that the Dutch government was not itself a participant in the abuses in Sri Lanka, but that it regularly knew of them and did not act when there was reason to, and that both individual intermediaries and larger organisations were involved [S12]. In Bangladesh, the committee placed later adoption practice in the context of war, displacement, famine and corruption in the 1970s; it found that reports of alleged abuses had circulated from at least September 1978, that the Dutch government made enquiries of local authorities and of Dutch organisations but did not itself investigate, and that oversight of adoptions from Bangladesh was weak [S12]. In Indonesia, it found that all imaginable forms of abuse — from incorrect documents to child trafficking and baby farms — occurred, and that the Dutch government and the embassy in Jakarta were aware of this from at least November 1977 [S12]. These are inquiry findings about historical intercountry-adoption systems. They are not a current census of institutions in those countries, and they are not findings against named private persons in this case.

**India.** The 2017 Supreme Court judgment is an official judicial record of incomplete registration, weak inspection and the need for minimum standards and social audit in child-care institutions, arising from a petition concerning exploitation of children in orphanages [S16]. No separate opened court judgment on a named intercountry-adoption prosecution was used as evidence here. That is a gap, not a finding that no such cases exist.

### 1.6 Young people leaving care

The opened official Cambodia mapping reported 9,187 people older than 18 still living in the 639 mapped facilities, on staff self-reports [S17] **[GOV-flag]**. That is an official stock figure, not an outcome study.

A peer-reviewed 2024 article on Indonesia, opened as the official abstract, states that previous literature has highlighted poor quality of care in out-of-home institutions and adverse experiences of institutionalisation; that the government has encouraged deinstitutionalisation by preventing family separation, promoting family-based alternative care and using residential care only as a last resort; and that little scholarly attention has been given to young people leaving care, who are “likely to have poor life outcomes.” The article is a policy briefing based on existing government and non-government literature, not a new outcome survey [S21].

No representative, peer-reviewed outcome study of care leavers in India, Nepal, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Myanmar or Central Asia was opened in full for this case. That is a gap. Claims about homelessness, unemployment or poor health among care leavers in this region cannot be treated as measured facts on the basis of the opened set.

### 1.7 Deinstitutionalisation efforts

**Cambodia.** In 2016 the government adopted an Action Plan for Improving Child Care with the target of safely returning 30 per cent of children in residential care in five provinces (Phnom Penh, Battambang, Siem Reap, Kandal and Preah Sihanouk, holding 267 of the 406 mapped institutions and 11,788 children) — around 3,500 children — to their families by 2018 [S18] **[GOV-flag]**. The official implementation report, covering 2016 to 2019 after an extension, states that nationwide official counts fell from 406 institutions and 16,579 children in the 2014–15 mapping to 232 institutions and 6,778 children in the 2019 inspection (falls of 43 per cent and 59 per cent). In the five target provinces, official counts of children in institutions fell from 11,788 in 2015 to 4,209 in 2019. Of the 7,579 children who had left those provincial institutions, 1,419 (12 per cent of the 2015 provincial stock, and 41 per cent of the 3,500 target) had received reintegration support; 6,160 had left without the social-affairs department’s involvement, and the department and its partners were still working to trace them for case management and to confirm their safety [S19] **[GOV-flag: reported by the ministry against its own 30 per cent target]**. A later official Action Plan on Improving Alternative Care for Children 2023–2027 continues the policy of family preservation, kinship and foster care, reintegration, and revision of regulation and monitoring [S20]. All of these figures are official. They show a large change in inspected headcount and a much smaller supported-reintegration caseload. They do not, by themselves, show what happened to the children who left without official support, and they do not show a measured fall in trafficking or abuse.

**Indonesia.** A 2024 peer-reviewed study, opened as the official abstract, examined the transformation of child-welfare institutions in Bandung after the 2011 National Standard of Care, which was intended to shift institutions from residential homes toward community-based family services. A survey of 50 providers, analysed by cluster, found three groups: medium-to-large faith-based non-government institutions that primarily provide privately funded institutional care (50 per cent); small secular non-government institutions and government protection services that primarily deliver family-support services (26 per cent); and medium-to-large secular non-government institutions that deliver both privately funded institutional care and family support at provincial level (24 per cent). The authors conclude that there is some deinstitutionalisation activity, but that the number and size of privately operated institutions suggest a high continuing dependence on institutional care, with limited government funding and the missions of religious organisations as possible constraints. They call for further research on enablers and barriers [S8].

**Kazakhstan and the post-Soviet space.** An and Kulmala, in an official abstract, compare deinstitutionalisation reforms in Russia and Kazakhstan, both of which inherited residential-dominated systems and later adopted similar global child-rights reforms. They find different institutionalisation of the policy: a fast, sweeping reform in one state and an incremental, gradual process in Kazakhstan. They attribute the difference to the interplay of structural factors and the way policy actors translated global ideology into domestic discourse, including different geopolitical frames, rather than to a measured difference in child outcomes [S9]. The abstract does not report developmental or safety outcomes for children who left institutions.

**South Asia generally.** A 2020 peer-reviewed literature review, opened as the official abstract, finds that knowledge of child-protection reform and deinstitutionalisation in eight South Asian countries is scarce. It proposes three strategies — a regional approach, decentralised implementation, and use of traditional alternative-care practices — and states that future studies are needed to evaluate their effectiveness [S10]. Those strategies are, in the authors’ own terms, untested in the opened record.

**Nepal.** The opened official Hague report recommended family preservation, alternative care to parental care, gatekeeping, and better regulation of homes, and it recorded the absence of nationwide long-term family-preservation programmes [S13]. No opened peer-reviewed outcome evaluation of a subsequent national deinstitutionalisation programme in Nepal was found for this case.


## 2. Country notes

**Cambodia.** The opened record is the densest in the set: the 2014–15 national official mapping that documented a large unregistered or uninspected sector [S17]; a peer-reviewed account of orphanage tourism as a funding and visitor market [S2][S6][S11]; statutory receiving-state restrictions on intercountry adoption [S14]; and two successive official action plans plus an implementation report that show a large official fall in institutional headcount and a much smaller supported-reintegration caseload [S18][S19][S20].

**Nepal.** Official and peer-reviewed sources converge on weak gatekeeping, unreliable adoptability and abandonment documents, and a voluntourism market that, in one opened abstract, is said to have grown after intercountry-adoption restrictions [S7][S13][S14][S15]. No opened national census of current registered and unregistered homes was used here.

**India.** The 2017 Supreme Court judgment is the principal opened official record of incomplete registration and the legal duty to inspect and to plan for reintegration [S16]. Peer-reviewed regional reviews describe South Asian deinstitutionalisation evidence as scarce [S10]. Later official national counts of unregistered institutions were not opened.

**Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and Indonesia (historical adoption).** The 2021 Dutch official inquiry is the principal opened record of systematic intercountry-adoption abuses, strongest for Sri Lanka, with additional findings of weak oversight and unrebutted reports in Bangladesh and of awareness of document fraud, trafficking and “baby farms” in Indonesia from the late 1970s [S12]. Current inspection systems in those three states are not documented in the opened peer-reviewed set, except for a Bandung study that finds continuing dependence on privately funded institutional care in one Indonesian city [S8].

**Kazakhstan.** The opened abstract describes incremental official deinstitutionalisation relative to a faster reform elsewhere in the post-Soviet space, as a matter of policy translation and geopolitics, without child-level outcome data [S9].

**Pakistan, the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Myanmar and the other Central Asian states (Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan).** No peer-reviewed full text or official abstract sufficient to support findings on current registration, inspection, voluntourism, adoption fraud or leaving care was opened for this case. Their inclusion in the scope is therefore a statement of intended coverage, not of findings. That is a gap.

## Fair counterarguments

**How many children are in institutions, and how many of them have parents.** One widely circulated pair of estimates, reproduced in the opened legal literature, is eight million children in orphanages globally and four out of five of them not orphans [S1]. A peer-reviewed compilation of official sources estimates about 2.7 million children aged 0–17 in institutional care and warns that unregistered facilities and weak administrative systems make even that figure incomplete [S5] **[GOV-flag]**. The linked Lancet policy paper gives a third estimate, 5–6 million children aged 0–18 in institutions, and notes that it rests on scarce data and might be an underestimate [S4]. The spread from about 2.7 to 8 million reflects different definitions and sources, not measured change **[GOV-flag: all three derive partly from official counts]**. These cannot all be treated as measured stocks. The safer statement is that official counts are incomplete, that in several documented settings many institutionalised children are not without parents, and that the true numbers are unknown.

**Whether paper orphaning is “trafficking.”** Van Doore argues that the process meets the current interpretation of child trafficking under the Trafficking Protocol, the dispute turning on the meaning of exploitation [S1]. Official adoption inquiries and receiving-state decisions document fraud, false orphan status and, in some systems, child trafficking as a finding of fact [S12][S13][S15]. That is not the same as a legal consensus that every recruitment into an institution for donor income is trafficking, and it is not a count of cases.

**Whether a fall in official institutional numbers means children are safer.** Cambodia’s official progress report presents large percentage falls in institutions and in children living in them, including a headline comparison with the 30 per cent target [S19] **[GOV-flag: reported by the ministry against its own 30 per cent target]**. The same report shows that, in the five target provinces, most children who left did so without the social-affairs department’s supported reintegration, and that those children were still being traced [S19]. A reduction in inspected headcount can reflect closures, reclassification, transfers, unrecorded returns, or children moving into unmonitored settings. It cannot, without follow-up, be read as safe reunification with parents.

**Whether deinstitutionalisation policy is being implemented.** Indonesia has a national care standard that, on paper, converts institutions into family-support centres; the opened Bandung study finds that privately funded institutional care remains common [S8]. Kazakhstan has adopted a global deinstitutionalisation policy but, in the opened abstract, has implemented it only incrementally [S9]. A South Asian review finds the literature too thin to evaluate reform strategies [S10]. Formal policy and practice on the ground should not be assumed to be the same thing.

**How far global developmental evidence applies in this region.** The Lancet systematic review finds substantial harm associated with institutional care and partial recovery after family placement, and those estimates converge with an experimental trial conducted outside this region [S3][S4]. Institutions in the countries in scope vary in size, staffing, funding and purpose, and no opened regional trial repeats that experiment. The direction of the developmental evidence is consistent; the size of effects in a particular Cambodian, Nepali or Indian facility has not been measured in the opened set.

## 4. What has been shown to work (ranked, with evidence strength)

Ranks reflect the strength of the opened evidence that the measure improves children’s development, safety or protection from trafficking and fraudulent adoption in the settings in scope, not a judgement of moral priority.

1. **Shortening or ending stays in institutions by placing children in stable family-based care (including kinship care, high-quality foster care, or return to parents when that is safe), and preventing admission where possible.** A global systematic review and meta-analysis finds strong negative associations between institutional care and development, especially physical growth, cognition and attention (combined Hedges’ *g* in those domains ranging from 0.50 to 1.44), a dose–response pattern with longer stays, and significant recovery after a move to foster or family care for some outcomes (growth and cognition) but not for attention. The authors conclude that every effort should be made to minimise exposure, that stays should be as short as possible even when institutional care is unavoidable, and that preventive approaches should keep children with their parents when possible [S3]. A linked policy paper sets out recommendations for family-based alternatives, regulation and workforce development [S4]. The only experimental trial in the field, which used random assignment to foster care, was conducted outside this region; the meta-analytic estimates are globally pooled, not Asia-specific. Evidence: **Moderate** for developmental harm of institutional care and for recovery in some domains after family placement, as a general finding; **Limited** as a measured effect of any named programme in the countries in scope.

2. **Establishing adoptability, genuine consent and reliable documents before any intercountry adoption, and suspending new cases when those conditions cannot be met.** The Hague mission to Nepal recommended a temporary suspension until law and procedure existed, after finding regular document fraud and false abandonment claims [S13]. Receiving states then restricted or suspended Nepal and Cambodia on official records of unreliable documents, forbidden facilitators, coercion or payments to parents, and inability to determine whether a child was in fact an orphan [S14][S15]. The Dutch official inquiry found that, even after the receiving state joined the Hague Convention, abuses in the wider system did not disappear, and that weak checks and a demand-driven market were structural causes [S12]. Suspension stops a contaminated official channel. It does not, in the opened record, measure informal onward movement, and it does not repair past cases. Evidence: **Limited** that suspension is an effective immediate safeguard against further official placements through a system already found to be unreliable; **Very limited** as a remedy for past harm.

3. **Registering and inspecting all residential facilities, not only those with a prior memorandum or licence, and identifying every child in them.** Cambodia’s mapping showed that inspection limited to institutions with a ministry memorandum missed a large share of the sector [S17] **[GOV-flag]**. India’s Supreme Court held that minimum standards cannot be ensured if institutions are not identified, and ordered universal registration and regular multi-member inspection [S16]. The Hague Nepal report likewise called for better regulation of homes and for statistics on the children in them [S13]. Registration and inspection are preconditions for oversight. The opened sources do not include a trial showing that inspection alone reduces abuse or trafficking. Cambodia’s later official headcount fall occurred after mapping, regulation and an action plan were combined, so the isolated effect of inspection cannot be estimated, and most exits were not officially supported [S19]. Evidence: **Limited** as a demonstrated protection against abuse or trafficking; **Moderate** as a documented necessary condition for knowing which institutions exist.

4. **Supported reintegration with follow-up, rather than unrecorded exit.** Cambodia’s official plan aimed at safe return to families, not mere discharge [S18][S20]. The implementation record shows that supported reintegration reached only a minority of children who left institutions in the target provinces [S19]. Global developmental evidence supports family placement with care for quality and stability [S3][S4]. No opened peer-reviewed study in this set evaluates the quality or safety of Cambodia’s supported-reintegration cases against the unsupported exits. Evidence: **Limited** in this region (official process data, not child-outcome trials); the measure is partly **UNTESTED** as a completed evaluation.

5. **Reducing donor and volunteer demand for residential “orphan” experiences, and shifting funding toward family preservation and family-based care.** Peer-reviewed work consistently describes voluntourism and unregulated donations as demand drivers for keeping children in institutions [S2][S7][S11]. That is a coherent mechanism. No opened study measures a fall in institutionalisation or trafficking after a national or sectoral voluntourism ban. Evidence: **Limited** for the mechanism; **UNTESTED** as an outcome-evaluated intervention in the opened set.

6. **Family-preservation programmes so that poverty or a wish for schooling is not treated as a ground for admission.** The Hague Nepal report found no nationwide long-term family-preservation programme and no gatekeeping against admission for free education, and it recommended both [S13]. The Lancet review likewise recommends prevention and keeping children with their parents when possible [S3]. No opened outcome evaluation of a family-preservation programme in the countries in scope was found. Evidence: **Very limited** in this regional set; the specific programmes are **UNTESTED** here.

7. **Aftercare for young people who reach the age of majority in institutions (identity documents, housing, education, work and psychosocial support).** Official mapping in Cambodia recorded thousands of people over 18 still in facilities [S17] **[GOV-flag]**. India’s Supreme Court directed attention to rehabilitation and social reintegration, including skills and vocational schemes [S16]. No opened peer-reviewed outcome trial of an aftercare model in the countries in scope was found. Evidence: **Very limited**; the models are **UNTESTED** in the opened regional set.

## 5. VALUES (labelled: these are not findings)

The evidence above does not dictate a single care system. Choices among measures turn on values as well as on facts, and the facts are thin.

- **Children’s safety and development** are the shared aim. That aim is not the same as maximising the number of registered institutions, official reintegration cases, or donor visits, all of which can rise or fall with reporting effort and with incentives to look successful.
- **What to do with weak evidence.** One view is that, because the harms of institutional care, trafficking and fraudulent adoption are severe, authorities should close institutions quickly and ban volunteer visits even without local outcome trials, relying on global developmental evidence and on official inquiries. Another is that rapid closure without family support can move children into unmonitored settings, as the unexplained exits in Cambodia’s official figures illustrate, and that scarce resources should follow measures with clearer outcome evidence. Both views are value judgements. The evidence does not settle them.
- **Family-based care is not automatically safe care.** Preferring placement with parents or relatives is consistent with the opened developmental evidence and with official policy in several countries. It does not require assuming that every family is safe, or that unsupported discharge is the same as reunification.
- **Equal treatment of boys and girls, and of children with and without surviving parents.** Oversight and family support should not be designed around a single family role or around a sentimental image of the “orphan.” Many children in the documented institutions are not without parents. Policy that only targets children labelled as orphans will miss them.
- **Accountability for past fraudulent adoptions.** Official inquiries have documented systematic abuses and have recommended acknowledgement and support for adopted people who were presented as orphans while their parents were alive [S12]. Whether, and how, receiving and sending states should offer records, contact or redress is a legal and political choice, not a finding of this case.
- **Funding sources shape the system.** So long as residential institutions can raise more money from foreign visitors than family-support services can raise from public budgets, operators have an incentive to keep children in view. Changing that incentive is a political choice about how aid and private donations are allowed to flow.

## 6. What this does not show, and GAPs

- It does not show how many children are currently in residential or informal alternative care in any country in scope, or how many of them have living parents.
- It does not show the incidence of orphanage trafficking, abuse inside institutions, or fraudulent adoption now.
- It does not show what happened to the majority of children who left Cambodian institutions in the target provinces without official reintegration support [S19].
- It does not isolate the effect of inspection, funding reform, or voluntourism restrictions from other policy changes.
- It does not provide a peer-reviewed outcome evaluation of deinstitutionalisation in Nepal, of aftercare in India, or of incremental reform in Kazakhstan.
- It does not assess every inquiry or court case in the region. In particular, no official judgment on a named Indian intercountry-adoption prosecution, and no official inquiry report on a named domestic institution outside the records cited above, was opened.

**GAP 1.** Current, independently verified counts of registered and unregistered residential facilities, and of the children in them, for Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, Myanmar, Nepal (post-2010) and the Central Asian states other than the policy description of Kazakhstan.

**GAP 2.** Peer-reviewed, representative studies of young people leaving care in the countries in scope, with outcomes for housing, education, work, health and safety, and with checked author affiliations.

**GAP 3.** Child-level outcomes for Cambodia’s supported reintegration cases compared with children who left institutions without official support.

**GAP 4.** Outcome evaluations of voluntourism bans, donor-routing rules, or “ethical volunteering” programmes, against institutionalisation or trafficking — not only against visitor attitudes.

**GAP 5.** Outcome evaluations of family-preservation and gatekeeping programmes in Nepal, India, Indonesia and Cambodia, including whether poverty or schooling is still used as a ground for admission.

**GAP 6.** Opened official court or inquiry records on domestic abuse in institutions in India after 2017, and in the other countries in scope, sufficient to describe findings without relying on news.

**GAP 7.** Peer-reviewed evidence on current intercountry or domestic adoption practice in Vietnam, the Philippines, Thailand, Myanmar and Central Asia, and on whether past suspensions remain necessary.

**GAP 8.** Independent, peer-reviewed assessment of inspection quality (not only inspection frequency) and of whether inspection predicts lower abuse.

## 7. Proposed solutions

Each proposal is tied to a failure documented above. Evidence strength refers to the opened record that the measure would address that failure. Proposals without an outcome test in the opened sources are marked **UNTESTED**.

1. **Complete, public registration of every residential facility that houses children, including pagoda-based, group-home, boarding and transit settings, whether or not they already have a ministry memorandum, with a child-level list updated on a fixed cycle and with confidentiality safeguards.** This addresses the failure, documented in Cambodia’s mapping and in India’s 2017 judgment, that unregistered or unmemorandumed facilities were outside inspection and that minimum standards cannot be applied to institutions that have not been identified [S16][S17] **[GOV-flag]**. The Hague Nepal report made the same diagnosis of missing statistics and weak regulation of homes [S13]. Evidence: **Limited** that registration itself reduces harm; **Moderate** that it is a necessary first step. A registry without inspection would be **UNTESTED** as a protection.

2. **Mandatory, regular inspection of all listed facilities by a multi-member team that includes at least one woman and one medical officer, at least once a quarter, with written reports and a power to close or to bar new admissions.** This follows the Indian Supreme Court’s direction under sections 41 and 54 and the failure of Cambodia’s earlier practice of inspecting only institutions with a ministry memorandum [S16][S17]. Evidence: **Limited** as a standalone protection (no opened trial links inspection to lower abuse); the legal duty and the documented gap make it a concrete response to a known failure. Quarterly multi-member inspection is **UNTESTED** against child-safety outcomes in the opened set.

3. **No official claim of successful deinstitutionalisation unless every child who leaves an institution is in a named, followed-up family or alternative-care arrangement.** This addresses the failure in Cambodia’s official implementation figures, where 6,160 children in the five target provinces left without the social-affairs department’s involvement, against 1,419 with supported reintegration, while the headline counts still fell sharply [S19] **[GOV-flag: reported by the ministry against its own 30 per cent target]**. Global developmental evidence supports family placement, not unrecorded exit [S3]. Evidence: **Limited** in regional outcome data; the follow-up standard itself is **UNTESTED** as a completed evaluation in the opened set, but it is the only way to tell whether a headcount fall is a protection success.

4. **Gatekeeping rules that prohibit admission to residential care solely on the grounds of poverty or a wish for schooling, and that require a documented assessment of parents and relatives first.** This addresses the Hague mission’s finding that Nepal had no gatekeeping against admission for free education, and the wider paper-orphan mechanism in which families are induced to place children in institutions with a promise of education [S1][S2][S13]. Evidence: **Limited** (official diagnosis and legal literature; no opened regional trial); implementation quality is **UNTESTED**.

5. **Public funding and donor rules that stop residential institutions raising income from short-term volunteer visits and unregulated orphanage-tourism performances, and that route comparable funds to family preservation, kinship care and monitored foster care.** This addresses the demand-side failure described in the voluntourism literature and, in the Nepal abstract, the shift from adoption fees to volunteer and donor income after the 2010 restrictions [S2][S7][S11]. Evidence: **Limited** for the mechanism; **UNTESTED** as a measured reduction in institutionalisation or trafficking after a funding rule is introduced.

6. **Keep in force, or re-impose, a suspension of new intercountry-adoption cases wherever adoptability, the consent of parents, and the authenticity of documents cannot be independently verified, and do not lift the suspension on the basis of new legislation alone.** This addresses official findings of systematic document fraud and false orphan status in Nepal and Cambodia, and the Dutch inquiry’s finding that abuses continued after a receiving state had joined the Hague Convention and that legal form was not enough [S12][S13][S14][S15]. The documented outcome is that receiving states did suspend Cambodia and Nepal. Evidence: **Limited** (stops a contaminated official channel; does not measure unofficial movement or redress past cases). The rule against lifting a suspension on legislation alone is **UNTESTED**; it rests on the Dutch inquiry’s finding that abuses continued after Hague ratification [S12].

7. **Where family-based placement is used, fund it as a professional service (selection, training, payment where appropriate, and ongoing supervision), not as an unfunded discharge.** This follows the Lancet review’s finding that recovery is associated with foster or family care of documented quality, and the Bandung abstract’s finding that privately funded institutional care persists where government funding for family services is limited [S3][S4][S8]. Evidence: **Moderate** for family-based care in the global developmental literature; **UNTESTED** as a funded foster-care model in most countries in scope.

8. **Before any institution is closed, require a named plan for each child, including identity documents, a family or alternative placement, and aftercare if the person is already at or near the age of majority.** This addresses both the unrecorded exits in Cambodia and the official presence of thousands of people over 18 still in mapped facilities, as well as the Indian Court’s emphasis on rehabilitation and reintegration [S16][S17][S19] **[GOV-flag]**. Evidence: **Limited**; child-level closure protocols in this region remain **UNTESTED** in the opened peer-reviewed set.

9. **Create an official, accessible route for adopted people to obtain their records and to have fraudulent documents recognised as such, without requiring them to prove fraud unaided.** This addresses the Dutch inquiry’s findings that searches often fail because documents are inaccurate, that many adopted people still had parents, and that receiving-state authorities had not been a sufficient source of support [S12]. The inquiry itself made related recommendations. Evidence: **Limited** (inquiry findings and recommendations; later outcomes of those recommendations were not opened); the effect of a records-and-redress office on wellbeing is **UNTESTED** in this case.



## What this does not replace

This is an ethics case study based on public sources. It is not legal advice, a finding about any named child, parent or worker, or a substitute for a court, inquiry, regulator, inspector or caseworker. It is not a ranking of countries, states or agencies, and it is not a current compliance score for any system. Proposed solutions are options to be tested, not evaluated results. Anyone acting on them should read the cited records and consult the people and institutions affected.
