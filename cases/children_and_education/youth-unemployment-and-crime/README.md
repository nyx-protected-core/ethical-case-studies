# Youth Unemployment and Crime

Date: 20 November 2026
Author: anonymous

## Thesis

This paper argues that a rise in youth unemployment can increase crime when young people have
little or no legal way to earn a wage. If lawful work is scarce or pays too little, some turn to
theft, burglary, drug selling and related offences for income. The claim is not that every
unemployed young person offends, or that official national crime rates always rise in the same
year as unemployment. It is that identified research finds a causal link, especially for property
crime, and that official data in Canada, the United States, France and Britain now show youth
joblessness well above adult rates, and higher than the early-2020s lows.

**Evidence** is what published studies and official releases have measured. **Argument** is the
inference drawn from that evidence.

**Overall evidence strength.** Strong for a positive effect of unemployment, and of weak low-wage
opportunity, on property crime in the United States, France and Britain, including a causal
design for youth unemployment in France. Moderate for Canada, where official youth unemployment
has risen sharply and a provincial study finds that economic conditions matter for crime, but
recent police-reported youth crime rates have fallen. Weak for any claim that violent crime moves
one-for-one with youth unemployment, and for any single total of offences caused by the recent
rise in youth joblessness.

## 1. The economic mechanism

In the standard economic model of crime, a person compares the returns to legal work with the
expected returns to illegal activity, net of the chance of being caught [1]. Unemployment and
very low wages reduce the opportunity cost of offending. Young people have the weakest foothold
in the labour market, and they account for a large share of recorded property crime in the
studies below. **Argument.** Where hiring is scarce and wages at the bottom are too low to live
on, some young people stay in education, leave the labour force, move, or live on family or
public support. Some seek income by any remaining means, including crime. That is a statement
about incentives and averages, not about the character of a generation.

## 2. What the research shows

**United States.** Raphael and Winter-Ebmer study a state panel from 1971 to 1997, with state and
year effects, state-specific trends, and instruments for unemployment. They find stable positive
effects on property crime: a one-percentage-point fall in unemployment is associated with a 1 to
5 percent decline in property crime. Their most conservative estimates attribute nearly 40
percent of the 1990s decline in US property crime rates to the concurrent fall in unemployment.
The evidence for violent crime is considerably weaker [2]. Gould, Weinberg and Mustard study
county crime rates from 1979 to 1997, and individual offending in the National Longitudinal
Survey of Youth, focusing on young, less-educated men. Both wages and unemployment are
significantly related to crime, but wages play the larger long-term role. From 1979 to 1997 the
wages of unskilled men fell by 20 percent, while demographically adjusted property and violent
crime rates rose by 21 percent and 35 percent. In their annual ordinary-least-squares analysis,
those wage trends explain more than 50 percent of the increase in both crime indices.
Unemployment still matters: in 1979–1993, the unemployment rate of non-college-educated men
accounts for 24 percent of the rise in adjusted property crime and 8 percent of the rise in
adjusted violent crime [3].

**France.** Fougère, Kramarz and Pouget study the 95 metropolitan départements from 1990 to 2000,
using offences reported to the Ministry of the Interior and unemployment from the labour-force
survey. Crime and unemployment are positively associated in the cross-section. Increases in youth
unemployment induce increases in crime. Instrumenting unemployment with predicted industrial
structure, they find that this effect is causal for burglaries, thefts and drug offences, and they
conclude that strategies against youth unemployment should be examined as a way to combat crime
[4].

**Britain.** Machin and Meghir study police-force areas in England and Wales from 1975 to 1996.
They find that falls in the wages of unskilled workers lead to increases in crime. The result is
robust to alternative low-wage measures, including a wage that accounts for changes in the
composition of employment, and it is reinforced by the effects of deterrence and of a measure of
the returns to crime [5].

**Canada.** Andresen analyses a panel of the ten provinces from 1981 to 2009, using unemployment,
low income and two measures of provincial product. All of these economic variables matter for
property and violent crime, but the sign and size of the estimates vary with context. He warns
that relying on a single economy-related variable can produce omitted-variable bias [6]. That is
evidence that labour-market conditions belong in any account of Canadian crime, not a single
elasticity that can be read off the latest annual release.

## 3. Official labour-market and crime data

Youth unemployment is taken from each country's official labour-force survey, following
International Labour Organization ideas: to be counted as unemployed, a person must be without
work, available, and actively seeking work. **[GOV-flag]** Headline unemployment excludes
discouraged people who have stopped searching. It is sensitive to survey response, seasonal
adjustment, and, for young people, to how students are classified. Governments are routinely
judged on these rates. Crime figures below are official series with their own problems, noted in
place.

**Canada.** In the Labour Force Survey for October 2026, the unemployment rate for ages 15 to 24
was 13.7 percent, up 0.7 percentage points from September, compared with 6.7 percent for ages 15
and over. Youth employment was 2.665 million and youth unemployment was 424,100 [7].
**[GOV-flag]** Statistics Canada also reported that in September 2025 the youth rate reached 14.7
percent, the highest September figure since 2010 except for 2020, and that in the third quarter of
2025 the rate for ages 15 to 19 was 20.8 percent, up from 12.6 percent in the same quarter of 2022
[8]. The police-reported youth crime rate (youth aged 12 to 17 accused of a Criminal Code
offence, excluding traffic) was 2,791 per 100,000 youth in 2024, down 4.2 percent, and 2,694 per
100,000 in 2025, down a further 3.8 percent. The Crime Severity Index had risen to 81.27 in 2023,
then fell to 77.9 in 2024 and 75.0 in 2025 [9][10]. **[GOV-flag]** Police-reported crime counts
only incidents that come to police attention. In the 2019 General Social Survey on victimization,
under one-third (29 percent) of incidents were reported to the police [9]. Youth rates also move
with charging and diversion practice.

**United States.** In the October 2026 Employment Situation, the unemployment rate was 4.1
percent for all persons aged 16 and over, 13.9 percent for ages 16 to 19, and 7.5 percent for ages
20 to 24 [11]. The Current Population Survey series for ages 16 to 24 stood at 9.3 percent in
October 2026. That combined youth rate had been as low as 6.6 percent in April 2023, and reached
10.6 percent in August and November 2025 [12]. **[GOV-flag]** These rates exclude discouraged
workers (448,000 in October 2026, all ages) and were interrupted in October 2025 because data were
not collected during a federal government shutdown [11][12].

**France.** In the third quarter of 2026, the ILO unemployment rate for ages 15 to 24 was 21.0
percent, down 0.5 points on the quarter but up 1.6 points on the year. The overall rate was 8.2
percent. The employment rate for ages 15 to 24 was 33.7 percent, down 0.8 points on the year. The
"halo" around unemployment — people who want work but do not meet the ILO definition — was 7.4
percent of 15-to-24-year-olds [13]. **[GOV-flag]** INSEE notes that implementation of the law on
full employment, including the registration of some RSA recipients and some young people with
France Travail, has contributed to the measured rise in unemployment. The ILO rate also excludes
the halo.

**Britain.** In the Office for National Statistics Labour Force Survey, the seasonally adjusted
unemployment rate for ages 16 to 24 was 15.1 percent in July to September 2026. The annual
average rose from 10.7 percent in 2022 to 11.9 percent in 2023, 14.0 percent in 2024 and 15.0
percent in 2025 [14]. The overall unemployment rate for ages 16 and over was 4.6 percent in the
same quarter [15]. **[GOV-flag]** ONS has warned that Labour Force Survey estimates have been
volatile, that response rates fell sharply in 2023–2024, and that short-term changes should be
read with caution and alongside other sources. For the year ending June 2026, the Crime Survey for
England and Wales estimated 2.7 million theft incidents, not a statistically significant change
from 2.8 million in the previous survey year, and 9.7 million incidents of headline crime, also
not a statistically significant change [16]. **[GOV-flag]** The crime survey and police-recorded
crime can diverge; recorded series move with reporting, counting rules and new fraud systems, and
both are used in political debate about whether crime is falling.

## 4. Argument, limits and options

The research in section 2 identifies a motivation effect: when legal earnings opportunities
worsen, property crime rises, and in France the effect is identified for youth unemployment. The
official data in section 3 show that the labour-market conditions which activate that effect are
present. In all four countries, youth unemployment is far higher than the all-ages rate, and
higher than early-2020s lows. That is not the same as saying that official crime has already risen
in lockstep. Canada's police-reported youth crime rate fell in 2024 and 2025, and survey-measured
theft in England and Wales was statistically unchanged in the latest year. Those facts bound the
argument. They do not cancel the identified elasticities. Crime series lag, miss a large share of
incidents, and can fall when charging or recording practice tightens even if the underlying
incentive has worsened. **Argument, not a measured offence count:** if youth joblessness remains
high where legal opportunity is thin, the research implies more property crime than would
otherwise occur, whether or not that increment is visible in the next national release.

Cantor and Land distinguish a motivation effect from an opportunity effect: higher unemployment
can also reduce crime by keeping people at home and reducing the circulation of people and goods
available to steal [17]. Andresen's Canadian results show that signs can change with the economic
measure and the setting [6]. Raphael and Winter-Ebmer find a much weaker effect for violent crime
than for property crime [2]. Gould, Weinberg and Mustard, and Machin and Meghir, find that wages
at the bottom can matter more than the unemployment rate itself [3][5]. Crime also depends on
deterrence, age structure, drug markets and policing. This paper does not treat a falling recorded
youth crime rate as proof that the motivation channel is inactive, and it does not treat a single
year of higher youth unemployment as proof of a crime wave.

If the mechanism is taken seriously, the direct response is to restore a legal earnings option
where it has disappeared: hiring, apprenticeships, and better wages at the bottom of the labour
market, especially where joblessness is highest. Income support that remains available when work
cannot be found preserves a legal alternative to illegal earnings. A falling police-reported youth
crime rate should not, on its own, be read as showing that the risk has passed.
