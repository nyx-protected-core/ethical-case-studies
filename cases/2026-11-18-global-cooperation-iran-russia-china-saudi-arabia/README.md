# One world: pathways to cooperation between Iran, Russia, China, Saudi Arabia and every other nation

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. Several of the precedents discussed here changed sharply in 2025 and 2026, and some are still moving. Facts are described as of mid-November 2026, from the sources listed at the end. Where governments disagree, each position is attributed to the government that holds it rather than presented as fact. Proposals marked "this study's own proposal" are suggestions made here. They are not positions of any cited author, institution or government. This study does not take the side of any government, including those of the four countries discussed or of the countries in dispute with them.*

*Scope note: an earlier draft of this study treated "the rest of the world" mostly as the United States, Europe and their allies. That framing was too narrow. Most of the world's people live in Asia, Africa, Latin America, the Middle East and the Pacific, and many of those states are already deeply tied to Iran, Russia, China and Saudi Arabia through trade, energy, food, debt, migration and shared institutions. This version treats the West as one important part of a multipolar picture, not as the whole of it, and asks how the whole world can cooperate with itself.*

## The ethical question

Iran, Russia, China and Saudi Arabia are very different countries. China is the world's second-largest military spender (SIPRI, 2026a). Russia is a nuclear superpower at war with Ukraine (UN General Assembly, 2022). Iran is under heavy sanctions and has been at war with the United States and Israel since February 2026 (IISS, 2026). Saudi Arabia is a close security partner of the United States that restored relations with Iran in 2023 (China, Saudi Arabia & Iran, 2023). What they share is that each is, for different reasons, in tension with large parts of the world, and each is home to people whose lives depend on how that tension is handled.

They are not isolated, though. All four sit inside a dense web of relationships that runs mostly through the Global South. China is the largest official creditor to developing countries (Horn et al., 2021). Russia and Saudi Arabia coordinate oil output with producers from Mexico to Kazakhstan and Malaysia through OPEC+ (OPEC, 2016). Saudi Arabia and Russia are among the world's top destinations for migrant workers, whose earnings support families from South Asia to Central Asia (Ratha et al., 2024). In 2023 BRICS invited Iran and Saudi Arabia, together with Argentina, Egypt, Ethiopia and the United Arab Emirates, to become full members from 2024. Argentina declined. The 2025 BRICS presidency lists Iran and Saudi Arabia among the members, although this study did not find a formal Saudi statement confirming membership (BRICS, 2023; BRICS Brasil, 2025). Iran also became a full member of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation in 2023 (SCO, 2023). For billions of people, how these four states relate to the world is not an abstract question of East versus West. It shows up in the price of bread, cooking fuel and fertiliser, in the money a relative sends home, and in whether a road, port or power plant gets built or becomes an unpayable debt.

The question this study asks is:

**What realistic pathways could bring Iran, Russia, China and Saudi Arabia into closer cooperative coexistence with every other nation, in every region, and help the whole world cooperate with itself, without asking anyone to stay silent about aggression or human rights abuses, and without punishing ordinary people for what their governments do?**

Four narrower questions follow:

1. When does trade and investment make conflict less likely, and when does it become a weapon?
2. Which forms of cooperation (science, health, food, climate, debt, arms control) have survived deep rivalry in the past, and why?
3. How can sanctions and other pressure be designed so that they do not fall hardest on people who have no say over their government, including people in third countries far from the dispute?
4. What can middle powers, regional bodies and global institutions do that the great powers cannot or will not do for themselves?

"Cooperative coexistence" here does not mean friendship, alliance or approval. It means a working relationship in which disputes are handled without war, shared dangers are managed together, and people in all countries can live, trade, study and stay healthy.

"The world" here means all of it. Acharya (2017) argues that the order now emerging is not a simple return to rival poles but a "multiplex" world: many interlinked centres of power, ideas and institutions, with rising states in Asia and the Global South shaping the rules alongside older Western ones. He and others have also argued that the study of international relations has long treated Western history as the universal story and the rest of the world as an afterthought (Acharya, 2014). This study tries not to repeat that mistake.

## Current state

**Military spending is at a record high.** World military spending rose for the 11th year in a row in 2025, to $2,887 billion, or 2.5 percent of world GDP. SIPRI estimates that China spent about $336 billion (12 percent of the world total), Russia about $190 billion (7.5 percent of its GDP) and Saudi Arabia about $83.2 billion (6.5 percent of its GDP). Iran spent about $7.4 billion, down 5.6 percent from 2024. The United States remained the largest spender at $954 billion. SIPRI marks the figures for China, Russia and Saudi Arabia as estimates (SIPRI, 2026a).

**Nuclear arsenals are growing again.** SIPRI estimates that the world had about 12,187 nuclear warheads in January 2026, of which about 9,745 were in military stockpiles and about 4,012 were deployed. China had about 620 warheads and is expanding its arsenal faster than any other country. SIPRI describes states as relying more on nuclear weapons "as instruments of national power", reversing decades of effort to reduce their number and role (SIPRI, 2026b).

**The last US–Russia arms treaty has lapsed.** New START expired on 5 February 2026, leaving no legally binding limits on US and Russian strategic nuclear forces for the first time in decades. Russia's foreign minister said Russia would keep to the treaty's central limits informally for as long as the United States does. The United States said it wants a new, "modernized" treaty that also addresses China's arsenal. China's disarmament ambassador said China "will not take part in nuclear disarmament negotiations at this stage", restating China's long-standing view that the United States and Russia must cut first (Liang, 2026).

**The Iran nuclear deal has unravelled, and its legal status is disputed.** The 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) gave Iran sanctions relief in return for limits on its nuclear programme and a ten-year monitoring framework. The United States withdrew in 2018, and Iran then reduced its commitments. In September 2025, France, Germany and the United Kingdom said the "snapback" mechanism had restored earlier UN sanctions. Russia and China reject this and argue that the Security Council's consideration of the issue ended on 18 October 2025. Because of this split, the Council has been unable to choose a chair for the relevant sanctions committee or appoint its panel of experts (UN Security Council, 2026). This study does not take a side on that legal dispute.

**Nuclear inspectors cannot see what they need to see in Iran.** The head of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) told the Security Council in October 2026 that inspectors had to withdraw for safety reasons after military attacks began in June 2025, and that Iran has not provided the information and access needed to verify previously declared nuclear material. Russia's representative disputed this account and said Iran had continued to engage with the IAEA (UN Security Council, 2026). Under the JCPOA, by contrast, the IAEA had verified in January 2016 that Iran was not enriching above 3.67 percent and held no more than 300 kg of low-enriched uranium (IAEA, 2016).

**The Gulf is at war.** The US–Israeli war with Iran began on 28 February 2026. According to the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), Iran's strikes quickly widened from US bases to civilian and dual-use targets across the Gulf states, and most Gulf states have reportedly responded militarily, although most have not confirmed it officially. IISS calls this "the first instance of large-scale direct military confrontation between the Gulf states and Iran" (IISS, 2026).

**Russia's war in Ukraine continues.** In March 2022, 141 UN member states voted for a General Assembly resolution demanding that Russia withdraw its forces from Ukraine; 5 voted against and 35 abstained (UN General Assembly, 2022). The war continued through 2025, when SIPRI estimates Ukraine spent about 40 percent of its GDP on its military (SIPRI, 2026a).

**The economic cost of splitting the world is large.** An International Monetary Fund staff study estimated that severe fragmentation of world trade could cost up to about 7 percent of global output in the long run, with some countries losing far more if technology flows are also cut (Aiyar et al., 2023).

**Sanctions are more common than ever.** Economists tracking sanctions since 1945 describe "a remarkable increase" in their use as a foreign-policy tool (Morgan et al., 2023), and the Global Sanctions Data Base was built to measure that growth across countries and decades (Felbermayr et al., 2020).

**The four countries matter for the climate.** Using the Global Carbon Budget's national data, China, Russia, Iran and Saudi Arabia together produced about 40 percent of the world's fossil carbon dioxide emissions in 2024: China about 31.8 percent, Russia about 4.6 percent, Iran about 2.1 percent and Saudi Arabia about 1.8 percent (Friedlingstein et al., 2026; shares calculated for this study from the published national data). No serious climate plan can succeed without them.

**Much of the world declines to pick a side.** The 141 votes for the 2022 General Assembly resolution on Ukraine came alongside 35 abstentions and 5 votes against (UN General Assembly, 2022), and many governments in Africa, Asia and Latin America have avoided taking sides since. At the G20 in Bali that year, under Indonesia's presidency, leaders could only agree that "most members strongly condemned the war in Ukraine" and that there were "other views and different assessments of the situation and sanctions" (G20, 2022). That formula did not hide disagreement; it recorded it, and it let the group keep working on food, energy and debt. In 2023, under India's presidency, the G20 welcomed the African Union as a permanent member and called for full implementation of both the Black Sea grain initiative and the parallel UN–Russia memorandum on getting Russian food and fertiliser to world markets, "particularly those in Africa" (G20, 2023).

**Wars far away raise prices everywhere.** The war in Ukraine pushed up the prices of food, fuel and fertiliser at once. A study using national economy-wide models for 19 developing countries in Africa and Asia, including Nigeria, Egypt, Kenya and Bangladesh, estimated that the shock pushed 27.2 million more people into poverty and 22.3 million more into hunger in the near term. It found that farming and poverty were more exposed to fuel and fertiliser prices, while hunger and diet quality were more exposed to food prices (Arndt et al., 2023). A global modelling study concluded that high energy and fertiliser prices did more damage to food prices, health and the environment than the loss of Russian and Ukrainian food exports themselves (Alexander et al., 2023). A third study, covering 116 countries, estimated that the energy price crisis raised household energy costs by roughly 63 to 113 percent and could push an additional 78 to 141 million people into extreme poverty (Guan et al., 2023). Energy from the Gulf and Russia, and grain and fertiliser from Russia and Ukraine, are not only geopolitical assets. They are part of the household budget of people in every region.

**Remittances tie these countries to the world's poorest households.** Officially recorded remittances to low- and middle-income countries were about $656 billion in 2023, more than foreign direct investment. Saudi Arabia is the world's second-largest source of remittances after the United States, and Saudi Arabia and Russia are among the top five destination countries for migrants. In 2023 remittances to Europe and Central Asia fell by 10 percent, mainly because of a slowdown in transfers from Russia to its neighbours, especially in Central Asia, after a 39 percent fall in the rouble against the dollar. Remittances were equal to about 39 percent of Tajikistan's GDP. Sending $200 still cost an average of 6.4 percent, more than twice the Sustainable Development Goal target of 3 percent (Ratha et al., 2024). When sanctions, currency shocks or wars hit Russia or the Gulf, families from Dushanbe to Dhaka to Cairo feel it.

**Debt links China to much of the developing world.** Horn, Reinhart and Trebesch (2021) document China's rise to become the largest official creditor to developing countries, and estimate that about half of its lending was not reported to the IMF or World Bank. AidData estimates that low- and middle-income governments together underreport about $385 billion in actual and potential repayment obligations to China, and that 35 percent of Belt and Road infrastructure projects have run into major implementation problems such as corruption scandals, labour violations, environmental hazards or public protests (Malik et al., 2021). The World Bank estimated that, if fully carried out, Belt and Road transport projects could lift 7.6 million people out of extreme poverty and raise trade in the corridor economies by 2.8 to 9.7 percent, but warned that about a quarter of those economies already had high debt and that benefits depend on transparency, debt sustainability and environmental and social safeguards (World Bank, 2019). The popular claim that China lends in order to seize assets in a "debt trap" is itself disputed: Brautigam (2020) examines cases including Angola, Djibouti, Sri Lanka and Venezuela and argues that the evidence does not support the story as usually told. Both the benefits and the risks are real.

**Each government's own stated interests.** A fair account has to set out, in their own terms, what these four governments say they want, and then hold those claims against the record of abuses and broken commitments described later.

- *China* published a Global Security Initiative Concept Paper in February 2023. It calls on all countries to abide by the purposes and principles of the UN Charter, to respect the "sovereignty and territorial integrity of all countries", and to treat "sovereign equality and non-interference in internal affairs" as basic principles of international law. It states that "humanity is an indivisible security community" and that "security of one country should not come at the expense of that of others", and argues that "abusing unilateral sanctions and long-arm jurisdiction does not solve a problem, but only creates more difficulties and complications" (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of China, 2023). Those are China's stated terms for cooperation. They sit alongside the human-rights findings and debt-transparency problems set out below, and alongside China's refusal to join nuclear disarmament talks until the United States and Russia cut first (Liang, 2026).
- *Russia*'s official Foreign Policy Concept of 31 March 2023 describes Russia as "a unique country-civilization" and "one of the sovereign centres of global development", with a mission of "building a multipolar international system". It says a small group of states is trying to replace the international legal system with "the concept of a rules-based world order", identifies "deployment or reinforcement of military infrastructure of unfriendly states" near Russia as a threat, names the North Atlantic Treaty Organization among the "unfriendly" European associations from which Russia says it must protect its security, and says Russia will work to "restore the UN's role as the central coordinating mechanism in reconciling the interests of UN Member States" and to "eliminate the vestiges of domination by the US and other unfriendly states in global affairs" (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the Russian Federation, 2023). That is Russia's stated view. It does not cancel the UN General Assembly's demand that Russia withdraw from Ukraine, or the findings of UN investigators on abuses there and at home (UN General Assembly, 2022; UN Human Rights Council, 2025; UN Human Rights Council, 2025b).
- *Iran* has long held that Article IV of the Non-Proliferation Treaty recognises the inalienable right of all parties to develop nuclear energy for peaceful purposes, including enrichment, and that this right cannot be bargained away (Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, 1968, Art. IV; see also the JCPOA's own preamble, which reaffirms that right). Iranian officials have presented the 2018 US withdrawal from the JCPOA, and later military strikes on nuclear sites, as proof that negotiated limits are not honoured. Those claims need to be heard; they do not dispose of the IAEA's later finding that, after inspectors withdrew in 2025, Iran had not provided the access needed to verify previously declared material (UN Security Council, 2026).
- *Saudi Arabia* has presented itself as a regional mediator and a partner in de-escalation, including through the 2023 agreement with Iran (China, Saudi Arabia & Iran, 2023). At the same time, IISS and other contemporary analyses describe official Saudi security concerns as centred on Iran's missile programme and Iran-aligned armed groups in Yemen and elsewhere, and describe Saudi policy in 2026 as combining diplomacy with deterrence, including a defence pact with Türkiye and Pakistan (IISS, 2026; Alghannam, 2026). Those security concerns are real to Riyadh. They do not erase the UN findings on executions, on the killing of Jamal Khashoggi, or on the war in Yemen, set out below.

**New groupings are growing, and their purpose is contested.** BRICS describes its expansion as serving "inclusive multilateralism" and the Global South (BRICS, 2023). The SCO now brings China, Russia, India, Pakistan, Iran and Central Asian states into one forum (SCO, 2023). Supporters see these bodies as a fairer voice for countries long under-represented in Western-led institutions; critics fear they could harden into blocs. China and Brazil, in a 2024 joint statement on Ukraine, said that "dividing the world into isolated political or economic groups should be opposed" (China & Brazil, 2024). Whether the new groupings become bridges or walls is one of the open questions of this decade.

## Root causes of distrust

### The security dilemma

When one state arms to feel safe, its neighbours often feel less safe and arm in turn, even if no one wants war. Jervis (1978) showed that this "security dilemma" is worst when defensive and offensive weapons look alike and when it is hard to tell what the other side intends. Kydd (2005) adds that what matters is whether each side believes the other is a "security seeker" or an expansionist. Reassurance can work, but only through costly signals the other side can believe.

### Arms control is hard to verify, and that is why it is rare

Coe and Vaynman (2019) argue that the main barrier to arms control is monitoring. Monitoring must make a state's weapons transparent enough to prove it is keeping its word, but not so transparent that it exposes the state to attack. They estimate that arms races driven by this problem account for almost 40 percent of all global arming over the past two centuries. Iran today is a sharp example: without inspector access, no one outside the Iranian government can confirm what has happened to its enriched uranium (UN Security Council, 2026).

### Broken promises and competing memories

Each government has its own account of who broke faith first. Western governments point to Russia's invasion of Ukraine and to Iran's reduced nuclear commitments. Russia points to NATO enlargement after the Cold War, a dispute that historians still argue about (Sarotte, 2021). Iran points to the United States leaving the JCPOA in May 2018. The IAEA's quarterly report of 22 February 2018, the last issued before that withdrawal, found that Iran was not enriching uranium above 3.67 percent, that as of 12 February 2018 its stock of uranium enriched up to 3.67 percent was 109.5 kg (well below the JCPOA cap of 300 kg of UF6, equivalent to 202.8 kg of uranium), and that the Agency "continues to verify the non-diversion of declared nuclear material" and had, since Implementation Day, "been verifying and monitoring the implementation by Iran of its nuclear-related commitments under the JCPOA" (IAEA, 2018). The UN Security Council later recorded that after the United States withdrew, Iran reduced its commitments (UN Security Council, 2026). These accounts cannot all be fully right, but each is sincerely held by many people, and any lasting settlement has to address them rather than dismiss them.

### Economic ties can be turned into weapons

Farrell and Newman (2019) show that states sitting at the centre of global financial and information networks, such as the dollar payment system and the SWIFT messaging network, can use those positions to watch others (a "panopticon effect") or to cut them off (a "chokepoint effect"). Targets learn from this. A Chatham House study finds that the growing number of Western sanctions has itself pushed China, Russia, North Korea and Iran into closer cooperation with one another (Chatham House, 2025).

### Human rights abuses deepen the gap

Distrust is not only about weapons. United Nations bodies have documented grave abuses in all four countries (see "Human rights" below). For many people elsewhere, closer ties with governments that commit such abuses feel like complicity. For the governments concerned, outside criticism is often seen as interference. This gap is real and cannot be wished away.

### Double standards, as seen from much of the world

Many governments and citizens in Africa, Asia, the Middle East and Latin America hear calls to "isolate" Iran or Russia, or to "de-risk" from China, as coming from states that have their own record of interventions and selective enforcement of rules. Some also see Western-led institutions as having given them too little voice; the BRICS expansion criteria, for example, explicitly include support for reforming the UN Security Council and "no imposition of unilateral sanctions" (BRICS Brasil, 2025). Equally, many people in these regions have reasons of their own to distrust the four governments: debts they cannot see clearly (Horn et al., 2021; Malik et al., 2021), wars that raise the price of food and fuel at home (Arndt et al., 2023), or abuses that UN bodies have documented (see "Human rights" below). A cooperation agenda that serves only one camp's grievances will not be trusted by the other camps.

### Exposure without a voice

The people most exposed to these rivalries often have the least say over them. A family in Tajikistan that depends on wages earned in Russia, a farmer in Kenya or Bangladesh who pays more for fertiliser, or a household in Egypt facing higher bread prices did not choose the wars or the sanctions that changed their budgets (Ratha et al., 2024; Arndt et al., 2023). Their governments are often price-takers in markets for energy, grain and credit that are shaped by a few large states. That gap between exposure and voice is itself a source of resentment and instability.

## What has worked historically

### Détente and the Helsinki Final Act (1975)

During the Cold War, the Soviet Union and Western states signed the Helsinki Final Act. The Soviet side wanted recognition of post-war borders. Western states insisted on including human rights commitments. Thomas (2001) shows that those human rights commitments, which many saw as empty words at the time, were taken up by citizen monitoring groups inside the Soviet bloc and by networks abroad, and became a lasting source of pressure for change. Snyder (2011) traces how this transnational "Helsinki network" kept human rights on the agenda of East–West diplomacy.

**Lesson:** a cooperation agreement can include human rights without collapsing, if each side also gets something it values and if independent civil society can use the text.

### Scientists talking across the Iron Curtain

Evangelista (1999) documents how unofficial contacts between Soviet and Western scientists, for example through the Pugwash Conferences, carried ideas about arms restraint into Soviet policy circles, ideas that later shaped real arms reductions.

**Lesson:** unofficial expert channels can keep ideas alive when official diplomacy is frozen.

### Track II diplomacy

"Track II" means informal dialogue among scholars, retired officials, religious leaders and others who can speak more freely than serving diplomats. Jones (2015) describes how such dialogue can feed new ideas into official talks, while warning that officials often misunderstand what it can do. A RAND study of Track II security dialogues in the Middle East and South Asia found them most useful as a slow process of shaping how elites think, rather than as a route to quick breakthroughs (Kaye, 2007). Rigorous measurement of Track II's effects is thin, so claims about it should be modest.

### Rivals cooperating on health: smallpox

In 1958 the Soviet Union tabled a proposal at the World Health Assembly for a worldwide smallpox eradication programme. It passed after intense diplomatic negotiation, and the effort later drew in a US-supported programme in western Africa (Bhattacharya & Campani, 2019). The disease was eventually eradicated. The same historians caution that the real story is more complex and contested than the usual heroic version.

**Lesson:** rivals can work together on a shared enemy that threatens everyone.

### The JCPOA (2015): verification can work while political will lasts

For several years the JCPOA did what it promised on paper. The IAEA verified in January 2016 that Iran's enrichment level and stockpile were within the agreed limits and installed equipment for continuous monitoring (IAEA, 2016). It was still reporting, in February 2018, that Iran was implementing those nuclear-related commitments (IAEA, 2018). In this study's judgement, the deal later unravelled for political reasons rather than because inspection had failed. The Security Council record describes the United States withdrawing in May 2018, Iran then reducing its commitments, and a later dispute over snapback; it does not itself judge why the bargain collapsed (UN Security Council, 2026).

**Lesson:** technical verification can be trusted; the hard part is keeping the political bargain alive across changes of government on all sides.

### The 2023 Saudi–Iran agreement

After seven years without diplomatic relations, Saudi Arabia and Iran agreed in Beijing in March 2023 to restore relations and reopen embassies within two months, affirm respect for each other's sovereignty and non-interference, and revive a 2001 security cooperation agreement. Their joint statement with China thanked Iraq and Oman for hosting earlier rounds of talks in 2021 and 2022 (China, Saudi Arabia & Iran, 2023). Those rounds were official contacts, not strictly Track II, but they show how quiet mediation by neighbours can prepare the ground for a public deal.

The agreement's record is mixed and still contested:

- *Evidence it endured:* a Carnegie analysis published in November 2026 says the diplomatic framework has survived even though the current war draws Saudi Arabia in on the side of the United States, and that Saudi and Iranian officials reaffirmed it in 2026 (Alghannam, 2026). IISS similarly says that before 2026 the de-escalation survived even though the agreement's terms were not fully implemented (IISS, 2026).
- *Evidence of its limits:* IISS reports that Riyadh "reportedly carried out limited retaliatory strikes" against Iran and Iran-backed militias in March 2026, after attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure, and argues that Iran disregarded its diplomatic arrangements with Gulf states during both the 2025 and 2026 wars (IISS, 2026). Carnegie notes that Saudi Arabia is now pursuing diplomacy and deterrence together, including a 2026 defence pact with Türkiye and Pakistan that could raise tensions with Tehran if it becomes an anti-Iran alliance (Alghannam, 2026).

**Lesson:** a channel kept open between rivals is worth a great deal in a crisis, even if it cannot stop a war on its own.

### Arctic science cooperation

The Arctic Council brings together the eight Arctic states, including Russia, and six Indigenous peoples' organisations. In March 2022 the other seven Arctic states paused their participation in Council meetings after Russia invaded Ukraine, while restating the Council's "enduring value" (Canada et al., 2022). Working-level project work resumed through written procedures in August 2023, and virtual working-group meetings resumed gradually from February 2024, while diplomatic-level meetings stayed paused (Arctic Council, 2024). In February 2026 the chair of the Senior Arctic Officials said political-level meetings were still paused, but scientific and working-group meetings continued with all states, and linked this resilience to the founding decision to keep security and defence outside the Council's remit (Arctic Council, 2026). Koivurova and Shibata (2023) examined which Arctic arrangements kept working and tested whether treaty-based cooperation, such as the Central Arctic Ocean fisheries agreement and the Arctic science cooperation agreement, held up better than soft-law forums. They report, for example, that the fisheries agreement's parties, including Russia, adopted rules of procedure by consensus in November 2022. That 2018 agreement, whose parties include Canada, China, Denmark (for the Faroe Islands and Greenland), Iceland, Japan, Korea, Norway, Russia, the United States and the European Union, commits them to prevent unregulated commercial fishing in the newly ice-free central Arctic high seas while joint scientific research proceeds, for an initial period of 16 years (Agreement to Prevent Unregulated High Seas Fisheries in the Central Arctic Ocean, 2018). It is a rare example of a precautionary deal reached *before* a resource conflict could start, and it includes both China and Russia. (This study did not confirm from a primary source when the agreement entered into force or how it has been carried out since 2022.)

**Lesson:** cooperation built on binding, specific, technical commitments may survive political shocks better than cooperation built on goodwill alone.

### Shared science facilities

- **ITER**, the fusion energy experiment being built in France, counts China, the European Union, India, Japan, Korea, Russia and the United States as members in a decades-long collaboration (ITER Organization, n.d.).
- **SESAME**, a synchrotron light source in Jordan, has Cyprus, Egypt, Iran, Israel, Jordan, Pakistan, Palestine and Türkiye as members, with China among its observers (SESAME, n.d.). Its members include states in deep dispute, such as Iran and Israel, sharing one research machine.

**Caution:** these projects show that "low-politics" cooperation is possible and can survive rivalry. The sources reviewed here do not show that it reduces rivalry on security issues.

### Climate and water

**US–China climate statements.** In 2014 the United States and China announced climate targets together: the United States set an economy-wide target of 26–28 percent below its 2005 emissions in 2025, and China said it intended to peak carbon dioxide emissions around 2030. They said they hoped to "inject momentum into the global climate negotiations" (The White House, 2014). In 2023, the Sunnylands statement set up a joint working group on energy transition, methane, circular economy, subnational action and deforestation, committed both countries to economy-wide 2035 climate targets covering all greenhouse gases, and even promised to "facilitate track II activities" (U.S. Department of State, 2023). These ties proved only as durable as domestic politics: the United States notified its withdrawal from the Paris Agreement on 27 January 2025, effective 27 January 2026 (UN Secretary-General, 2025). This study could not confirm whether the bilateral working group continues in any form.

**Saudi Arabia in climate talks.** A 2008 study described Saudi Arabia as a long-standing "obstructionist" in climate negotiations, taking part in order to slow or block agreement, while asking whether it might be moving beyond that role (Depledge, 2008). That study is old. Saudi Arabia's positions at recent climate conferences, and the results of its regional green initiatives, were not verified for this study, so no claim is made about them.

**Shared rivers.** A study of every reported event of water conflict and cooperation between nations over 50 years found that physical scarcity is only weakly linked to disputes. Institutions such as river-basin bodies and treaties matter as much or more, and most tension arises when rapid change outpaces institutions (Wolf et al., 2003). But "cooperation" between unequal neighbours is not always fair: the hydro-hegemony framework shows how treaties and knowledge production can also serve a stronger upstream state's control of a river (Zeitoun & Warner, 2006). This study did not examine specific basins shared by these four countries and their neighbours, so it makes no claim about how fair or effective those arrangements are.

**Does environmental cooperation build peace?** The first comprehensive review of the evidence found that environmental cooperation can help reduce violence within states and bring "symbolic rapprochement" within and between states, mainly by building understanding, trust and especially institutions. The same review found it unlikely to produce deep integration between states, and its effects depend strongly on context (Ide, 2019).

**Lesson:** climate, water and Arctic cooperation is best seen as a resilient floor that keeps channels open, not a proven route to security trust.

### Food in wartime: the Black Sea Grain Initiative

Even during the war in Ukraine, a UN- and Türkiye-brokered deal allowed about 32.8 million tonnes of grain and other food to leave Ukrainian ports between August 2022 and mid-July 2023, when Russia ended its participation (United Nations, 2023).

The 2023 G20 declaration, agreed by Western states, Russia, China, India, Saudi Arabia and the African Union among others, called for full implementation of both the grain initiative and the UN–Russia memorandum on food and fertiliser exports (G20, 2023). Even after the initiative ended, the principle that food and fertiliser should reach poorer countries during a war was one that rivals could sign together.

**Lesson:** narrow humanitarian deals can be made between states at war, but they need constant tending and can be withdrawn.

### Middle powers and small states as mediators

Much of the quiet work of keeping rivals talking has been done not by the great powers but by middle powers, small states and religious institutions.

- **Oman and Iraq** hosted the Saudi–Iran talks of 2021 and 2022 that led to the 2023 Beijing agreement (China, Saudi Arabia & Iran, 2023).
- **Qatar** has made mediation a central part of its foreign policy. Kamrava (2011) examines its mediation in Lebanon, Yemen and Sudan's Darfur conflict and argues that it rested on perceived neutrality, wealth and personal diplomacy at the top. It helped calm acute crises but generally did not resolve the underlying conflicts, partly because Qatar lacked the capacity to follow through on the ground.
- **Switzerland** has acted since 1980 as the "protecting power" for the United States in Iran, passing messages and providing consular services when the two states have no diplomatic relations (Swiss FDFA, n.d.).
- **The Holy See** hosted delegations from the United States and Cuba in October 2014 and offered its "good offices to facilitate a constructive dialogue", which preceded the two countries' announcement that they would restore relations (Vatican Radio, 2014).
- **Türkiye and Brazil** negotiated the 2010 Tehran Declaration with Iran, under which Iran agreed to deposit 1,200 kg of low-enriched uranium in Türkiye, with IAEA and Iranian observers, in return for reactor fuel (Brazil, Iran & Türkiye, 2010). The deal was never carried out. This study did not verify from primary sources why it failed, and the reasons are disputed, so no claim is made about them. What it shows is that middle powers could put a concrete, verifiable nuclear proposal on the table.
- **Türkiye and the UN** brokered the Black Sea Grain Initiative described above (United Nations, 2023).
- **The United Arab Emirates** reports that its mediation had produced exchanges of 4,641 prisoners of war between Russia and Ukraine by August 2025 (UAE Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2025). This figure comes from the mediator itself and was not independently verified for this study.
- **African states** sent a mission of leaders and envoys from Senegal, the Comoros, Zambia, South Africa, Egypt, the Republic of Congo and Uganda to Kyiv and St Petersburg in June 2023, with a peace initiative that included resuming negotiations, restoring grain exports, exchanging prisoners and returning children (Ramaphosa, 2023). It did not produce a ceasefire, but it put African priorities, especially food, at the centre of the conversation.
- **China and Brazil** issued joint "common understandings" on Ukraine in 2024, calling for de-escalation, an international peace conference "recognized by both Russia and Ukraine", protection of civilians and nuclear facilities, and opposition to dividing the world into blocs (China & Brazil, 2024). Ukraine and many Western governments did not accept this as a balanced framework, and its value is contested.

**Lesson:** mediators from outside the main rivalry, especially those trusted by both sides, can open channels the parties cannot open themselves. Their weakness is follow-through: they can broker a pause or a swap, but rarely enforce a settlement.

### Regional zones that bind the great powers

Regions can write rules that the great powers then sign. The five Central Asian states created a nuclear-weapon-free zone, and in May 2014 China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States all signed its protocol, which commits them not to use or threaten to use nuclear weapons against the zone's members (U.S. Department of State, 2014). The treaty also bars nuclear weapons from being stationed in the zone and requires its members to accept the IAEA's Additional Protocol, which gives inspectors wider access. The same source notes similar zones in Latin America and the Caribbean (Tlatelolco), Africa (Pelindaba), the South Pacific (Rarotonga) and Southeast Asia (U.S. Department of State, 2014). In Central Asia, a group of smaller states located between Russia and China obtained the same legal promise from rival nuclear powers, and accepted stronger inspection in return.

**Lesson:** regional agreements led by smaller states can create shared obligations for rivals who would not negotiate them bilaterally.

### Debt: rival creditors at one table

When Zambia defaulted, its official creditors formed a committee under the G20 Common Framework in June 2022 with 16 countries, co-chaired by France, as chair of the Paris Club, and China, and vice-chaired by South Africa. In June 2023 the committee reached agreement with Zambia on a debt treatment (Paris Club, 2023). The process was slow and painful for Zambians, but it put Chinese, Western and African creditors at the same table under a shared framework.

**Lesson:** practical problems such as unpayable debt can bring rival creditors into shared rules, if there is a forum where all of them sit.

### Small states shaping global rules

- **The Pacific.** A campaign that began with law students in the Pacific and was led by Vanuatu persuaded the UN General Assembly to ask the International Court of Justice for an advisory opinion on states' climate obligations (Sabin Center for Climate Change Law, 2025). The Court delivered it on 23 July 2025 (ICJ, 2025). According to a leading law-school summary, the Court affirmed that states have binding obligations to prevent significant environmental harm, to cooperate internationally and to protect human rights in the face of climate change (Sabin Center for Climate Change Law, 2025). The obligations apply to every state, including the four discussed here.
- **Food at the WTO.** In 2022, all WTO members, including China, Russia and Saudi Arabia, agreed not to impose export bans or restrictions on food bought by the World Food Programme for humanitarian purposes (WTO, 2022). This matters because export restrictions make world food price spikes worse: Martin and Anderson (2012) estimate that insulating behaviour by exporting and importing countries caused about 45 percent of the rise in world rice prices and 30 percent of the rise in wheat prices in 2006–08.

**Lesson:** the global rules most useful to ordinary people are often won by coalitions of small and middle states working through universal institutions, not by the great powers alone.

## Ethical tensions

### 1. Does trade make peace? The evidence is genuinely split

- *For:* using data from 1885 to 1992, Oneal and Russett (1999) found that democracy, economic interdependence and international organisations each significantly reduce the chance of militarised disputes.
- *Against:* using data from 1870 to 1938, Barbieri (1996) found that extensive interdependence *increased* the likelihood of militarised disputes, though it had no effect on whether full wars occurred, and that low to moderate interdependence may reduce disputes while very high interdependence may increase them.
- *Conditional:* Copeland (2014) argues that what matters is expectations. Leaders who expect trade to continue have reasons to keep the peace. Leaders who fear being cut off have reasons to act first.
- *Recent experience:* IISS notes that during the 2026 war Iran directed most of its strikes at the United Arab Emirates, its second-largest trading partner and home to over half a million Iranian expatriates, which IISS reads as showing the limits of relying on economic incentives alone (IISS, 2026).

This study treats the question as open. Trade is most likely to help when partners expect access to be stable and fair, and least likely to help when it is used as a lever.

### Realist objections: can institutions and trade keep the peace?

The most serious theoretical objection to a cooperation agenda is not that trade sometimes fails. It is the realist claim that, in an anarchic international system, institutions and economic ties cannot be relied on to keep the peace.

Kenneth Waltz argued that the absence of a world government, not misunderstanding or mistrust alone, forces states to look to their own security. Even states that want only to be safe can threaten one another, because no one can be sure of anyone else's future intentions (Waltz, 1979). John Mearsheimer pushed this further. In "The False Promise of International Institutions" he argued that institutions mainly reflect the distribution of power and have little independent effect on whether states go to war (Mearsheimer, 1994/95). In *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* he argued that great powers seek to maximise their share of power, that they worry about relative as well as absolute gains, and that they may rationally refuse cooperation that would leave them relatively weaker, even if it would make both sides better off in absolute terms (Mearsheimer, 2001). On this view, today's forums — the UN, BRICS, the SCO, OPEC+, a revived JCPOA, science projects — are at best useful talking shops and at worst a screen for power politics.

This study takes that objection seriously. Several findings already in this case line up with it: Coe and Vaynman (2019) show that fears about monitoring, not a lack of goodwill, explain much of the history of arms racing; Farrell and Newman (2019) show that economic networks can be weaponised; IISS reads Iran's 2026 strikes on a major trading partner as a warning against relying on trade alone (IISS, 2026); and the JCPOA held only as long as the political bargain held. Realists are right that verification, relative gains and the security dilemma are hard problems, not communications failures.

They are not, however, entitled to the last word. Oneal and Russett (1999), using data from 1885 to 1992, found that democracy, economic interdependence and international organisations each significantly reduced the chance of militarised disputes, even after allowing for "realist influences" such as distance and power predominance. The Helsinki Final Act shows that a formal commitment can be used by citizens and other states in ways the signing governments did not fully control (Thomas, 2001; Snyder, 2011). The IAEA's monitoring of the JCPOA, the Central Arctic Ocean fisheries agreement and smallpox eradication show that specific, verifiable bargains can work for a time, including among rivals, when each side gets something it values and cheating can be seen. The honest position is therefore conditional, not naive: cooperation can change incentives, but only where it is monitored, where the gains are hard to seize by force, and where no one is asked to ignore aggression or grave abuse. The proposals below are written to those constraints, not against them.

### 2. Sanctions: pressure on governments, harm to people

Sanctions are meant to change what governments do without war. The ethical problem is that their costs often fall on people with no power.

- Rodríguez, Rendón and Weisbrot (2025), studying 152 countries from 1971 to 2021, estimated that unilateral sanctions were associated with about 564,000 deaths a year, a toll comparable to armed conflict, with children under five most affected. They found the strongest effects for unilateral, economic and US sanctions, and no statistical evidence of an effect for UN sanctions. The paper states that it was funded by the Center for Economic and Policy Research, which readers may wish to weigh.
- Gutmann, Neuenkirch and Neumeier (2021), using a different method and sample, found the opposite ranking: an average episode of UN sanctions reduced life expectancy by about 1.2 to 1.4 years, compared with 0.4 to 0.5 years for US sanctions, and women were affected more severely than men.
- Peksen (2009) found that economic sanctions tend to *worsen* governments' respect for physical integrity rights, such as freedom from torture and political imprisonment, that broad sanctions are more harmful than targeted ones, and that multilateral sanctions have a greater overall negative impact on human rights than unilateral sanctions.
- Drezner (2011) reviews "smart" targeted sanctions aimed at leaders and elites, and finds that they reduce some humanitarian costs but are not a cure-all.

These studies disagree about which sanctions hurt most, but they agree that broad economic sanctions carry serious humanitarian costs. In 2022 the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 2664, a broad humanitarian exception to UN financial sanctions. Chatham House calls it a turning point, but notes that it does not cover sanctions imposed by individual states or the European Union, and that banks often "de-risk" by refusing lawful humanitarian payments anyway (Gillard, 2022).

The tension: dropping sanctions entirely could reward aggression and abuse. Keeping broad sanctions in place could cost many innocent lives. An ethical approach tries to keep pressure on decision-makers while keeping food, medicine and humanitarian payments flowing.

### 3. Human rights: engage without excusing

UN bodies have documented serious abuses in all four countries:

- **China:** the UN Human Rights Office found in 2022 that serious human rights violations had been committed in Xinjiang, and that the extent of arbitrary and discriminatory detention of Uyghurs and other mostly Muslim groups "may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity" (OHCHR, 2022). The Chinese government's response disputing the assessment is annexed to the same report (OHCHR, 2022). The record is not limited to Xinjiang. In 2022 the UN Human Rights Committee, reviewing Hong Kong, called for the National Security Law to be repealed and, pending repeal, not applied, citing vague offences, arbitrary use, and the arrest of "over 200 people, including 12 children" (UN Human Rights Committee, 2022; UN News, 2022). In February 2023, UN special-procedure mandate holders said they were alarmed by reports that about one million Tibetan children had been placed in government-run boarding schools, with consequences for language, culture and family life, and called on China to allow independent access (OHCHR, 2023).
- **Iran:** a UN fact-finding mission concluded in 2024 that the repression of the 2022 "Woman, Life, Freedom" protests involved crimes against humanity, including murder, imprisonment, torture, rape and gender persecution (UN Human Rights Council, 2024). The UN High Commissioner reported that at least 1,500 people were reportedly executed in Iran in 2025 (OHCHR, 2026). During the 2026 war, IISS documents Iranian strikes on civilian and dual-use targets in Gulf states, including ports, airports, hotels and water utilities (IISS, 2026). Those attacks belong in any human-rights and humanitarian-law account of the conflict, not only in the military narrative.
- **Russia:** the UN Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine found in 2025 that enforced disappearances and torture by Russian authorities amount to crimes against humanity (UN Human Rights Council, 2025). The repression is also domestic. The UN Special Rapporteur on the situation of human rights in the Russian Federation has documented a widening use of national-security laws to criminalise dissent and anti-war expression, including arbitrary detention, torture and politically motivated prosecutions (UN Human Rights Council, 2025b).
- **Saudi Arabia:** the UN High Commissioner reported that at least 356 people were reportedly executed in 2025, a new record, 78 percent of them for drug-related offences, including at least two people convicted of crimes committed as children (OHCHR, 2026). Two further, well-documented UN findings also belong here. In 2019 the UN Special Rapporteur on extrajudicial, summary or arbitrary executions found that the journalist Jamal Khashoggi "was the victim of a premeditated extrajudicial execution, for which the State of Saudi Arabia is responsible", and that there was "credible evidence, warranting further investigation of high-level Saudi officials' individual liability, including that of the Crown Prince" (OHCHR, 2019; Callamard, 2019). In 2020 the UN Group of Eminent International and Regional Experts on Yemen found that the parties to the conflict, including the Saudi-led coalition, had committed acts that may amount to war crimes, including murder of civilians, torture and the use of children in hostilities, and that coalition air strikes had not met the legal duty to take sufficient precautions and to assess proportionality. The same report also found that the Houthi authorities (the de facto authorities) had committed acts that may amount to war crimes, including indiscriminate attacks, the use of anti-personnel landmines, murder, torture and impeding humanitarian relief (UN Human Rights Council, 2020).

The same UN statement also criticised executions in the United States, which carried out 47 in 2025, the most in 16 years (OHCHR, 2026). Human rights standards apply to every government, including those that criticise others. Applying them evenly is part of what makes them credible.

The ethical tension is between two duties: not to legitimise abuse, and not to cut off the contact through which abuses are sometimes reduced. The Helsinki experience suggests both can be held at once (Thomas, 2001; Snyder, 2011).

### 4. Who speaks for the people?

Governments negotiate, but people live with the results. Citizens of these four countries are not their governments. Survivors of abuse, exiles, workers, scientists and ordinary families each have a stake. Cooperation that only serves governments may entrench the very abuses that cause distrust.

### 5. Sovereignty and accountability

Every state has a right to its own political system, and outside pressure has sometimes been used for self-interested ends. But sovereignty does not cover crimes against humanity or invading a neighbour. Holding both ideas at once is hard, and every government involved has been accused of double standards.

### 6. Blocs or bridges?

BRICS, the SCO and OPEC+ bring the four countries together with India, Brazil, South Africa, Indonesia, Egypt, Ethiopia, the United Arab Emirates, Central Asian states, Mexico, Kazakhstan, Malaysia and others (BRICS, 2023; BRICS Brasil, 2025; SCO, 2023; OPEC, 2016). These forums can be read in two ways. In one reading, they give under-represented countries a stronger voice and push older institutions to reform, as the G20's admission of the African Union did (G20, 2023). In the other, they risk becoming a rival bloc that deepens the split the IMF warns is costly for everyone (Aiyar et al., 2023). Several members also keep close ties with Western states; Saudi Arabia, for example, remains a close US security partner (IISS, 2026). Acharya (2017) argues that in a multiplex order such overlapping memberships are normal rather than a choice of sides. This study treats the outcome as open. It depends on whether these forums are used to build shared goods or to wage economic rivalry.

### 7. Development ties: shared goods or leverage?

Chinese lending and Belt and Road projects can bring real gains: the World Bank estimates meaningful reductions in poverty and higher trade if transport projects are well run (World Bank, 2019). Russian grain and fertiliser, and Gulf energy and jobs, are vital to many importing countries (G20, 2023; Ratha et al., 2024). But the same ties can create opaque debts (Horn et al., 2021; Malik et al., 2021), dependence that can be used as leverage (Farrell & Newman, 2019), and projects that harm workers or the environment (Malik et al., 2021). The ethical question is not whether these ties should exist, but whether they are transparent, fairly priced, open to scrutiny by the people who pay for them, and protected from being switched off as a weapon.

### 8. Neutrality: wisdom or complicity?

Many middle powers refuse to take sides in the wars involving Russia and Iran. Supporters argue that this keeps them able to mediate and protects their citizens from the costs of other people's quarrels; the African and China–Brazil initiatives and the UAE's prisoner exchanges depended on it (Ramaphosa, 2023; China & Brazil, 2024; UAE Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2025). Critics argue that neutrality towards aggression or atrocity is itself a choice that helps the stronger party. Both points have force. A reasonable test is whether a neutral state uses its position to reduce harm, for example through prisoner exchanges, grain corridors or humanitarian access, or only to avoid costs.

## Original proposals (this study's own)

These are this study's own suggestions. They are offered for discussion and are not endorsed by any cited source. They are designed to work across the whole world, through universal institutions where possible, and not only through bilateral deals between these four states and the United States or Europe.

### Proposal 1: A "humanitarian channel guarantee" for every sanctions regime (this study's own proposal)

Building on UN Resolution 2664 (Gillard, 2022), every state or group that imposes sanctions, whether the United States, the European Union, or any other government or regional body, would publish a standing humanitarian exception covering food, medicine, medical equipment, agricultural inputs such as fertiliser, and payments for them, with a named public bank channel that is legally protected from penalties. Each regime would publish an annual humanitarian impact review using the kinds of measures researchers already use, such as child mortality and life expectancy (Rodríguez et al., 2025; Gutmann et al., 2021). The review would also count effects on *third countries*, such as food and fertiliser importers in Africa and Asia (Arndt et al., 2023; Alexander et al., 2023) and migrant families who depend on remittances from sanctioned economies (Ratha et al., 2024). Sanctions that fail the review would be redesigned toward individual decision-makers.

### Proposal 2: Verification first, as a confidence-building ladder (this study's own proposal)

Because monitoring is the main obstacle to arms control (Coe & Vaynman, 2019), start with small steps that increase transparency without exposing military secrets: data exchanges on deployed strategic forces, notification of missile launches, and joint technical work on how to verify warhead numbers. For Iran, the immediate step is restoring IAEA access to declared material, which the IAEA says is the foundation for any diplomatic agreement (UN Security Council, 2026). Each step would be paired with a reciprocal, verifiable step by the other side. Middle powers would have a defined role: the 2010 Tehran Declaration (Brazil, Iran & Türkiye, 2010) shows that third countries can offer custody or observation arrangements, and the Central Asian zone shows that regional treaties can pair legal guarantees from nuclear powers with stronger IAEA access (U.S. Department of State, 2014). A Gulf-wide arrangement, in which Iran, Saudi Arabia and the other Gulf states all accept the IAEA Additional Protocol in return for security assurances from every nuclear-armed state, would be one rung on this ladder.

### Proposal 3: Neighbour channels and a roster of trusted mediators that stay open in wartime (this study's own proposal)

The Iraq- and Oman-hosted talks before the 2023 Saudi–Iran agreement (China, Saudi Arabia & Iran, 2023) and the survival of that channel through war (Alghannam, 2026) suggest a model: standing, low-profile channels between neighbours, hosted by trusted third countries, with a written commitment that they continue during crises. Their agenda would start with practical shared risks such as maritime incidents, attacks on civilian infrastructure, prisoner exchanges and the safety of nuclear facilities. Beyond the Gulf, a standing UN-registered roster of mediators, drawing on states and institutions with a record in this work such as Oman, Qatar, Iraq, Switzerland, Türkiye, Brazil, Indonesia, South Africa, the UAE and the Holy See (Kamrava, 2011; Swiss FDFA, n.d.; Vatican Radio, 2014; UAE Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2025; Ramaphosa, 2023), would let any pair of rivals call on a pre-agreed channel quickly, without either side having to admit it asked first.

### Proposal 4: A "shared dangers" science and health compact (this study's own proposal)

Using the ITER, SESAME and smallpox models (ITER Organization, n.d.; SESAME, n.d.; Bhattacharya & Campani, 2019), create a protected list of cooperation that continues regardless of political disputes: pandemic surveillance and vaccine research under the WHO Pandemic Agreement adopted in 2025 (WHO, 2025); Arctic and climate observation (Arctic Council, 2024; Koivurova & Shibata, 2023); and seed, crop and water research. Projects would be bound by specific, treaty-like commitments rather than goodwill alone, following the Arctic experience in which binding technical agreements kept working after political meetings stopped (Koivurova & Shibata, 2023; Arctic Council, 2026). Funding would come from many states in every region, so that no single government can switch it off, and facilities would be sited in the Global South as well as in Europe, so that the scientists and health systems that gain most are part of the work.

### Proposal 5: Human rights clauses with independent monitors, applied to everyone (this study's own proposal)

Following the Helsinki model (Thomas, 2001; Snyder, 2011), any new trade, investment, development or security framework involving these states, whether with Western states, BRICS partners, the SCO or African, Asian and Latin American partners, would include human rights and labour commitments that apply to *all* parties, with an independent monitoring body and a public reporting process that citizens and civil society can use. Commitments would be specific and measurable, such as ending executions for drug offences and for crimes committed as children (OHCHR, 2026), allowing UN investigators access, and protecting workers and communities affected by infrastructure projects (Malik et al., 2021).

### Proposal 6: Trade and food rules that make access predictable for everyone (this study's own proposal)

If Copeland (2014) is right that expectations of future trade matter most, then predictable rules matter more than trade volume. This would mean clear, published conditions under which trade restrictions would be lifted; no surprise measures on food, fertiliser, medicine or humanitarian goods; and dispute settlement through recognised institutions. It would extend the 2022 WTO decision that protects World Food Programme purchases (WTO, 2022) into a broader commitment by all major food, fuel and fertiliser exporters, including Russia, Saudi Arabia, the United States, the European Union, Brazil, India and others, not to impose sudden export bans that spike world prices (Martin & Anderson, 2012). It also means accepting that some technology and security restrictions will remain, but stating them openly and narrowly, to limit the fragmentation costs estimated by the IMF (Aiyar et al., 2023).

### Proposal 7: Climate and energy as a shared project (this study's own proposal)

Because these four countries together produce about 40 percent of fossil carbon dioxide emissions (Friedlingstein et al., 2026, with this study's calculation), invite them into joint, verifiable projects with measurable results, such as methane leak detection and reduction in oil and gas, grid connections for renewable power, and shared climate data. Partners would include not only Western states but also the climate-vulnerable states of the Pacific, Africa and South Asia, whose initiative led to an ICJ advisory opinion affirming that every state has a duty to cooperate on climate change (ICJ, 2025; Sabin Center for Climate Change Law, 2025). Because energy price spikes hurt poor households worldwide (Guan et al., 2023), producers coordinating output through OPEC+ (OPEC, 2016) would be invited to pair any supply decisions with support for the most exposed importing countries. These projects can create mutual benefit even when wider relations are poor. The evidence suggests environmental cooperation can build institutions and symbolic rapprochement but rarely deep integration (Ide, 2019), and that climate pledges can be reversed by domestic politics (UN Secretary-General, 2025). So this proposal should be tested, not assumed to work, and it should be backed by binding commitments rather than pledges alone.

### Proposal 8: A Global South–led "shared goods compact" (this study's own proposal)

This proposal would be led by middle powers and the Global South, not by any of the four countries or by the West. A coalition convened by middle powers such as Indonesia and India, which held the G20 presidency in 2022 and 2023 (G20, 2022; G20, 2023), together with Brazil, South Africa, Mexico, Nigeria and Türkiye, the African Union as a G20 member, ASEAN, and Pacific and Latin American states, would invite every major power, including Iran, Russia, China, Saudi Arabia, the United States and the European Union, to sign a short list of commitments that protect shared goods regardless of disputes:

1. Food, fertiliser and humanitarian goods are never targeted by sanctions or export bans (building on WTO, 2022 and G20, 2023).
2. Civilian nuclear facilities, undersea cables, pipelines and power grids are never attacked (a principle already found in the China–Brazil statement; China & Brazil, 2024).
3. Debts of the poorest countries are restructured through one shared table where every creditor, Chinese, Western, Gulf and private, sits together, building on the Zambia process (Paris Club, 2023), with all lending terms published (Horn et al., 2021; Malik et al., 2021).
4. Pandemic and climate cooperation continues in wartime (WHO, 2025; ICJ, 2025).

Signatories would report each year to the G20 and the UN General Assembly. The coalition's strength would be that it does not ask anyone to choose a bloc; its weakness is that it has no enforcement power beyond reputation. Its value would be tested by whether the great powers sign, and whether they keep their word when it is costly.

### Proposal 9: A people-first corridor for remittances and migrant workers (this study's own proposal)

Because Saudi Arabia, the other Gulf states and Russia are among the world's largest hosts of migrant workers, whose earnings support families across South Asia, Central Asia, Africa and the Middle East (Ratha et al., 2024), origin and destination countries would agree that remittance channels are kept open during sanctions and conflicts, with a legally protected payment route like the one in Proposal 1. They would also commit to cut the average cost of sending money toward the 3 percent SDG target (Ratha et al., 2024), and to protect migrant workers' wages, documents and safety, monitored by an independent body. This touches the daily lives of more ordinary people than almost any treaty on weapons.

## Risks

- **Legitimising abuse.** Closer ties could be used by governments as proof of acceptance while repression continues. Human rights clauses with real monitoring (Proposal 5) are meant to reduce this risk, but cannot remove it.
- **Rewarding aggression.** Easing pressure on a state that has invaded a neighbour, or that blocks nuclear inspectors, could encourage more of the same. Every step should be tied to verifiable action.
- **Dependence as a weapon.** Deeper economic ties create new chokepoints that can be used for coercion by either side (Farrell & Newman, 2019). This applies to energy, grain, fertiliser and credit from the four countries as much as to Western finance.
- **Blocs hardening.** New and expanded groupings could become rival camps rather than bridges, raising the costs of fragmentation for everyone (Aiyar et al., 2023). Overlapping membership and universal institutions are the best guard against this.
- **Debt and opacity.** Development ties can leave poorer countries with hidden or unpayable debts (Horn et al., 2021; Malik et al., 2021). Cooperation that is not transparent can harm the people it is meant to help.
- **Mediators without follow-through.** Small-state mediation can calm crises without resolving them (Kamrava, 2011), and mediators' own reports of success may be hard to check independently.
- **Neutrality as cover.** A refusal to take sides can be used to avoid costs rather than to reduce harm.
- **Track II as cover.** Informal channels can be used for propaganda or intelligence gathering. Participants need clear rules and honest expectations (Jones, 2015; Kaye, 2007).
- **Science projects as technology transfer.** Shared research can raise real dual-use and security concerns. Projects should be chosen for open, civilian purposes.
- **Deals that collapse.** The JCPOA, New START, the 2010 Tehran Declaration and the Black Sea Grain Initiative all ended or unravelled (UN Security Council, 2026; Liang, 2026; Brazil, Iran & Türkiye, 2010; United Nations, 2023). Broken agreements can deepen distrust and make the next one harder.
- **Uncertain evidence.** The research on trade and peace, on sanctions' human costs and on the "debt trap" is contested. Policies built on it should be reviewed regularly and changed when evidence changes.
- **Bad faith on any side.** None of these pathways work if a government is determined to dominate or to destroy another. Cooperation is a tool for managing rivalry, not a guarantee against it.

## Recommendation

There is no single key that unlocks cooperation between Iran, Russia, China, Saudi Arabia and the rest of the world. Nor is "the rest of the world" one actor. It is Lagos and Jakarta, Dushanbe and Dhaka, São Paulo, Nairobi and Port Vila as much as Washington or Brussels. History suggests something slower and more practical: keep channels open, verify rather than trust, protect people everywhere from the costs of disputes between governments, keep human rights on the table for everyone, and let the countries most exposed to great-power rivalry help write the rules.

In order of urgency, this study recommends:

1. **Protect people first, everywhere.** Make humanitarian exceptions to all sanctions real and usable, keep food, fertiliser and remittances flowing to third countries, and measure the human costs every year (Proposals 1 and 9).
2. **Restore verification.** Get IAEA inspectors back to Iran's declared material, build regional verification with middle-power help, begin rebuilding US–Russia transparency, and invite China into risk-reduction talks even if it refuses to join arms reduction talks yet (Proposal 2).
3. **Keep neighbour and mediator channels open in war.** Strengthen the kind of quiet mediation by Oman, Iraq, Qatar, Switzerland, Türkiye, the UAE, African states and others that has produced the 2023 Saudi–Iran agreement, prisoner exchanges and grain deals (Proposal 3).
4. **Back a Global South–led shared goods compact** that asks every major power to protect food, infrastructure, debt fairness, health and climate cooperation regardless of disputes (Proposal 8).
5. **Protect shared science, health, food, trade and climate work** from political shocks through binding, specific commitments open to every state (Proposals 4, 6 and 7).
6. **Hold every government to the same human rights standards**, with independent monitoring built into any new framework, whoever the partners are (Proposal 5).

Ending hostility is beyond any one study. Reducing the harm it does to ordinary people in every region, and keeping open the paths that have worked before, is within reach.

### Revisions (18 November 2026)

This study was revised on 18 November 2026 after an independent balance review. The revisions correct the claim that the IAEA had found Iran in compliance in 2018, so that it now cites the IAEA's February 2018 quarterly verification report rather than a 2016 Implementation Day document; add a fair statement of realist objections to cooperation-based peace, and this study's response; add the 2019 UN Special Rapporteur findings on the killing of Jamal Khashoggi and the 2020 UN Group of Eminent Experts findings on Yemen, including Houthi as well as coalition violations, to the Saudi human-rights entry; add UN findings on domestic repression in Russia, on Hong Kong and Tibet, and on Iranian strikes on civilian and dual-use targets in the Gulf; add each of the four governments' own stated interests from primary documents; attribute the judgement that the JCPOA unravelled for political reasons to this study, rather than to a UN press release that does not say that; restore IISS's "reportedly" hedge on Saudi retaliatory strikes; and note Peksen's finding on multilateral sanctions.
