# Moving money to where hunger is: social and financial mechanisms that widen worldwide participation in ending food insecurity

*A case study prepared on 18 November 2026. Every figure comes from the sources listed at the end, checked in November 2026 through doi.org, Crossref or the publisher's own page. Figures from different reports use different years, methods and coverage, so they should not be added together. Calculations marked "(this study's own calculation)" are simple arithmetic on the cited figures, not numbers any source states. Proposals marked "(this study's own proposal)" are this study's suggestions, not positions of any cited author or institution. Anything that could not be confirmed is marked "unverified" or left out.*

## The ethical question

A companion case on food insecurity in this collection argued that hunger is mainly a problem of access and price, not of total food supply. This case asks the follow-on question: **where should the money come from, through which channels should it move, and who gets a say?**

The world already moves very large sums through food and agriculture. Most of it is not aimed at hunger. Governments spend around half a trillion dollars a year supporting their own farmers, while official aid and other official flows for food security and nutrition come to a fraction of that (FAO, UNDP & UNEP, 2021; FAO et al., 2024). Military spending is larger still (Liang et al., 2026).

So the ethical question is: **is it right to keep money flowing through channels that mostly do not reach hungry people when better-tested channels exist, and which mechanisms let more countries, communities and faiths take part in paying for, and deciding about, an end to hunger?**

Four narrower questions follow:

1. Is it fair to ask farmers in rich and middle-income countries to give up support they rely on, and how should they be compensated?
2. Should money be released *before* a crisis, on a forecast, even if the forecast is sometimes wrong?
3. When private investors are brought in, who carries the risk, and who keeps the gains?
4. Should faith-based giving, migrants' own money and levies on travellers be steered towards hunger, and who decides how they are spent?

## Scale with real figures

**The need.**

- About **645 million people** faced hunger in 2025, or 7.8% of the world's population (FAO et al., 2026).
- The 2026 *Global Report on Food Crises* counted **266 million people** in 47 countries and territories facing high levels of acute food insecurity in 2025 (Global Network Against Food Crises & FSIN, 2026).

**What it would cost.** Estimates differ by a factor of more than twenty, depending on the model and the goal.

- The 2024 edition of *The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World* (SOFI), whose theme was financing, reviewed the cost studies. Getting on track for the hunger and nutrition targets would need **additional resources from now until 2030 of between US$176 billion and US$3.98 trillion** to eradicate undernourishment, plus US$77–90 billion for selected undernutrition targets. Adding policies to make healthy diets affordable raises the estimate to US$15.4 trillion. These are cumulative totals to 2030, not annual figures (FAO et al., 2024).
- The Ceres2030 project (Cornell University, IFPRI and IISD) estimated that ending hunger sustainably by 2030 needs an **extra US$33 billion a year**. Donors would provide US$14 billion of that, roughly doubling their food-security aid, and low- and middle-income countries the other US$19 billion (Laborde, Parent & Smaller, 2020).

**What is spent now.**

- Official development assistance and other official flows for food security and nutrition came to **US$77 billion in 2021**. Of that, US$35 billion went to food consumption, US$27 billion to the drivers of food insecurity, and US$15 billion to health status (FAO et al., 2024).
- Over 2020–2022, about **US$1.2 billion a year** of blended-finance deals were aligned with the hunger goal (SDG 2), according to SOFI's reading of Convergence data (FAO et al., 2024).

**The money already in the system.**

- **Support to agricultural producers**: almost **US$540 billion a year** (2013–2018 average across 88 countries), about 15% of the value of farm output. Some **87%, roughly US$470 billion**, takes the form of price incentives and coupled subsidies that the report calls distorting and often harmful to nature, health and equity. On current trends it could reach almost **US$1.8 trillion by 2030** (FAO, UNDP & UNEP, 2021).
- **World military spending**: **US$2,887 billion in 2025**, the 11th rise in a row (Liang et al., 2026).
- **Remittances** to low- and middle-income countries: about **US$685 billion in 2024**, more than foreign direct investment and official aid combined (World Bank, 2024a).

**Comparisons (this study's own calculation).**

- Producer support is about **7 times** the 2021 aid flows for food security and nutrition (US$540 billion ÷ US$77 billion). The two figures cover different years.
- The Ceres2030 annual gap of US$33 billion equals about **6.1% of producer support**, or about **1.1% of 2025 world military spending**.
- The donor share alone, US$14 billion, equals about **0.5% of military spending**.

## Root causes: why the money does not reach hunger

1. **Support is locked in to producers, not to people who cannot afford food.** Most farm support is tied to particular commodities or kept in place through trade barriers. That favours larger producers and staple commodities over nutritious foods, and reaches smallholders, many of them women, the least (FAO, UNDP & UNEP, 2021). The World Bank–IFPRI study found that farmers get only about **35 cents of benefit for each dollar of support** (Gautam et al., 2022).
2. **Crisis money arrives late.** Most humanitarian funding is raised by appeal after a crisis is visible. The evidence from anticipatory transfers suggests the weeks before a shock peaks matter most (Pople et al., 2026).
3. **Debt crowds out food spending.** Many low-income countries spend heavily on debt service. A debt swap frees some of that money, but only for the part of the debt it covers (Chamon et al., 2022; Hurley & Martin, 2024).
4. **Pooled funds depend on voluntary, shrinking budgets.**
   - The UN Central Emergency Response Fund (CERF) allocated **US$451.6 million in 2025**, down from US$574.9 million in 2024 (CERF, 2026). That is a fall of about 21% (this study's own calculation).
   - The WFP Immediate Response Account (IRA) advanced **US$196.1 million in 2024**, less than half the US$433.5 million of 2023 (WFP, 2025).
   - IFAD's 13th replenishment reached **US$1.57 billion in pledges, 84% of its US$1.875 billion target** (IFAD, 2025).
5. **Large private and social money flows sit outside the system.** Remittances, faith-based giving and diaspora funds are huge, but they move privately. Remittances are also costly to send: in late 2023 the average cost of sending US$200 was **6.4%**, more than double the 3% SDG target (World Bank, 2024b).
6. **Many countries take part only as recipients.** Most of the mechanisms are designed and funded by a small group of donors, which narrows ownership and makes them politically fragile.

### 1. Repurposing agricultural support

The FAO, UNDP and UNEP report argues for **repurposing** support rather than simply removing it. Its plan is to phase out the most distorting price incentives and coupled subsidies and to redirect the money to public goods such as research and development and infrastructure, and to decoupled payments. It sets out six steps governments can follow and stresses protecting vulnerable groups, including smallholders, from short-term losses (FAO, UNDP & UNEP, 2021).

The World Bank–IFPRI modelling shows why the details matter (Gautam et al., 2022):

- **Simply removing support** lowers farm output and raises poverty, with only modest climate gains.
- **Making support conditional** on greener but lower-yielding practices cuts emissions, but raises food prices, poverty and land use.
- **Redirecting about US$70 billion a year**, about 1% of global farm output, into innovations that raise productivity and cut emissions could cut agricultural emissions by more than 40% and return 105 million hectares to nature. It would also bring net benefits of over US$2 trillion in 20 years and lower the cost of healthy diets.

*Assessment.* This is the largest pool of money in this study, and it is already being spent. The evidence on cost-effectiveness is modelled, not yet observed at scale. It works for participation, because every country with a farm policy can take part, but it is politically hard.

### 2. Debt-for-food and debt-for-nature swaps

In a debt swap, a creditor cancels or reduces debt on condition that the debtor spends an agreed amount in local currency on an agreed purpose.

- **Egypt and Italy** ran three debt-for-development tranches: US$149.09 million in 2001, US$100 million in 2007 and US$100 million in 2012. Part of the money went to food security, nutrition and school feeding projects (Egypt Ministry of International Cooperation, n.d.).
- **Spain** released **Guinea-Bissau** from about US$12 million of debt on condition that Guinea-Bissau invest US$6.7 million with WFP against malnutrition (WFP, n.d.).
- The largest nature swap so far is **Ecuador's 2023 Galápagos deal**, which exchanged US$1.628 billion of bonds for a US$656 million loan. It brought more than US$1.126 billion in lifetime debt-service savings and an estimated US$323 million for marine conservation over 18.5 years (US International Development Finance Corporation, 2023).

The overall scale has been small. A 2024 study for the School Meals Coalition counted **128 bilateral debt swaps across 38 countries**, covering about US$6.1 billion of debt. They freed **about US$3.3 billion**, roughly **US$165 million a year**, compared with about US$119.5 billion under the HIPC and MDRI debt relief initiatives (Hurley & Martin, 2024). That annual figure is about 1.2% of the Ceres2030 donor gap (this study's own calculation).

IMF researchers conclude that partial, conditional relief is generally **less efficient than conditional grants or broad debt restructuring**, because part of the benefit goes to creditors who are not in the deal. Swaps can still help when grants or wider relief are off the table, or when the spending commitment can be made effectively senior to debt service (Chamon et al., 2022).

*Assessment.* Swaps are useful in specific settings, such as a creditor with a bilateral loan and a debtor with a ready school-meals or nutrition programme. They are not a substitute for debt relief.

### 3. Pooled funds

Pooled funds let many donors, including smaller and newer ones, put money into a shared mechanism with joint rules.

- **IFAD** lends to and supports smallholder and rural development through replenishments. IFAD13 received US$1.57 billion in pledges, 84% of target (IFAD, 2025). The IFAD14 pledging session had not concluded when this study was written, so no IFAD14 total is reported here.
- **The Global Agriculture and Food Security Program (GAFSP)** was set up after the 2009 L'Aquila pledges. It has deployed **US$2.4 billion** in grants and concessional finance to countries, producer organisations and agribusinesses (GAFSP, n.d.).
- **The WFP Immediate Response Account** is a revolving last-resort reserve for life-saving help when no other funding is available. Its 2024 advances of US$196.1 million covered 35 country plans (WFP, 2025).
- **The CERF** has two windows: rapid response and underfunded emergencies. In 2025 it allocated US$241.0 million through the first and US$210.5 million through the second (CERF, 2026).

*Assessment.* Pooled funds are the backbone of the system. They are fast, they let smaller donors take part, and they reach neglected crises. Their weakness is that they rely on voluntary contributions that fall when donor budgets shrink, as the 2024–2025 figures show.

### 4. Anticipatory and forecast-based financing

- **Evidence that acting early helps.** WFP gave cash transfers in Bangladesh in 2020 to households forecast to face extreme flooding, about five days before the flood peak. A study of that programme found that recipients had **better welfare during the flood and three months afterwards, and lost fewer assets**. The gains came in the months before a conventional humanitarian response would have arrived (Pople et al., 2026).
- **The Start Network's Start Fund** is run by its member NGOs. It disburses money **within 72 hours** of an alert, including alerts based on forecasts (Start Network, n.d.).
- **The African Risk Capacity (ARC)**, an African Union agency, pools drought risk across member governments through parametric insurance, which pays out when a model or index crosses a trigger. Its weakness showed in **Malawi in 2016**. The model's crop assumptions did not match what farmers were growing, so it at first did not trigger. After the model was re-customised, ARC paid **US$8.1 million**, following a revised implementation plan submitted in December 2016 (ARC, 2024). Johnson (2021) documents several African cases in which parametric insurers made discretionary *ex gratia* payments after their models failed to match real losses. That raises the question of what such cover is really worth to the governments that pay premiums for it.

*Assessment.* There is good experimental evidence for early cash. Pooling drought risk regionally lets African states take part as owners, not just recipients. The main risk is "basis risk", when the model and reality diverge. Contracts need independent checks on the model and a clear, fair way to resolve failed triggers.

### 5. Shock-responsive social protection and cash transfers

Cash has the strongest evidence base of any mechanism here.

- In Kenya, GiveDirectly's unconditional transfers raised household monthly consumption from **US$158 to US$193** (in purchasing-power terms). Monthly payments improved food security more than lump sums (Haushofer & Shapiro, 2016).
- A larger trial gave about US$1,000 per household across 653 villages. It found gains for non-recipients and local firms, little inflation, and a **local multiplier of about 2.5**: each dollar transferred generated about US$2.50 in local economic activity (Egger et al., 2022).
- Reviews across low- and middle-income countries find that cash transfers improve food expenditure and consumption (Bastagli et al., 2018).
- Comparisons of cash, vouchers and food aid suggest that cash is usually cheaper to deliver, and that the right form depends on how well markets work (Gentilini, 2016; Alderman et al., 2017).
- National systems that can scale up in a shock, such as Ethiopia's Productive Safety Net Programme, show that a standing system improves food security (Gilligan et al., 2009).

*Assessment.* This is the most cost-effective core for most of the other mechanisms to feed into. Countries design and run their own systems, which makes them strong for national participation and ownership. The Kenyan multiplier comes from particular market settings, and may not hold where markets are thin or cut off.

### 6. School meals coalitions

- At least **466 million children** received school meals in 2024, almost 80 million more than four years earlier (WFP, 2024).
- The **School Meals Coalition**, launched in 2021, has **113 member countries**, 6 regional bodies and over 160 partners (School Meals Coalition, n.d.).
- A 14-country modelling study estimated **benefit–cost ratios of about 7 to 35**, counting returns to health, education, social protection and local agriculture, though the results are sensitive to assumptions about wages (Verguet et al., 2020).
- Trial evidence is more modest. In Ghana, school meals raised height-for-age mainly in girls and in children from poor households (Gelli et al., 2019). A Cochrane review found small average benefits (Kristjansson et al., 2007).

*Assessment.* School meals are strong for participation. Most of the cost is paid from countries' own budgets, and the coalition links governments of every income level. The modelled returns are high, while trial-measured nutrition effects are smaller and concentrated among the poorest children.

### 7. Islamic social finance: zakat, waqf and the IsDB

- **Zakat** is an obligatory alms tax in Islam, and **waqf** is a charitable endowment. The Islamic Development Bank (IsDB) Group has estimated the potential of zakat alone at **up to US$1 trillion a year** (Rehman, 2019). That is a potential figure, not money actually collected. Other estimates circulating in the press could not be traced to a primary source and are not used here.
- UNHCR's **Refugee Zakat Fund** received **over US$23 million in zakat in 2025**. It distributes all of it, under its stated 100% zakat-distribution policy, backed by 18 fatwas and scholarly endorsements (UNHCR, 2026).
- In 2022 the IsDB Group endorsed a **US$10.54 billion Food Security Response Program** for its member countries (Islamic Development Bank, 2022).

*Assessment.* This channel reaches communities and governments that often sit outside Western-led donor structures, which is a major plus for participation. Rigorous evaluations of zakat- or waqf-funded food programmes are scarce: this study found none at scale, so cost-effectiveness is **unverified**. Strong governance and transparency standards are the key to trust.

### 8. Remittance-linked food programmes

- **Evidence.** Data from 92 low- and middle-income countries show that both domestic and international remittances reduce food insecurity. International remittances have the larger effect, and the protection is greatest in lower-income countries (Smith & Floro, 2020). In rural Mexico, remittances reduced food insecurity but did not make households food secure. The authors warn that remittances should complement, not replace, public responsibility (Mora-Rivera & van Gameren, 2021).
- **The cost of sending money.** Cutting the average cost of sending money from 6.4% to the 3% SDG target, applied to US$685 billion, would leave roughly **US$23 billion a year** more with families (this study's own calculation). This is an upper-bound illustration: the 6.4% is the average cost of sending US$200, and larger transfers usually cost less as a share (World Bank, 2024a, 2024b).
- **No direct evidence found.** This study did not find rigorous evaluations of specific schemes that let migrants send money straight to food programmes, such as diaspora matching funds for school meals. Their effectiveness is **unverified**.

*Assessment.* The ethical way to use remittances is to make sending them cheaper and safer, not to tax them or steer them. That money belongs to migrant families.

### 9. SDR rechanneling: the IMF Resilience and Sustainability Trust

- **Background.** The IMF's 2021 general allocation of special drawing rights (SDRs), its reserve asset, came to about **US$650 billion**. Because SDRs are distributed in proportion to IMF quota shares, only about US$275 billion went to emerging and developing countries, and about US$21 billion to low-income countries (IMF, 2021).
- **The Trust.** The Resilience and Sustainability Trust (RST) lets countries with strong reserves channel SDRs into long-term loans for climate and pandemic resilience. By mid-2025, pledges had reached about **SDR 35.8 billion (about US$48.9 billion)**.
- **The terms.** Loans run for 20 years, with a 10½-year grace period. A borrower must have a concurrent IMF-supported programme, which means conditions are attached.

These RST figures were checked through search results that return the IMF's own pages and policy papers, because the IMF site did not load directly (IMF, 2025a, 2025b).

During the 2022 food crisis, the IMF also opened a temporary **Food Shock Window** inside its emergency lending (IMF, 2023).

*Assessment.* SDR rechanneling creates fiscal space but is not designed around food. The conditions attached limit who can take part.

### 10. Solidarity levies

- **The airline-ticket levy.** France introduced it on 1 July 2006, ranging from €1 to €40 per ticket depending on distance and class. Its revenue sits outside the general budget and finances health programmes in developing countries (France, n.d.). Cameroon, Chile, the Republic of the Congo, Madagascar, Mali, Mauritius, Niger and South Korea have also adopted airline levies for development, so some countries that receive aid also act as donors (France, n.d.).
- **Unitaid.** France has been Unitaid's largest contributor, providing **56% of all its funding** from 2006 to 2022 (France, Ministry for Europe and Foreign Affairs, 2023).
- **Financial transaction taxes.** The European Commission's 2011 proposal for an EU-wide tax was estimated to raise **about €57 billion a year** (European Commission, 2011). An enhanced-cooperation version for 11 member states was projected at €30–35 billion a year. It was never adopted, and the Commission announced its withdrawal in its 2026 work programme (European Parliament, n.d.).

*Assessment.* Levies are predictable and spread the effort widely, but they have not been dedicated to food. A financial transaction tax has repeatedly failed politically.

### 11. The G20 Global Alliance Against Hunger and Poverty

The Alliance was launched at the G20 summit in Rio de Janeiro on 18 November 2024. It began with **148 founding members, including 82 countries**, the African Union, the European Union, international organisations, financial institutions and NGOs (Global Alliance Against Hunger and Poverty, 2024). A year later it had **over 200 member countries and organisations**. Ethiopia, Haiti, Kenya, Palestine and Zambia had unveiled multi-partner plans for large national programmes (Global Alliance Against Hunger and Poverty, 2025).

*Assessment.* This is the broadest coalition in the field, and it is built around country-led plans, which is good for participation. It matches countries with funders rather than creating a new fund, and its cost-effectiveness is too early to judge.

### 12. Regional food reserves: APTERR

The **ASEAN Plus Three Emergency Rice Reserve (APTERR)** has been in force since 2012. It links the ten ASEAN states with China, Japan and South Korea (APTERR, n.d.).

- **Earmarked reserves.** Members hold **787,000 tonnes** of rice earmarked for each other: 87,000 tonnes from ASEAN and 700,000 tonnes from the Plus Three countries.
- **Stockpiled reserves.** A collective stockpile funds humanitarian releases. For example, it released 3,500 tonnes after a devastating earthquake in Myanmar (APTERR, 2025).

*Assessment.* APTERR keeps rival powers cooperating on a shared good. The economics literature warns that public grain reserves are costly and work best when kept small and paired with open trade (Wright, 2011). It also stresses that export restrictions drive price spikes (Martin & Anderson, 2012). The released volumes have been modest.

### 13. Blended finance and its critiques

Blended finance uses public money to lower risks for private investors. ODI found that each US$1 of development-bank and development-finance-institution money mobilised **only US$0.37 of private finance in low-income countries**, against US$1.06 in lower-middle-income countries (Attridge & Engen, 2019). SOFI counts about **US$1.2 billion a year** of blended deals aligned with the hunger goal (FAO et al., 2024).

*Assessment.* Blended finance can help agribusiness and infrastructure in middle-income settings. It mobilises little in the poorest, hungriest places, and it can divert scarce grants towards deals that would have happened anyway.

### 14. Results-based financing

In pay-for-results schemes, money is paid only when agreed outcomes are verified. AgResults, a multilateral initiative backed by four governments and the Gates Foundation, ran prize competitions for farm technologies.

An evaluation of AgResults found that such prizes can build **competitive, smallholder-inclusive markets** under certain conditions (Narayan et al., 2022):

- multi-year, proportional prizes work better than single grand prizes;
- the enabling environment is decisive;
- private action alone may not solve the problem.

*Assessment.* This is a useful targeted tool for specific market failures. It is not a general hunger-financing mechanism.

### 15. The share of military spending that could be redirected

SIPRI researchers estimate that redirecting **7–13% of 2024 world military spending** would have covered developing countries' gap in finance for climate adaptation (Tian & Liang, 2025). By comparison, the Ceres2030 annual hunger gap is about **1.1% of 2025 military spending**, and the donor share about **0.5%** (this study's own calculation).

*Assessment.* There is no experimental evidence on redirection itself. The arithmetic shows that the money for ending hunger is small relative to military budgets.

## Ethical tensions

1. **Farmers' livelihoods versus hungry people elsewhere.** Repurposing support can hurt farmers who depend on it, including poor ones. Both the FAO-led and World Bank reports insist on compensating them and phasing changes in (FAO, UNDP & UNEP, 2021; Gautam et al., 2022).
2. **Speed versus accuracy.** Anticipatory money saves more when it is early, but forecasts and parametric models can be wrong (Pople et al., 2026; Johnson, 2021). Is it worse to pay when no crisis comes, or to fail to pay when one does?
3. **Whose money is it?** Remittances and zakat belong to families and faith communities. Steering them risks turning private generosity into public obligation, and the Mexican evidence warns that remittances should not replace state duty (Mora-Rivera & van Gameren, 2021).
4. **Private gains, public risks.** Blended finance and swaps involve investors and banks whose returns can exceed the public benefit (Attridge & Engen, 2019; Chamon et al., 2022).
5. **Conditions and sovereignty.** IMF-linked and results-based funds attach conditions. They can improve accountability but also narrow countries' choices (IMF, 2025b).
6. **Who decides?** Donor-designed pools give recipients little voice. Models like the Start Network (run by its members), ARC (owned by African states), the School Meals Coalition and the Global Alliance give them more.

## Original proposals

1. **A food-security share in farm-support reform (this study's own proposal).** When a country repurposes coupled farm support, it would pledge a fixed share, for example 5%, to food-security public goods, such as research on nutritious crops, rural roads and storage, at home or through IFAD and GAFSP. Five per cent of US$540 billion is US$27 billion a year (this study's own calculation), close to the Ceres2030 annual gap. The share should come with compensation for small farmers, as both reports recommend.
2. **A floor under pooled emergency funds (this study's own proposal).** Donors would agree a minimum annual funding level for CERF and the WFP Immediate Response Account, indexed to the number of people in acute food insecurity, so that the last-resort funds do not shrink at the moment need peaks.
3. **Trigger-linked cash with a "model audit" clause (this study's own proposal).** Anticipatory contracts would pay through national cash systems when forecasts cross agreed thresholds. They would include independent annual checks of the risk model against field data, and a pre-agreed, transparent route to pay when a trigger fails, so payouts like Malawi's 2016 one are not left to discretion.
4. **A remittance-cost pledge, not a remittance tax (this study's own proposal).** G20 and host countries would commit to reaching the 3% cost target, through digital channels, competition and fair compliance rules. They would publish annual progress, leaving the savings with families.
5. **Voluntary, matched diaspora food funds (this study's own proposal).** Diaspora and faith organisations could choose to give to national school-meal or nutrition programmes, with governments or development banks matching each dollar. Each fund would follow published governance rules, such as audited accounts, and Islamic funds would also meet zakat compliance, following the transparency standards already used for waqf. These would be voluntary and never a deduction from remittances.
6. **Debt-for-school-meals windows (this study's own proposal).** Bilateral creditors would offer swaps tied to existing national school-meal or safety-net programmes that are independently audited, rather than new projects. Swaps would be used alongside, not instead of, wider debt relief.
7. **A hunger window in SDR rechanneling (this study's own proposal).** Countries rechanneling SDRs would earmark part of the money for food-system resilience in low-income countries, with lighter conditions where a country already has a credible national food-security plan under the Global Alliance.
8. **A one-percent peace dividend for food (this study's own proposal).** States would pledge to move 1% of military spending into a multilateral hunger fund governed jointly by contributing and recipient countries. On 2025 spending that is about **US$29 billion a year** (this study's own calculation), close to the Ceres2030 gap.
9. **Regional reserves plus open trade (this study's own proposal).** Other regions, such as the African Union and the Gulf, could build APTERR-style earmarked reserves. Each would be paired with a commitment not to restrict food exports to humanitarian buyers, keeping physical stocks small and relying on trade.

## Risks

- **Overpromising.** Cost estimates differ by orders of magnitude (FAO et al., 2024). Picking the lowest figure can make the problem look cheaper than it is.
- **Displacement.** Money in a new channel may just replace existing aid rather than add to it.
- **Model failure and basis risk** in anticipatory and parametric finance (Johnson, 2021; ARC, 2024).
- **Capture.** Blended finance and swaps can mainly benefit financial intermediaries (Attridge & Engen, 2019; Chamon et al., 2022).
- **Inflation in thin markets** if cash is scaled where markets cannot respond (Egger et al., 2022; Gentilini, 2016).
- **Politicisation** of faith-based or diaspora money, or of food reserves, in tense relationships.
- **Fragmentation.** Many small, separate mechanisms add transaction costs. Coordinating platforms such as the Global Alliance help only if countries actually use them.

## Recommendation

On the evidence reviewed, money for ending hunger should be routed **first through national, shock-responsive cash and social-protection systems, and school meals**. Those have the strongest cost-effectiveness evidence and the deepest national ownership. **Pooled funds** with a guaranteed floor should back them up, along with **anticipatory triggers** whose models are independently audited.

The biggest new sources are **repurposed farm support** and a small share of **military spending**. Either could close the Ceres2030 gap on its own (this study's own calculation). Both should be pursued gradually, with compensation for small farmers and workers.

Remittances and faith-based giving should be supported, not taxed. That means making remittances cheaper to send, and creating voluntary, matched, well-governed funds. Debt swaps, SDR rechanneling, solidarity levies, blended finance and results-based finance are useful additions in specific settings, but none should be treated as the main answer.

The test for every mechanism is the same: **does the money reach people who cannot afford food, quickly, at low cost, and do the countries and communities affected help decide how it is used?**
