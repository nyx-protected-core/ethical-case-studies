# Reforming sponsorship-based labour migration (kafala): what changed on paper, what changed in practice

*Case study prepared 18 November 2026. Every DOI below was checked against Crossref and every link was opened on that date. Figures and quotations come only from the sources listed. Peer-reviewed work is marked **[PR]**. Material from rights organisations is labelled **advocacy**: it is useful evidence but is produced to campaign, and its samples are not designed to be representative. Government pages are labelled as such and describe what a government says about its own system.*

---

## Framing: a cooperation problem, not an oversight problem

"Kafala" is the shorthand for systems in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states, Jordan and Lebanon. Under these systems a migrant worker's legal residence, and often the right to change jobs or leave the country, depends on one sponsoring employer. Most of the reforms since 2018 did not come from outside pressure alone. They were made by destination states, mostly with ILO technical cooperation that those states requested and in Qatar's case paid for. Origin countries pushed alongside them through their own laws, deployment rules and bilateral agreements, and regional forums of both sending and receiving states did the same. This case study therefore asks three things. What did each party commit to? What do the available data show about delivery? Where do the joint mechanisms still fail? It treats the GCC's own innovations, such as electronic wage protection systems, as policy tools to assess on their merits.

Two points of method:

- **Qatar has the most evidence.** The ILO Project Office in Doha publishes annual progress reports with administrative figures. The other states mostly have legal texts and short ILO country factsheets. Different amounts of evidence are not the same as different amounts of reform.
- **Administrative data measure use of a system, not outcomes for workers.** A job-change approval or a company blocked by a wage system shows the system is working. It does not show that the worker was paid in full or moved freely. Where only a rights organisation has measured an outcome, that is said in the text.

## 1. Qatar's 2020 reforms and the ILO's assessment

### 1.1 What the laws did

| Measure | What it does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Law No. 13 of 2018 and Ministerial Decision No. 95 of 2019 | Workers may leave the country temporarily or permanently without the employer's prior approval. Employers may keep an exit-permit requirement for up to five per cent of their workforce in certain senior posts. | ILO progress reports 2021–2024 |
| Ministerial Decision in force 16 January 2020 | Extended the end of exit permits to groups outside the 2018 law: "domestic workers; workers in government and public institutions; workers employed in the oil and gas sector and workers employed at sea and in agriculture and grazing" | ILO news, 20 Aug 2020 |
| Law No. 19 of 2020 (adopted 30 Aug 2020) with Ministerial Decision No. 51 of 2020 | Workers can change jobs before the contract ends without a No Objection Certificate (NOC) from the employer | ILO news, 30 Aug 2020; ILO progress report 2021 |
| Law No. 18 of 2020 | Amends the Labour Law's rules on ending a contract, including notice periods. A worker changing jobs during probation may owe the former employer compensation, and the ILO country factsheet says the *new* employer pays it, capped at two months' basic wage | ILO progress report 2021; ILO Qatar factsheet 2023 |
| Law No. 17 of 2020 | A non-discriminatory minimum wage of QAR 1,000 a month that covers domestic workers (the law's title names "Workers and Domestic Workers"), plus food and housing or allowances of at least QAR 500 for housing and QAR 300 for food. It set up a Minimum Wage Commission to review the level. | ILO news, 30 Aug 2020; ILO progress reports 2021 and 2024 |

Two corrections to the usual summary:

- **Exit permits ended before Laws 18 and 19.** They went in two steps, in 2018 and January 2020, and residual exceptions remain.
- **Law 18 did not abolish the NOC; Law 19 did.** Law 18 changed how contracts end.

When the 2020 package was adopted, the ILO said the removal of the NOC, "coupled with the removal of exit permit requirements earlier in the year, effectively dismantles the "kafala" sponsorship system". It expected the minimum wage to "directly affect around 400,000 workers in the private sector".

### 1.2 What the ILO's progress reports measured

**Job mobility (employment change applications to the Ministry of Labour):**

| Period | Applications | Approved | Rejected | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nov 2020 – Aug 2022 | 532,290 | 348,455 | 183,835 | About 16% of approvals were from women (women are 15% of the workforce); about 5.6% were from domestic workers |
| Sept 2020 – Oct 2023 (cumulative) | 1,037,644 | 669,198 | 364,053 | The Ministry has 12 grounds for rejection, "above all when new employers are blocked from recruitment due to non-compliance with the laws on wage protection" |
| Jan – Oct 2023 | – | 167,048 men, 25,295 women | – | 10,827 domestic-worker approvals as of Sept 2023 (cumulative) |
| Jan – Sept 2024 | – | 50,127 men, 10,124 women | – | 3,399 domestic-worker approvals in 2024, 60% from men; construction is about one-third of all changes |

- **Speed of processing.** The redesigned electronic platform launched in May 2024 removed a paper form whose errors had been a main reason for rejection. Average processing took 22 days in August 2021, 6 days in September 2023 and 9 days in September 2024.
- **Scale of the change.** In a study with the Ministry and Strategy Hub, the ILO found approvals rose "by around 830 per cent" in the first year after the reforms compared with the year before.
- **The drop in 2024.** The 2024 total is about 60,000 against about 192,000 for the first ten months of 2023. The report does not explain this, and this case study does not guess at a reason.

**Minimum wage.** Between February and September 2021, "over 280,000 workers or approximately 13 per cent of the total workforce in the private sector" saw their basic wage rise to the new floor (ILO 2021). This is the clearest measured income effect of the package. It is smaller than the 400,000 the ILO expected in 2020, but the 2021 figure covers only the first seven months.

**Exit permits in practice.** For the senior-staff exception, the Ministry approved 32,408 exit permits between November 2018 and September 2021. There were 13,282 requests from January to October 2023 and 4,759 in 2024 (ILO 2021, 2023, 2024).

**Arrears and the Workers' Support and Insurance Fund (WSIF).**

- The WSIF paid QAR 14 million to 5,500 workers in 2020. By August 2023 it had paid out about QAR 2.3 billion. "Around half of this amount has been disbursed from the Fund to more than 70,000 workers"; the other half passed through the Fund from companies to their workers (ILO 2023).
- The Fund pays workers when employers default. Its size therefore shows both protection and the scale of non-payment.

**Labour complaints** (ILO 2024, Table 2):

| | 2022 | 2023 | Jan–Sept 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complaints | 31,549 | 28,808 | 22,158 |
| Settled in conciliation | 80% | 75.2% | 72.8% |
| Sent to Dispute Settlement Committees | 19.5% | 24.8% | 25% |

The 2024 report says "Unpaid wages and benefits continue to be the primary cause for complaints". It also says the growing share sent to the committees means "a long period for workers to wait to have their cases heard".

### 1.3 The ILO's overall assessment

The 2024 progress report is the most candid official summary:

> "there is broad acceptance of the legislation on the minimum wage, on occupational heat stress, and on the removal of exit permits. While there is increased recognition of the value of labour mobility ... it is far from universal, and there is still resistance to it from many employers."

It names two open problems: whether domestic workers can in practice change jobs or leave without their employer's permission, and "the high costs that employers incur when recruiting domestic workers".

**Employer survey (ILO, Ministry and Strategy Hub).**

- A "significant majority of employers asked for NOCs or equivalent documents when recruiting workers locally". They gave two reasons: to avoid delays in the transfer and to avoid conflict with the former employer.
- Small and medium enterprises reported a 20 per cent fall in cross-border hiring between 2019 and 2023 (ILO 2024).

The NOC was abolished in law but is partly kept alive by employers' own risk management.

**The independent final evaluation of Phase 2** (completed January 2024) confirms this from interviews:

- "some employers also still resist the reforms. This translates into NOCs requested to workers changing jobs despite having been removed from legal requirements".
- Retaliation can take the form of "threats of deportation, cancelling residency permits or filing absconding charges".

The government's response was administrative:

- The Ministry of Labour and Ministry of Interior systems were linked. Once a worker files a job-change request or has a pending complaint, the employer cannot cancel the worker's residence permit (QID) or file an absconding case.
- Following discussions in 2022, an employer reporting a worker as absconding must give the worker's salary, any money owed, any complaint, the accommodation address and witnesses. A penalty applies for inaccurate reports (ILO 2023).

**Continuity and funding.**

- ILO technical cooperation moved from Phase 2 (2021–2024) to Phase 3 (2024–2028) under a Joint Work Programme signed in March 2024.
- The ILO's February 2026 brief puts total Qatari funding for the partnership since 2018 at USD 39.9 million. It describes the 2024–2028 Joint Work Programme as funded through a USD 19 million Direct Trust Fund.

This is cooperation in its plainest form: the host state pays the multilateral body to advise on and monitor its own reforms. That raises an independence question, taken up in section 9.

### 1.4 What rights organisations found (advocacy)

- **Amnesty International (2022, advocacy)** accepts that the reforms "improved the living and working conditions for hundreds of thousands of Qatar's migrant workers". It also reports:
  - domestic workers "typically working between 14 and 18 hours a day without a weekly day off";
  - "The payment of recruitment fees also remains a near-universal experience, while tens of thousands of workers continue to suffer delayed and unpaid wages";
  - employer-led joint committees "cover only 2% of the workforce".
- **Human Rights Watch (2020, advocacy)** found wage abuses "persistent and widespread across at least 60 employers and companies in Qatar".

Neither sample is representative. Both match the ILO's own finding that unpaid wages are the main cause of complaints.

## 2. Saudi Arabia: the 2021 Labour Reform Initiative and domestic workers

### 2.1 The Labour Reform Initiative (LRI)

The Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development (HRSD) announced the LRI on 4 November 2020, and it took effect on 14 March 2021. It has three services, run through the Absher and Qiwa platforms:

- **Job mobility.** "Employee mobility will allow expatriate workers to transfer between employers upon the expiry of the binding work contract without the employer's consent." The ministry's guidebook adds conditions for moving *during* a contract: one year with the current employer, a documented contract and an offer through Qiwa. It also allows transfer without conditions in listed cases, for example:
  - no contract registered within three months;
  - wages unpaid for three consecutive months;
  - an absent employer;
  - an expired work permit;
  - a worker reporting a commercial cover-up;
  - an employer who misses two court hearings.
- **Exit and re-entry.** Workers can apply themselves; the employer is notified electronically.
- **Final exit.** Workers can leave after the contract ends, or before it, "with the worker bearing all consequences (financial or otherwise) relating to breaking the employment contract".

**What it excludes.** The ministry says the LRI "applies to all expatriate workers in the private sector". It sits on the Labour Law, and Article 7 of that law exempts, among others, "Domestic workers and their equivalents" and "Private agriculture workers, herders and their equivalents". The ILO's 2023 Saudi factsheet confirms the result. In the private sector, confiscating a passport carries a fine of 5,000 riyals and workers covered by the Labour Law need no exit permit. Under the domestic-worker rules then in force, an exit permit was still required, and passport confiscation was "not explicitly" prohibited.

### 2.2 Domestic-worker rules since 2022

Two HRSD instruments, both published in English on the ministry's site, have since narrowed the gap:

- **Controls to Improve the Contractual Relationship of Domestic Workers** (ministerial decision No. 212875 of 29/11/1443 AH, mid-2022). These allow a domestic worker to transfer without the current employer's approval when, among other grounds:
  - wages go unpaid for three months, consecutive or not;
  - the employer does not pick the worker up from the recruitment office or shelter within 15 days of being notified of her arrival;
  - the employer fails to issue or renew the residence permit within 30 days;
  - the employer rents the worker out to others;
  - the worker is given dangerous tasks or is ill-treated.

  The same controls limit "absence" (absconding) reports: a report needs a verified contract on the Musaned platform and no pending claim by the worker. It can be withdrawn within 15 days, except where it was filed in the first 90 days.
- **Regulation for Domestic Workers and Those in Similar Positions.** The ILO factsheet identifies it as Ministerial Decision No. 40676/1445, taking effect on 21 September 2024. It sets:
  - at most ten actual working hours a day;
  - at least eight hours' continuous daily rest;
  - a paid weekly rest day of 24 consecutive hours;
  - recruitment costs paid by the employer;
  - no withholding of the worker's passport;
  - an end-of-service award of one month's wage for every four years of service.

The text and the in-force date are verified. That the regulation was approved in 2023 comes from a secondary legal database, not from the ministry, so the year is not confirmed here. No source found measures how far these rules are applied inside households.

**Wage payment.** According to the ILO–GCC wage protection report (2025), payment through the WPS became mandatory for domestic workers on new contracts. All existing domestic-worker contracts were to be brought in "by the beginning of January 2026". That would make Saudi Arabia the first GCC state to cover all domestic workers on a mandatory basis. Whether the January 2026 phase was completed was not verified from an official page that could be opened.

## 3. Other destination states

### 3.1 United Arab Emirates

- **Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021 (private-sector labour relations).**
  - It excludes domestic workers from its scope.
  - It says "The employer is prohibited from charging the worker for the fees and costs of recruitment and employment or collecting them from him, whether directly or indirectly".
  - It requires the employer to refrain from "withholding the official documents of the worker".
  - It moves all contracts to fixed terms "not exceeding (3) three years".
- **Federal Decree-Law No. 9 of 2022 on domestic workers** (amended by Federal Decree-Law No. 21 of 2023). The government portal lists these entitlements:
  - wages paid within 10 days of the due date;
  - one paid rest day a week;
  - 12 hours' daily rest, eight of them consecutive;
  - at least 30 days' paid annual leave;
  - a return ticket every two years;
  - the right to keep identity documents.

  The law covers 19 listed occupations and bars recruitment offices from taking commission from workers.
- **Tadbeer.** The Foreign Ministry's labour-rights page (a government page) says the labour ministry has set up "39 service centres across the country" called Tadbeer, which "offer training for domestic workers to know their rights and responsibilities, and provide them with copies of their employment contracts". The same page says "More than 3.2 million workers are paid through the Wage Protection System". These are government statements; no independent count was found.
- **Evidence on effects.** The UAE is the source of the strongest causal evidence on labour mobility (Naidu, Nyarko & Wang 2016; see section 8). That study analyses an earlier reform that relaxed restrictions on changing employers, not the 2021 law.

### 3.2 Bahrain: the flexi-permit and its replacement

- **Timing.** Bahrain's flexi-permit, which let workers hold their own permit rather than be tied to one employer, was ended in **2022, not 2019**. The labour regulator (LMRA) announced on 4 December 2022 that accredited centres would register workers under a "Labour Registration Program" replacing "Flexi Permits". It later gave irregular workers and flexi-permit holders until 4 March 2023 to regularise.
- **Costs.** Migrant-Rights.org (now MRRORS; advocacy) reports that the flexi-permit was "cancelled at the end of October" 2022 and that, "Similar to the Flexi-Permit, the permit cost is entirely borne by the worker". It gives BD527 (about US$1,398) for one year and BD879 (about US$2,331) for two.
- **ILO view.** The ILO's 2023 Bahrain factsheet gives similar fees (about US$1,400 and US$2,330). It says those with criminal or absconding records are not eligible. It also notes that recruitment fees are not explicitly prohibited.
- **Absconding.** Absconding reports remain. A private-sector worker has 30 days to contest one; a domestic worker has one week (ILO factsheet).
- **Lesson.** Bahrain shows a real self-sponsorship model, but one that moved the cost and the legal risk onto the worker.

### 3.3 Kuwait

- The ILO's 2023 factsheet records that changing employers generally still needs the employer's permission, with sector rules.
- The Public Authority of Manpower can transfer domestic workers without consent in specific cases, such as the employer's death or abuse.
- Passport confiscation is prohibited, and at the time "No exit permit is required".
- Domestic work is governed by Law No. 68 of 2015.
- The ILO–GCC wage report notes that Kuwaiti law does not require domestic workers to be paid into bank accounts. That limits any wage-protection coverage.
- Press reports describe an employer-approved exit permit for private-sector workers from July 2025. This was not verified against an official text and is not relied on here.

### 3.4 Oman: removing the NOC in 2021

- **The 2021 change.** Royal Oman Police Decision No. 157/2020 (published in the Official Gazette in June 2020, in force from 1 January 2021) rewrote the residence rule. A worker's residence can now move to another licensed employer once the contract has ended, been rescinded or been terminated, with the competent authority's approval.
- **What it covers.** This is NOC removal at the end of a contract, not free movement during one.
- **Later steps.** The 2023 Labour Law (Royal Decree 53/2023) followed. When the ILO wrote its 2023 factsheet, the ministerial decision on transfer conditions was "not yet announced".
- **Passports and wages.** Passports may be held only with the worker's written permission. Oman's WPS dates from 2014, and its latest version from July 2023. The ILO–GCC report found that in Bahrain and Oman "enforcement for non-compliance is yet to be fully implemented".

### 3.5 Lebanon and Jordan: the domestic-worker gap

- **Lebanon.**
  - Article 7 of the 1946 Labour Code excludes domestic workers, so their status rests on a standard contract and General Security rules.
  - On 8 September 2020 the labour ministry issued a revised Standard Unified Contract. It allowed a worker to end the contract on one month's notice and prohibited withholding wages and passports. The ILO called it "a crucial first step" towards dismantling kafala.
  - The ILO's 2023 factsheet records that it "was later suspended by the State Shura Council ... and thus is not currently in force".
  - Domestic workers still change employer only with approval. In cases of abuse or three months' non-payment, the authorities decide.
  - Pande (2013) argues from oral histories that the system "systematically produces a new population of readily exploitable worker – the category of "illegal workers."" She adds that blaming individual abusive employers "privatizes the structural problem".
- **Jordan.**
  - Domestic workers have been covered since 2008–2009: the Labour Code was amended to include them, and Regulation No. 90 of 2009 followed.
  - The residence law, Law No. 24 of 1973, keeps a sponsorship logic. The ILO's 2017 baseline study puts it this way: a worker "cannot change employment nor leave the country without first obtaining explicit written permission from the employer", with some exceptions.
  - Jordan is the clearest case of labour-law inclusion without residence-law reform.

### 3.6 Summary across destination states

| State | Change jobs without employer consent? | Exit permit for most private-sector workers? | Domestic workers under main labour law? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qatar | Yes, anytime with notice (Law 19/2020) | No (5% senior exception) | No (but the minimum wage applies) |
| Saudi Arabia | At contract end, or mid-contract under conditions (LRI 2021) | No (Labour Law workers) | No (separate regulation; transfer in listed cases) |
| UAE | Not assessed here | No (employer permission not needed, per government page) | No (Decree-Law 9/2022) |

| Lebanon | No (domestic workers) | No | No |
| Jordan | Limited | Permission required under the residence law (ILO 2017) | Yes (since 2008) |

## 4. Implementation gaps

### 4.1 Wage theft

The strongest official evidence is Qatar's: unpaid wages and benefits are "the primary cause" of labour complaints, which numbered 31,549 in 2022, 28,808 in 2023 and 22,158 in the first nine months of 2024 (ILO 2024), and the WSIF has paid out about QAR 2.3 billion (ILO 2023). HRW (2020, advocacy) documented one mechanism in detail: "pay when paid" clauses, which require "the subcontractor to delay payments to workers and leaves migrant workers vulnerable to payment delays in supply chain hierarchies". HRW also found the recruitment fees workers had paid ranged from $693 to $2,613. Debts of that size make workers reluctant to walk away from unpaid jobs.

### 4.2 Absconding charges

Absconding reports turn a labour dispute into an immigration offence, and every system in this study still has them in some form. The improvements are procedural:

- **Qatar:** linked ministry systems, more data required for a report, and a penalty for false reports.
- **Saudi Arabia:** domestic-worker absence reports need a verified contract and no pending claim.
- **Bahrain:** a contest period, shorter for domestic workers.

Amnesty (2022, advocacy) still lists the absconding charge as a barrier to be removed. Jordan requires employers to notify the authorities in writing within 48 hours when a domestic worker leaves (ILO factsheet).

### 4.3 Passport confiscation

The ILO factsheets and national texts record bans on passport confiscation, at least for private-sector workers, in these states:

- **Qatar:** penalties up to QAR 25,000, and employers may hold passports only with written consent and secure storage.
- **Saudi Arabia:** a fine of 5,000 riyals, plus the 2024 domestic-worker regulation.
- **UAE:** the 2021 decree-law.
- **Kuwait** and **Oman:** prohibited, though Oman allows holding with written permission.

No official prevalence survey of confiscation was found for any GCC state, so how far the bans are obeyed is not measured here. Rights groups' reports of continuing confiscation are not quantified on a representative basis.

### 4.4 Heat stress

- **The case for heat-related deaths.**
  - Pradhan et al. (2019) used Nepal's records of migrant deaths and Doha weather data for 2009–2017. They report an average death rate of 150 per 100,000 among Nepali workers in Qatar.
  - Cardiovascular causes were 22% of deaths in the cool season and 58% in the hot season, against roughly 15% globally for the 25–35 age group.
  - They estimate that "As many as 200 of the 571 CVD deaths during 2009-2017 could have been prevented if effective heat protection had been implemented".
  - They ask that death records "should be more precise than "cardiac arrest.""
- **The ILO's 2021 data and its limits.**
  - The ILO's 2021 study, *One is too many*, counted 50 fatal, 506 severe and 37,601 mild-to-moderate work injuries in 2020 from the sources feeding its national injury registry (WURQ). After cross-checking, the two ministries reached a final count of 66 fatal occupational injuries.
  - Public statistics for 2016–2018 showed "no spikes in the number of fatalities of non-Qatari men" in the hottest months. The ILO notes that those data cover all deaths and only split Qatari from non-Qatari.
  - It also notes that the Cardiology paper found the summer rise "far less so from 2015 to 2017".

  The two sources are not in conflict. Both point to poor cause-of-death data as the binding constraint.
- **Policy response.**
  - Qatar's May 2021 decision bans outdoor work from 10 a.m. to 3:30 p.m. between 1 June and 15 September. It requires all outdoor work to stop above 32.1° WBGT at any time.
  - The 2024 inspection campaign shut 368 worksites for non-compliance (ILO 2021, 2024).

  A threshold-based rule is a real advance on calendar bans. Whether it is enforced shows up in shutdown counts, not in health outcomes, which are not yet measured.

## 5. Wage protection systems (WPS)

**How they work.** In the ILO–GCC report's words, "WPSs are electronic salary transfer systems that are designed to give national authorities oversight of wages paid by employers to workers in the private sector".

- They are "an innovation developed in the GCC States".
- The first was the UAE's, in 2009. Oman followed in 2014 and Qatar in 2015; Bahrain made its system compulsory in 2022.
- Employers submit salary information files (SIFs) for each pay period. The ministry checks them and blocks non-compliant employers' access to ministry services, such as new work permits.

**What they achieve.**

- **Scale.** In Qatar, 102,786 companies filed salary files in September 2024, up from 69,806 registered in August 2023.
- **Enforcement.** Qatar's blocking figures (ILO 2024, Table 1):

| Year | Companies blocked | Violation notices |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 48,328 | 2,318 |
| 2020 | 25,200 | 3,360 |
| 2021 | 27,441 | 3,102 |
| 2022 | 28,266 | 7,769 |
| 2023 | 19,445 | 4,595 |
| 2024 (to 30 Sept) | 18,802 | 5,667 |

- **Link to job mobility.** Blocked employers are the leading reason job-change applications are refused. That protects workers from moving to a non-paying firm, but it also limits where they can move.

**Shortfalls documented by the ILO–GCC report (2025).**

- **Domestic workers.** Coverage "excludes most domestic workers". The exceptions are Saudi Arabia's extension to all domestic-worker employers and five "higher paid" categories in the UAE, mandatory from April 2023 under Ministerial Resolution No. 675 of 2022.
- **Free zones.** In the UAE, low take-up by free zones is estimated, from an interview with a payroll company, to leave about one million workers outside coverage.
- **Small firms.** In Bahrain, 100% of large firms but only 25% of small firms (1–49 employees) were registered.
- **Underpayment.** Qatar's system "cannot yet detect whether the wage paid according to the salary information file is less than specified by the employment contract" (ILO 2024).
- **Subcontractors.** Penalties "generally only apply to workers' direct employers". The report recommends banning "pay when paid" arrangements in construction so that clients and contractors are jointly liable.
- **Worker voice and transparency.** Only Saudi Arabia lets workers dispute employers' explanations for missing payments, and itemised payslips "are not yet mandated in the GCC".
- **Effect on outcomes.** "there is limited evidence that the current design and operation of WPSs actually reduces workers' need to submit complaints to other judicial or administrative mechanisms".

HRW (2020, advocacy) put it more sharply: Qatar's system is "a wage monitoring system with significant gaps in its oversight capacity". The ILO–GCC report's diagnosis is close to that, but it treats the WPS as a platform to extend rather than a failure.

## 6. Origin countries

### 6.1 Philippines

- **Department of Migrant Workers (DMW).** Republic Act No. 11641, approved 30 December 2021, created the DMW and merged the Philippine Overseas Employment Administration (POEA) and other agencies into it. The Secretary may "terminate, suspend, or impose a total ban on the deployment of migrant workers" to a country on protection grounds.
- **The household-worker standard contract.** The POEA's 2006 Governing Board resolutions include "Increasing the entry level minimum salary of all household workers deployed overseas to US$400.00". They also bar placement fees for household workers.
- **Deployment bans as leverage: Kuwait 2018.**
  - After reports of abuse and deaths of Filipino domestic workers, the Governing Board resolved on 14 February 2018 to stop deployment to Kuwait.
  - Deployment resumed under a memorandum circular of 13 June 2018. This followed a bilateral Agreement on the Employment of Domestic Workers signed on 11 May 2018.
  - The resumption rules say employers may not keep workers' passports, workers may keep their mobile phones, and unauthorised fees are barred. Foreign placement agencies must post an escrow deposit of US$10,000.

  This is the clearest case in this study of an origin-country ban producing a written bilateral commitment. Whether the commitments were then honoured was not measured in any source found.

### 6.2 Nepal: "free-visa, free-ticket"

- **The rule.** Nepal's 2015 "free-visa, free-ticket" policy requires employers in seven destinations to pay visa and air-ticket costs: Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, the UAE, Qatar, Oman, Bahrain and Malaysia. Agencies may charge workers at most NPR 10,000 where the employer does not pay service fees (ILO country profile 2021).
- **Evasion.** The ILO profile records "the failure to maintain receipts and providing the forged receipts with the authorized charge of NPR 10,000".
- **Costs found by the national survey.** Nepal's first national recruitment-cost survey (2023 data, published by the ILO and Nepal's statistics office in 2026) found:
  - workers needed "3.3 months of earnings to recover their recruitment costs" on average;
  - women needed around four and a half months;
  - unregistered brokers were the most expensive.
- **Verdict.** A cap that is not enforced through receipts and recruiter liability leaves costs where they were.

### 6.3 Indonesia: the 2015 moratorium and Law 18/2017

- **The moratorium.** Ministerial Decision No. 260 of 2015 (dated 26 May 2015, listed as in force on the ministry's legal database) stops and bans the placement of Indonesian workers with *individual* employers, meaning households, in Middle Eastern countries. The number of countries it covers is not confirmed here from the decree's text.
- **Law No. 18 of 2017 on the Protection of Indonesian Migrant Workers** (passed 22 November 2017). It provides that "Pekerja Migran Indonesia tidak dapat dibebani biaya penempatan" (migrant workers may not be charged placement costs; Art. 30). It allows placement only to countries with protective laws or agreements (Art. 31).
- **The trade-off.** A ban on household placement protects some workers. It can also push others into irregular channels, a trade-off no source found here measures for this ban.

### 6.4 Kenya: agreements with Saudi Arabia and Qatar

- **Published texts.** Kenya's National Employment Authority publishes its bilateral labour agreements (uploaded November 2021).
  - The Saudi document is the agreed minutes of negotiations in Riyadh on 1–2 December 2015 on an agreement on recruiting domestic workers. It attaches an agreed draft. The final signing date was not confirmed from an official source.
  - The Qatar document is an agreement on regulating the employment of Kenyan workers in Qatar, received and stamped in September 2013.
- **Review under way.** In August 2026, Kenya's foreign ministry reported that both governments "agreed to expedite discussions on the review of the 2012 Bilateral Labour Agreement". It said more than 70,000 Kenyans live and work in Qatar.

A ten-year-old text under review is typical of the region's agreements. They are signed, rarely published with monitoring data, and revised slowly.

## 7. Cooperation mechanisms

| Mechanism | Members | What it produced (verified) |
|---|---|---|
| ILO–GCC Plan of Joint Activities 2025–2029 (signed Nov 2024) | ILO; GCC labour ministers' council | Joint research and workshops on resolving labour disputes, minimum wage systems, occupational safety and health, prevention of forced labour and other areas. Separately, the ILO and the GCC Executive Bureau produced the 2025 regional wage-protection report. |

**What cooperation achieved:**

- **Technical design.** WPS, electronic job-change platforms and threshold-based heat rules were built by destination ministries with ILO support.
- **Joint information and orientation programmes** between origin and destination states (CIOP).
- **Origin-country leverage** turned into written terms, as with the Philippines and Kuwait.

**What it has not yet produced:**

- published monitoring of bilateral agreements;
- shared enforcement against recruiters who operate in both countries;
- a common position on domestic workers.

The Abu Dhabi Dialogue's own website could not be opened on 18 November 2026, so its recent outputs are not assessed.

## 8. Counterarguments, and what has measurably improved

### 8.1 Fair counterarguments

1. **State capacity.** Mobility rights need fast processing, labour courts and inspectors. The data show capacity binding in practice:
   - Qatar's dispute committees received a quarter of complaints in 2024, with long waits;
   - Bahrain and Oman had WPS frameworks without full enforcement;
   - Bahrain had 25% registration among small firms.

   Phasing reforms to match capacity is a reasonable choice, not just foot-dragging.
2. **Employer costs.** Recruiting from abroad has costs, which destination laws increasingly place on employers (UAE 2021, Saudi Arabia 2024, Indonesia 2017). An employer who pays to recruit a worker and then loses them early bears a real loss. Qatar handles this through probation compensation paid by the new employer, capped at two months' basic wage. The ILO itself names domestic employers' "high costs" as a live issue. The employer survey shows small firms cut cross-border hiring by 20% between 2019 and 2023.
3. **Effects on future migrants.** Naidu, Nyarko & Wang (2016) studied the UAE reform that eased employer changes. They found it "increased incumbent migrants' earnings and firm retention", driven by "a fall in country exits". But it "decreased demand for new migrants and lowered their earnings". Mobility helps the workers already present, and some cost may fall on those not yet recruited.
4. **Sovereignty and political economy.** Immigration and residence law is a core state power, and Gulf citizens are a minority in their own labour markets. Shah (2013) put non-nationals at about 47% of the GCC's combined population and found that sending and receiving states' policies "are at odds with each other". Diop, Johnston & Le (2015) found in a representative Qatari survey that "When primed to consider blue-collar workers, citizens strongly support the status quo and oppose reform". The strongest support for the status quo came from "a powerful coalition of economic interests (uniting business owners, workers and the wealthy)". Reform that ignores domestic opinion is unlikely to last. That is an argument for reforms that host societies accept as their own, and the cooperative model fits it.
5. **Security and irregularity.** States argue that tracking residence through a sponsor limits irregular stay. The evidence cuts both ways:
   - Bahrain's replacement of the flexi-permit was explicitly about regularising status.
   - Pande (2013) argues that tying status to one employer *produces* irregular workers when they flee abuse.

   No source found here quantifies the net effect.

### 8.2 What has measurably improved

- **Job mobility at scale.** 669,198 approved job changes in Qatar between September 2020 and October 2023. About 830% more approvals in the first year than in the year before.
- **Wage floors.** More than 280,000 Qatari private-sector workers (about 13%) raised to the minimum wage within seven months.
- **Arrears paid.** About QAR 2.3 billion paid through Qatar's WSIF by August 2023.
- **Wage-system reach.** About 100,000 Qatari companies filing salary data monthly. In Saudi Arabia, wage-system coverage is being extended to all domestic workers, the first such step in the GCC.
- **Exit permits.** Effectively gone for most private-sector workers in Qatar, Saudi Arabia (Labour Law workers), the UAE, Oman and Bahrain.
- **Written rights for domestic workers** where none existed: UAE 2022, Saudi Arabia 2022–2024, Qatar's minimum wage.
- **Causal evidence** from the UAE that freer employer transitions raise incumbent workers' earnings.

## 9. Proposals, limitations and open questions

### 9.1 Proposals, each with its tension

1. **Extend the WPS to all domestic workers, following the Saudi model, and to free zones.** *Tension:* household employers are not firms. Blocking ministry services is a weak sanction against a family, and payment mandates assume bank access the worker may not have (Kuwait has no such requirement).
2. **Check pay against the contract, not just the minimum wage, and issue itemised payslips.** *Tension:* this needs contracts to be digitised and linked, which raises the data-protection issues the ILO–GCC report flags.
3. **Make clients and main contractors jointly liable for wages, and ban "pay when paid".** *Tension:* this raises costs for main contractors and may concentrate the market, a trade-off between employer costs and worker protection.
4. **Replace absconding reports with civil contract claims.** At minimum, freeze any report while a labour complaint or job-change request is pending; Qatar already does this, so extend it everywhere. *Tension:* states see absconding reports as an immigration-control tool, which is the security argument in section 8.1.
5. **Pair origin-country fee caps with recruiter liability across both countries and published receipts,** building on Nepal's survey and the Colombo Process fair-recruitment work. *Tension:* this needs destination-state enforcement against foreign agencies, which bilateral agreements rarely provide.
6. **Publish bilateral agreements and yearly monitoring data** (complaints, repatriations, wage claims by corridor). The Kenya–Qatar review is a natural pilot. *Tension:* publication exposes both governments to criticism, which is why it is rare.
7. **Fund independent evaluation alongside host-funded technical cooperation,** for example from origin states or a pooled trust. *Tension:* the host-funded model is what made Qatar's detailed public reporting possible. A different funding model must not cost that openness.

### 9.2 Limitations

- Evidence is heavily weighted to Qatar. The absence of comparable data elsewhere is a gap in evidence, not a finding.
- Most figures are administrative counts of use, approvals and blocks, not outcomes for workers. Only one causal study, Naidu et al., estimates effects on earnings.
- The ILO country factsheets are short and laid out in columns. They were paraphrased conservatively, and laws may have changed since December 2023.
- Some government sites (the UAE labour ministry and legislation portals, and the Abu Dhabi Dialogue site) could not be opened. Government portal summaries and the ILO were used instead.

### 9.3 Open questions

- Why did approved job changes in Qatar fall from about 192,000 (January–October 2023) to about 60,000 (January–September 2024)? Is it the post-World Cup labour market, more retention, or more informal NOC demands?
- Are the Saudi 2022–2024 domestic-worker rules being used in practice: how many transfers and absence-report withdrawals?
- Did the January 2026 extension of the Saudi WPS to all domestic workers reach the target, and does it reduce wage claims?
- Is there an independent count of passport confiscation or recruitment-fee payment in any GCC state, apart from advocacy surveys?
- What did the Kenya–Saudi and Philippines–Kuwait agreements change for workers in the corridors they cover?
