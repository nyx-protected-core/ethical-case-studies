# Climate, Displacement and Exploitation: A Case Study in Evidence, Law and Responsibility

Date: 2026-11-18

## Purpose and scope

This case study looks at how climate change shapes human displacement and migration, and how that displacement connects to food insecurity and to the risk of trafficking and exploitation. It covers the slow-onset drivers (drought in the Sahel and the Horn of Africa, sea-level rise in Bangladesh and the Pacific). It weighs the claim that climate is a direct cause of migration against the view that it is a "threat multiplier". It sets out the legal gaps, the experience of planned relocation, and the state of loss-and-damage finance. It ends with policy lessons, ethical risks, counterarguments and open questions.

Two points about method. First, observed figures and modelled projections are kept apart, and projections are labelled as such. Second, wherever the evidence linking climate to trafficking is thin or mainly anecdotal, the text says so instead of filling the gap with assumptions.

## 1. The scale of the problem: what is measured, and what is projected

### 1.1 Observed disaster displacement

Global disaster displacement data comes mostly from the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre (IDMC). It counts *movements* (one person can be displaced more than once in a year) as well as the *stock* of people still displaced at year end.

- **2024 was a record year.** IDMC recorded nearly **45.8 million disaster displacements** across 163 countries and territories, the highest since monitoring began in 2008 and well above the 15-year annual average of **24 million** [1]. Storms caused more than half of these movements, and a large share were pre-emptive evacuations ordered after early warnings [1].
- **Long-term displacement is also at a record high.** By the end of 2024, **9.8 million people** were still displaced by disasters. IDMC describes this as a conservative figure, because many events stop being reported once the crisis phase ends [1].
- **The longer-run picture matches.** The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (Working Group II) finds that since 2008 "an annual average of over 20 million people have been internally displaced annually by weather-related extreme events, with storms and floods being the most common" (high confidence). It also finds that "most climate-related displacement and migration occur within national boundaries" (high confidence) [4].

The count of evacuations is not, on its own, a measure of harm. IDMC points out that some displacement was "an effective life-saving measure" [1]. A rising figure can partly reflect better early warning and better reporting, as well as worsening hazards.

### 1.2 Slow-onset drivers: drought in the Horn of Africa and the Sahel

**Horn of Africa.** The 2020–2023 drought shows what slow-onset displacement looks like in the data. In 2022 a third consecutive La Niña year fuelled "the worst drought on record in Somalia, Ethiopia and Kenya, triggering 2.1 million movements" [2]. Somalia alone accounted for **1.1 million** of these, the highest drought-displacement figure recorded globally since such data became available in 2017. Ethiopia recorded around **686,000** drought displacements [2]. Two years later the effects were still visible. The 2024 rains "were insufficient to counterbalance the effects of years of drought", and at the end of 2024 disasters had left nearly **757,000** people displaced in Ethiopia and more than **733,000** in Somalia [1]. The Ethiopian figure was in regions also affected by conflict and violence [2], which makes it hard to attribute the displacement to drought alone.

**Sahel.** Sub-Saharan Africa recorded a record **7.8 million** disaster displacements in 2024, but nearly 85 per cent of these were linked to floods, not drought. The floods were particularly severe in west and central Africa [1]. Chad recorded about **1.33 million** disaster displacements and Niger about **1.17 million** [1]. This is a useful caution. In the drylands most associated with "climate migration", the displacement that is easiest to count often comes from sudden events, while the effects of slow drying on livelihoods show up mainly in labour migration and seasonal mobility, which displacement statistics do not capture well.

### 1.3 Slow-onset drivers: sea-level rise in Bangladesh and the Pacific

**Bangladesh.** In 2024 Bangladesh recorded about **2.4 million** disaster displacements [1]. When cyclone Remal struck, nearly 80 per cent of the roughly 1.1 million movements it triggered were pre-emptive evacuations. At least 150,000 homes were destroyed, and 172,000 people were still displaced at year end [1]. Bangladesh adopted a national strategy on internal displacement in 2021 and an action plan for 2022–2042 [1]. In the World Bank's 2018 Groundswell modelling, Bangladesh is "projected to have a third of the internal climate migrants in South Asia by 2050 under the pessimistic scenario" [5].

**The Pacific.** The IPCC finds that climate and weather extremes "are increasingly driving displacement in all regions (high confidence), with Small Island States disproportionately affected (high confidence)". Over the mid to long term, displacement "will increase with intensification of heavy precipitation and associated flooding, tropical cyclones, drought and, increasingly, sea level rise (high confidence)" [3]. For low-lying atoll states such as Tuvalu and Kiribati, the issue extends beyond displacement to the continuity of the state itself, which is addressed in Section 3.

### 1.4 Projections: Groundswell

The World Bank's Groundswell reports are the most cited projections. They are scenario models of *internal* migration driven by slow-onset impacts (water availability, crop productivity, sea-level rise and storm surge). They are not forecasts, and they do not cover sudden disasters or cross-border movement.

- **Groundswell (2018)** covers Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and Latin America. Under its "pessimistic" reference scenario (high emissions, unequal development), internal climate migration could reach "more than 143 million" by 2050: around 86 million in Sub-Saharan Africa, 40 million in South Asia and 17 million in Latin America [5]. The "more inclusive development" scenario gives 65–105 million, and the "more climate-friendly" scenario gives 31–72 million. Concerted action "could help reduce the number of internal climate migrants by as much as 80 percent by 2050" [5].
- **Groundswell Part 2 (2021)** adds East Asia and the Pacific, North Africa, and Eastern Europe and Central Asia. Across all six regions, "as many as 216 million people could move within their own countries due to slow-onset climate change impacts by 2050", with hotspots "as early as 2030" [6].

The size of the gap between scenarios is the main finding: emissions and development choices change the projected outcome by a factor of several. Groundswell also notes that "others, even more vulnerable, will be unable to move, trapped in increasingly unviable areas" [5].

### 1.5 Food insecurity as a pathway

Food insecurity is one of the most direct routes from climate stress to distress migration. The IPCC finds that "flood and drought-related acute food insecurity and malnutrition have increased in Africa (high confidence) and Central and South America (high confidence)" [3]. The Global Report on Food Crises 2026 found that in 2025, **266 million** people in 47 countries and territories faced high levels of acute food insecurity. Weather extremes were the primary driver in **16** of those countries and territories, which accounted for **87.5 million** people, around one-third of the total [26]. Conflict remained the leading driver. The same report counted **85.1 million** forcibly displaced people in food-crisis countries, from all causes (mainly conflict), 74 per cent of them internally displaced. Where data exist (only 17 countries), acute food insecurity was more severe among displaced people than among residents [26].

## 2. Direct driver or "threat multiplier"? The scholarly debate

### 2.1 The large-number tradition

Public discussion still leans on large estimates. One influential example is a 2002 paper estimating 25 million "environmental refugees" in 1995 and warning that "as many as 200 million people" could eventually be "overtaken by sea-level rise" and other climate impacts once global warming takes hold [7]. Figures of this kind have been repeated widely, often without their caveats.

### 2.2 The sceptical critique

There is a substantial body of scholarship critical of such figures:

- **The numbers do not add up.** A review of published estimates and predictions of people displaced by environmental change examined why these headline figures do not add up, and why they should be used with great caution [8].
- **"Climate migration myths".** Writing in *Nature Climate Change*, a group of migration researchers argued that "misleading claims about mass migration induced by climate change continue to surface". They called for a new research agenda on "climate mobilities" that "moves beyond simplistic assumptions" about the link between human mobility and climate change [9].
- **The evidence is heterogeneous.** A cross-disciplinary review distinguishes fast-onset from slow-onset climatic events, examines "the causes of heterogeneity in migratory responses", and considers how migration interacts with other forms of adaptation. Migration is presented as one response to climatic stress among several, not an automatic outcome [10].
- **The meta-analysis evidence.** A meta-analysis of 30 country-level studies found that most report an effect of environmental hazards on migration, but the effect depends strongly on context. Migration was mostly internal or towards low- and middle-income countries, and income levels and conflict moderated the relationship [11].

### 2.3 What the IPCC concludes

The IPCC's assessment broadly reconciles the two traditions. It holds that climate hazards "act as direct drivers of involuntary migration and displacement and as indirect drivers through deteriorating climate-sensitive livelihoods" (high confidence) [4]. It also finds that over the near term, "migration patterns... will be driven by socioeconomic conditions and governance more than by climate change" (medium confidence) [3]. International movements "occur primarily between countries with contiguous borders" [4].

### 2.4 Trapped populations

The sceptical literature also brings out a less visible risk: people who cannot move at all. The IPCC states that "inability to migrate (i.e., involuntary immobility) in the face of climate hazards is also a potential risk to exposed populations" (medium confidence). It adds that immobility "reflects both vulnerability and lack of agency, but is also a deliberate choice", and that choosing to stay can be "an assertion of the importance of culture, livelihood and sense of place" [4]. A policy aimed only at "stopping climate migrants" can therefore miss the poorest, who may be stuck in place and exposed to harm.

**Assessment.** The best-supported position is that climate change is a real and growing driver of displacement, especially through extreme events, and an important *contributing* driver of migration that works through livelihoods, food and conflict. Most of this movement is internal and short-distance. Single global numbers for "climate refugees" are not reliable, and treating climate migration mainly as a cross-border security threat misreads the evidence.

## 3. The legal gap

### 3.1 Why most climate migrants are not "refugees"

The 1951 Refugee Convention protects people with a well-founded fear of persecution on grounds of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. Environmental harm does not fit these grounds easily. In the leading individual case, the New Zealand tribunal accepted that in many cases environmental degradation and natural disasters will not bring affected people within the Convention, but held that "no hard and fast rules or presumptions of non-applicability exist". The country's Supreme Court likewise did not rule out that such conditions could "create a pathway into the Refugee Convention or other protected person jurisdiction". The claim still failed on its facts [12]. The Nansen Initiative's Protection Agenda summarises the gap: "International law does not explicitly address whether and under which circumstances disaster displaced persons shall be admitted to another country, what rights they have during their stay, and under what conditions they may be returned" [14].

Regional instruments are somewhat broader. In the 2011–2012 Horn of Africa drought, states in the region applied the expanded definition in the 1969 OAU Convention Governing the Specific Aspects of Refugee Problems in Africa (often called the 1969 African Union Refugee Convention) to people fleeing Somalia, on the basis that the famine combined with conflict and the absence of capable authorities "seriously disturb[ed] public order" [14]. The pure climate case remains outside the refugee framework.

### 3.2 Human rights law: the 2019 Human Rights Committee views

In views adopted on 24 October 2019 under the Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (UN Doc. CCPR/C/127/D/2728/2016, distributed 23 September 2020), the UN Human Rights Committee examined a complaint that New Zealand had violated the right to life (article 6) by deporting a person to Kiribati (communication No. 2728/2016) [12]. These are the views of a treaty body under article 5 (4) of the Optional Protocol, not a judgment of a court, and they are not binding in the way a judicial decision is binding. The Committee found no violation. It reasoned that a projected time frame of 10 to 15 years before the islands became uninhabitable "could allow for intervening acts by Kiribati, with the assistance of the international community, to take affirmative measures to protect and, where necessary, relocate its population" [12].

The views are nonetheless significant for the principle they state:

- The Committee held that "without robust national and international efforts, the effects of climate change in receiving States may expose individuals to a violation of their rights under articles 6 or 7 of the Covenant, thereby triggering the non-refoulement obligations of sending States" [12].
- It added that because "the risk of an entire country becoming submerged under water is such an extreme risk, the conditions of life in such a country may become incompatible with the right to life with dignity before the risk is realized" [12].
- It reminded the state of its "continuing responsibility... to take into account in future deportation cases" updated data on climate impacts [12].
- Two Committee members dissented [12].

In short, climate-based protection from return is possible in principle under human rights law, but the threshold is high and depends on the facts and the time frame.

### 3.3 Bilateral innovation: the Australia–Tuvalu Falepili Union

The Falepili Union treaty was signed on 9 November 2023 and entered into force on 28 August 2024 [13]. In it, Australia:

- recognised, "for the first time in a legally binding treaty, Tuvalu's continuing statehood and sovereignty, notwithstanding the impact of climate-change related sea-level rise";
- committed to help Tuvalu in response to a major natural disaster, health pandemic or military aggression;
- created a special permanent-residence visa pathway "to support mobility with dignity" [13].

The pathway is allocated by random ballot for up to **280** places per programme year. Visa holders do not need a job offer and may move freely between the two countries [13]. The treaty also contains security provisions: Tuvalu must agree with Australia any partnership with another state on security and defence matters [13]. Supporters see it as a model of planned, dignified, rights-based mobility. Critics point to the security trade-off as a price for migration access. Both views can be held at once. At 280 places a year it is a modest, orderly channel, not a mass evacuation plan.

### 3.4 Soft law: the Nansen Initiative and the Platform on Disaster Displacement

- **The Nansen Initiative.** Launched in October 2012 by the governments of Switzerland and Norway, the Initiative produced a *Protection Agenda* that was endorsed at a global intergovernmental consultation in Geneva on 12–13 October 2015 [14]. "Rather than calling for a new binding international convention", it compiles effective practices, such as "humanitarian protection measures": the laws, policies and practices states already use to admit disaster-displaced people, or not return them, often on a temporary basis [14].
- **The Platform on Disaster Displacement.** This is a state-led follow-up whose main objective is to implement the Protection Agenda, which the Platform reports was "endorsed by 109 governmental delegations" [15].

The soft-law approach has a clear advantage: it is fast and builds consensus. It also has a clear weakness: it gives displaced people no enforceable entitlement.

## 4. Planned relocation

The IPCC notes that planned relocations "are not presently commonplace, although the need is expected to grow". Examples from coastal Alaska, the Solomon Islands and Fiji "suggest that relocated people can experience significant financial and emotional distress as cultural and spiritual bonds to place and livelihoods are disrupted" (high confidence) [4].

### 4.1 Fiji's Planned Relocation Guidelines (2018)

Fiji has issued national guidance. The 2018 *Planned Relocation Guidelines*, developed under the Ministry of Economy, treat relocation as a state-led process and a measure of last resort. Relocation is to be considered only when other adaptation options have been "exhausted", and "only with the full, free, and informed consent and cooperation of the communities at risk" [16]. A peer-reviewed account of the 2014 relocation of Vunidogoloa village describes how the community moved away from encroaching sea level and repeated inundation, and how the government was beginning to plan similar moves. It stresses careful and inclusive engagement given "the speed at which these national, top-down initiatives are being forged" [17].

### 4.2 Isle de Jean Charles, Louisiana

In January 2016 the US Department of Housing and Urban Development awarded Louisiana **$48.3 million** to resettle residents of Isle de Jean Charles. A peer-reviewed study describes it as "the USA's first federally funded, community-scale, climate-driven resettlement" [18]. The same study identifies one key concession: residents keep ownership of the land they leave, which many residents regarded as non-negotiable [18]. A second, ethnographic study takes a critical view. It argues that the state relied on Tribal planning to win federal funds, then "used those funds to reduce the scope of the resettlement", turning what Tribal leaders saw as "an act of cultural survival" into a "scalable model for managed retreat" [19].

### 4.3 Ethical risks in relocation

- **Consent and coercion.** Even with consent requirements, communities may face a choice between moving and losing services, which is not freely given consent.
- **Who controls the plan.** As the Louisiana case shows, external funding can shift decisions away from the affected community [19].
- **Loss that money does not cover.** Ties to land, graves, fishing grounds and identity are not compensated by a new house [4][18].
- **Precedent.** A model presented as "scalable" may be rolled out faster than its safeguards can be enforced.

## 5. Loss-and-damage finance: pledged versus delivered

### 5.1 Establishment

- **COP27 (Sharm el-Sheikh, November 2022).** In decision 2/CP.27, Parties decided to establish new funding arrangements for loss and damage, "including... a fund for responding to loss and damage", and created a Transitional Committee to set out how they would operate [20].
- **COP28 (Dubai, November–December 2023).** Decision 1/CP.28 approved the Fund's Governing Instrument and invited the World Bank to host it as a financial intermediary fund for an interim period of four years [21]. The decision welcomed offers "amounting to the equivalent of USD 792 million to the funding arrangements, including the contribution of USD 661 million to the Fund" [21].

### 5.3 Reading the numbers

- **Only part of what was pledged has arrived.** About 59 per cent of the pledged amount had been received by July 2026 [22].
- **Demand far exceeds supply.** Requests in the first call were about eight times the USD 342 million allocation [23]. A draft COP31 decision text (version of 17 November 2026, still under negotiation at the time of writing) "notes with concern" both this gap and the fact that "only USD 485.77 million of the USD 822.71 million pledged" had been received [24].
- **Very little has reached communities yet.** Fund documentation indicates that the first "starter package" of funding requests is to be considered for approval at the Board's tenth meeting in December 2026. None of the Fund documents consulted reports BIM money disbursed to projects [22][23].
- **The scale is small compared with the losses.** These amounts are small next to the losses described in Section 1. They are not adequate in size, timing or predictability.

## 6. Trafficking and exploitation after disasters

### 6.1 What institutional sources report

**The UN trafficking office (UNODC).** Its 2022 Global Report on Trafficking in Persons includes a section titled "Climate change: Affecting communities and increasing risks for trafficking in persons" [25]. It states plainly that "a systematic global analysis of the impact of climate change on trafficking in persons is missing", while "community level studies in different parts of the world point at weather induced disasters as root causes" [25]. Its examples:

- **The Philippines.** National authorities recorded about **670** trafficking cases between 2013 and 2015 in the regions hit by Typhoon Haiyan, and key informants reported that cases increased after the disaster [25].
- **The Bangladesh Sundarbans.** Forced and debt-bonded labour has been documented in fisheries and factories. People who move from disaster-affected areas to Dhaka or Kolkata can end up in "exploitative bonded labour schemes" [25].
- **Ghana.** Droughts and floods are associated with increasingly documented child trafficking for forced marriage or labour [25].

**The International Organization for Migration (IOM).** IOM's 2016 infosheet on the climate change–human trafficking nexus is candid about its evidence base. "In the absence of academic studies or policy documents on the topic, anecdotal evidence from field practitioners reflected in grey literature" indicates that sudden- and slow-onset events both affect trafficking [27]. It reports:

- increased trafficking in districts affected by Cyclone Sidr in Bangladesh in 2007, with similar trends after Cyclone Aila in 2009, when women-headed households were identified as especially vulnerable;
- the integration of counter-trafficking work into emergency responses after Typhoon Haiyan and the 2015 Nepal earthquake [27].

It also notes a gender bias in the evidence. Trafficking of men and boys for labour from disaster areas is less commonly reported [27].

### 6.2 What quantitative research finds, and where it disagrees

- **Disasters and internal trafficking.** A study of 158 countries over 2001–2011 found "a consistent positive link between natural disasters and the likelihood of internal trafficking". The authors argue that internal trafficking may rise because cross-border routes are disrupted [28]. This is an association in country-level data, not proof of individual-level causation.
- **Disasters and state anti-trafficking performance.** A separate study tested whether disasters weaken state anti-trafficking performance and found the opposite: states were "more likely to perform better", perhaps because disasters strengthen security institutions. The author notes that quantitative findings in this field often contradict qualitative ones [29].
- **The timing of harm.** Interviews with trafficking survivors in the Philippines challenge the assumption that traffickers mainly strike in the immediate aftermath of a disaster. Survivors described a "slow-burn effect": disaster-related disruption set off a chain of events that led to trafficking "many years later". The study also notes "a dearth of empirical evidence" in the field [30].

### 6.3 An honest summary

The plausible mechanism is well described: lost livelihoods, debt, displacement, family separation, weakened protection and the need to migrate quickly through irregular channels all increase exposure to exploitation. *Causal* evidence that climate change, as distinct from disasters generally or poverty, increases trafficking at scale remains thin. Much of the literature rests on case studies, key-informant reports and country-level associations. Detection is also affected by changes in reporting after disasters. The effect may be delayed by years, not concentrated in the emergency phase, which means short-term post-disaster counter-trafficking work could miss much of the risk [30].

## 7. Policy lessons

1. **Plan for internal, short-distance movement first.** Most climate-related mobility is internal [4][11]. Urban planning, social protection and land tenure in receiving towns matter more than border policy.
2. **Treat mobility as adaptation, not failure.** The IPCC finds that increasing adaptive capacity "improves the degree of choice under which migration decisions are made" (high confidence) [3]. Safe, regular channels (such as the Falepili pathway [13]) reduce reliance on smugglers and exploitative recruiters.
3. **Protect people who stay as well as people who move.** Trapped populations need investment in place [4][5].
4. **Build anti-trafficking measures into disaster response and recovery, and sustain them.** Given the "slow-burn" finding [30], counter-trafficking work should extend into multi-year livelihood recovery and labour-migration governance, not end with the emergency.
5. **Use humanitarian protection measures across borders.** The Nansen toolbox of temporary admission and suspension of return is available now without a new treaty [14].
6. **Make relocation community-led.** Fiji's consent and last-resort principles [16] and the Louisiana land-retention concession [18] are good practice, but they need enforcement and independent oversight [19].
7. **Close the gap between pledges and disbursement in loss-and-damage finance.** Convert pledges into binding agreements, set predictable replenishment and speed up access for small island states and least developed countries [22][23][24].
8. **Invest in data.** Disaster displacement figures are conservative and drought displacement is under-counted [1]. Data on food insecurity among displaced people exists for only 17 countries [26]. Trafficking data after disasters is weaker still [25][27].

## 8. Ethical risks and trade-offs

- **Alarmism versus neglect.** Inflated figures can feed securitised, anti-migrant responses [9], but debunking them can be misused to deny real harm. Honest uncertainty must not become an excuse for inaction.
- **The "climate refugee" label.** It draws attention to the problem, but it can blur legal categories and raise expectations that the 1951 Convention will not meet.
- **Mobility deals and security conditions.** Linking migration access to strategic or security commitments [13] may be accepted by a sovereign partner, but it raises questions of unequal bargaining power.
- **Relocation as dispossession.** Relocation done badly can repeat colonial patterns of removal [19].
- **Loss and damage as a substitute.** Small, slow funds may be used politically in place of emissions cuts or adequate adaptation finance.
- **Counter-trafficking that restricts movement.** Measures that discourage migration "to prevent trafficking" can push people into irregular channels, which raises their risk.

---

## 9. Counterarguments

- **"Climate is not the real cause; poverty and governance are."** This is largely right for near-term migration patterns [3]. But climate shocks act through those same channels, and for extreme events and sea-level rise the climate link is direct [3][4].
- **"Better data, not more displacement, explains the rising numbers."** This is partly true. IDMC credits better data with revealing some displacement [1]. Yet the IPCC's high-confidence finding that extremes increasingly drive displacement does not rest on raw counts alone [3].
- **"New legal protection would open the floodgates."** The evidence that most movement is internal and regional [4][11], and the Falepili quota of 280 places [13], suggest that managed, rights-based pathways can be modest and orderly.
- **"Loss-and-damage money will be wasted."** The start-up modalities are explicitly designed for "testing and learning" [23]. The bigger present risk is that too little money arrives too slowly [22][24].

---

## 10. Open questions

1. How much does slow-onset drought contribute to labour migration in the Sahel and the Horn, as distinct from conflict, and can it be measured without double-counting?
2. At what point, if any, will treaty bodies or courts find that conditions in a low-lying state have crossed the threshold set out in the views adopted on 24 October 2019 [12]?
3. Will the Falepili model be replicated, and can mobility agreements be separated from security conditions?
4. Can the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage reach community level directly, including for non-economic losses such as culture and land?
5. What study designs could establish whether climate shocks *cause* trafficking, and over what time frame, given the "slow-burn" pattern [30] and the contradictory quantitative results [28][29]?
6. How should policy weigh the right to stay against the right to move for communities facing relocation?

This study was revised on 18 November 2026 after an independent balance review.
