# Highly Neglected World Problems — Candidates, and a Deep Brief on Childhood Lead Poisoning

## PART 1 — Candidates

**1. Childhood lead poisoning (chosen for the deep brief).** The harm is very large. A World Bank modelling study estimates that children under five lost 765 million IQ points in 2019, that 5.5 million adults died of lead-related cardiovascular disease, and that the global cost was about US$6.0 trillion, or 6.9% of world GDP [1]. A multistakeholder working group convened by the Center for Global Development (CGD) estimates that about 815 million children, roughly one in three worldwide, have exposure levels that amount to lead poisoning [16]. Funding is tiny by comparison: the same group could identify only about US$11 million a year in philanthropic funding for lead prevention in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [16]. The problem is tractable. In Bangladesh, enforcement against turmeric adulteration cut the share of market samples containing detectable lead from 47% to 0% [12], and the CGD group judges that regulating spices, paint, consumer goods and battery recycling has the greatest potential to reduce exposure cost-effectively at scale [16]. **Lead poisoning is the top pick because it combines enormous, well-quantified harm, severe neglect in funding, and interventions backed by strong causal evidence.**

**2. Forced-labour trafficking into online-scam operations in Southeast Asia (runner-up).** The UN Human Rights Office (OHCHR) reports that credible sources put at least 120,000 people in Myanmar, and around 100,000 in Cambodia, in situations where they are forced to run online scams. Lao PDR, the Philippines and Thailand are also identified as destination or transit countries. Victims face torture, arbitrary detention, sexual violence and forced labour, and the scam centres generate billions of US dollars a year [C1]. The problem is neglected partly because the operations are clandestine and partly because official responses often treat victims as offenders rather than as people who were trafficked [C1]. Solutions exist: OHCHR sets out human-rights-based recommendations on identifying victims, not punishing them, and pursuing the criminal networks behind the centres [C1]. Tackling this problem also serves the wider goal of ending human trafficking worldwide. It is the runner-up rather than the deep pick because the evidence base is thinner. It rests mainly on UN, IOM and INTERPOL reporting and on investigative and academic journalism, with few peer-reviewed impact evaluations, and the victim counts are estimates of a hidden population.

**3. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR).** Global Burden of Disease (GBD) researchers estimate that 4.95 million deaths in 2019 were associated with bacterial AMR, of which 1.27 million were directly attributable to it, with the highest burden in low-resource settings [C2]. Their forecasts put attributable deaths at about 1.91 million in 2050. They also estimate that 92 million deaths could be averted between 2025 and 2050 through better care of severe infections and better access to antibiotics [C3]. The neglect lies partly in the drug pipeline. WHO's 2023 pipeline review found 32 traditional antibacterials in clinical development against its priority pathogens other than tuberculosis. Only 12 met at least one innovation criterion, and only four of those were active against a "critical" pathogen [C4]. Proven measures exist, including infection prevention, vaccination, appropriate antibiotic use and access to existing antibiotics [C3]. AMR is less neglected in absolute funding than lead, and its solutions depend more on new R&D.

### Key findings

1. **The global burden is among the largest of any single environmental risk.** The World Bank estimate for 2019 is 765 million IQ points lost in children under five and 5.5 million adult cardiovascular deaths. The cost, US$6.0 trillion or 6.9% of world GDP, puts lead roughly on a par with PM2.5 air pollution [1]. WHO, citing IHME, reports more than 3.5 million deaths and more than 71.5 million DALYs in 2023 [3]. The burden falls overwhelmingly on LMICs, which account for 95.3% of the IQ loss and 90.2% of the deaths [1].
2. **Exposure in LMICs remains high even after leaded petrol was phased out.** About 632 million LMIC children (48.5%) are estimated to exceed 5 µg/dL [4]. The CGD working group estimates 815 million children worldwide (about one in three) [16]. The CGD working paper notes that mean child blood lead in LMICs with reasonably representative samples is over 5 µg/dL, roughly ten times the US level [10].
3. **The dominant LMIC sources are industrial and consumer, not petrol.** They include the following:
   - **Informal ULAB recycling:** 6–16.8 million people are exposed at an estimated 10,599–29,241 informal sites [13].
   - **Spice adulteration:** lead-chromate pigment added to turmeric was found in 7 of 9 major producing districts in Bangladesh [11]. The CGD group reports adulterated spices across North Africa, the Middle East, and Central and South Asia [16].
   - **Lead paint:** often above 10,000 mg/kg in many LMICs [15]. Only 48% of countries had binding controls as of January 2024 [3].
   - **Artisanal aluminium cookware:** 15 of 42 pots tested released at least 1 µg of lead per serving [14].
   - **Ceramic glazes, cosmetics and traditional medicines:** WHO lists these among lead-containing products [3].
4. **No safe level has been identified.** In the pooled cohort analysis, a rise from 2.4 to 10 µg/dL cost 3.9 IQ points, while each further 10 µg/dL band cost less (1.9, then 1.1 points). The harm per unit is therefore steepest at low exposures [5]. WHO states that no level of exposure is known to be without harmful effects [3]. Adult cardiovascular mortality is also linked to low-level exposure [6]. Quasi-experimental US evidence shows that cutting blood lead from already low levels improves test scores [8].
5. **The effects appear to persist into adulthood.** In a New Zealand cohort, higher childhood blood lead was associated with lower IQ at age 38 and with downward socioeconomic mobility [7]. The CGD meta-analysis attributes more than a fifth of the rich–poor learning gap to lead [10].
6. **The economic cost is large under every method.** The cognitive-loss-only estimate is about 1.2% of world GDP [9]. The estimate that adds cardiovascular mortality is 6.9% [1], and 77% of that total comes from the welfare cost of the deaths [1].
7. **Funding is very small relative to the burden.** The CGD working group found about US$11 million a year in philanthropic funding for LMIC lead prevention. It estimates that US$350 million in development assistance through 2030 (US$50 million a year) would be enough to start scaled action [16].

### Contested / uncertain points

- **Global death estimates vary several-fold depending on method.** The IHME/GBD approach counts only the effect of lead on systolic blood pressure, which the CGD group describes as giving "around 1.6 million deaths per year" [16]. The World Bank instead models cardiovascular mortality directly from US cohort studies such as [6]. Four such studies give global totals of 2.3 to 8.3 million, and the authors' preferred figure is 5.5 million [1, 16]. [1] reports its estimate as six times GBD 2019. The most recent WHO/IHME figure for 2023 is above 3.5 million [3]. The direct approach rests on extrapolating US relationships to LMICs, which is the main point of dispute. All estimates also depend on sparse LMIC blood-lead data: background levels could be pooled for only 34 countries [4], and [1] itself calls for better measurement.
- **How much of the IQ effect persists, and how much is causal.** The long-run association in [7] comes from a single high-income cohort, adjusted for maternal and childhood IQ. Most LMIC learning estimates are observational. The CGD meta-analysis finds signs of publication bias and estimates that unobserved confounding could explain roughly a third of the OLS association [10]. Its Unjournal peer evaluation expressed low confidence in the point estimates while valuing the policy simulations [10]. The quasi-experimental evidence in [8] is causal but comes from the US.
- **Which source matters most varies by country and is poorly measured.** The CGD group states that "the exact contribution of spice contamination to overall lead exposure is unknown" [16]. It cites one analysis putting lead paint at 7.5% of the economic burden, with high uncertainty [16]. It also notes that battery recycling is an established cause of acute poisoning, but its share of population-wide exposure is less certain [16]. Turmeric is well established as a major source in Bangladesh [11, 12], but that cannot be generalised without local source studies. Cookware evidence so far comes from leaching tests, not population blood-lead attribution [14].

### Solutions, ranked by strength of evidence

1. **Spice and food testing and enforcement: strongest direct LMIC evidence.** Bangladesh's 2017–2021 package combined media coverage, education, rapid lead detection and fines enforced by the food safety authority. It cut market samples with detectable lead from 47% to 0% and mills with adulteration evidence from 30% to 0% [12]. Workers' blood lead fell a median 30% [12]. The CGD group also reports that Georgia largely eliminated lead from spices within two years through regulation and enforcement [16]. *Not yet proven:* the evaluation is a before–after study with no control group, and the blood-lead sample was only 15 workers. [12] itself calls for testing whether the approach can be replicated elsewhere.
2. **Lead-paint laws and enforcement: strong evidence that the products change; health benefits are slower and harder to measure.** Paint regulation and engagement with manufacturers have removed lead from market-leading paint brands in several Asian countries, and a before–after study found the same pattern in India and Brazil (as summarised in [16]). Paint is a well-documented global exposure route [15], and 52% of countries still lacked binding controls in January 2024 [3]. *Not yet proven:* the effect on population blood lead in LMICs, and paint's share of total exposure (one estimate is 7.5%, highly uncertain [16]). The benefits arrive over decades because paint already applied stays in buildings [16].
3. **Regulating used-lead-acid-battery recycling: the harm is well documented, but scale-up evidence is thin.** Informal ULAB sites cause severe exposure, with mean child blood lead of 31 µg/dL at exposed sites [13]. The CGD group states that robust, enforced regulation "can virtually eliminate" exposure among workers and nearby communities [16]. *Not yet proven:* evaluated programmes that formalise or close informal recycling in LMICs at national scale, and how much doing so lowers population-wide (rather than local) exposure [16].
4. **Blood-lead surveillance: essential for targeting the other measures, though it reduces nothing by itself.** Most countries have no system to detect lead exposure [16]. Background data could be pooled for only 34 LMICs [4], and WHO guidelines call for identifying and ending the source at 5 µg/dL or above [3]. The CGD group urges countries to build surveillance systems [16]. *Limitation:* surveillance only informs action. Its value depends on what is done with the results, and there is little evidence on low-cost designs at national scale.
5. **Donor funding: the cost-effectiveness case is strong on paper but rests on modelling.** The CGD group argues that US$350 million through 2030 would be enough to start scaled action [16]. Its working paper estimates that well-targeted interventions could raise learning by about 0.1 SD for highly exposed groups, and broad public-health campaigns by less than 0.05 SD, ignoring health benefits [10]. Set against a burden of up to 6.9% of world GDP [1] and about US$11 million a year in current funding [16], the potential return is very large. *Not yet proven:* these are simulations that assume a causal effect [10]. They are not measured results from programmes run at scale.
6. **Cookware and ceramics: weakest evidence.** Coating artisanal aluminium pots cut metal release by more than 98% in preliminary tests [14]. WHO lists ceramic glazes as a source [3]. *Not yet proven:* any evaluated programme that lowers blood lead by targeting cookware or glazes in LMICs.

### Open questions

- How large is the effect of lead on cardiovascular mortality at LMIC exposure levels, and which global death estimate should policy use? [1, 3, 6, 16]
- Which sources dominate exposure in each high-burden country, and can cheap source-attribution methods be scaled? [4, 16]
- Do the turmeric and paint successes [12, 16] hold up when they are replicated in other countries, and do they cut population blood lead as well as product contamination?
- How much of the learning effect is causal in LMIC settings? [8, 10]
