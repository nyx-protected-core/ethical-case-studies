# SNC-Lavalin: a fraud plea over Libya, a World Bank debarment, illegal political donations, the 2018 pressure on Canada's Attorney General, and her January 2019 removal

Date: 2026-11-19

*An ethics research case study. Research and writing only; this is not legal advice. Sources were checked on 19 November 2026. Three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Found**: what a court, regulator, parliamentary officer or plea record decided or admitted. **Alleged**: what a prosecutor charged, a witness claimed or a party argued; testimony and charges are not findings. **Reported**: facts known only from news coverage, labelled SECONDARY and used only for the timeline. Figures from prosecutors and agencies are flagged, because they have reasons to present large numbers when announcing results. Individuals are named only where a public office or a court record makes the name part of the public finding.*

## The ethical question

A large engineering firm won contracts in Libya with the help of the dictator's son, was caught, and then sought a remediation agreement (a negotiated settlement that avoids conviction). Senior government figures pressed the Attorney General to reconsider the prosecutor's refusal, citing jobs. When a company's size is used as an argument against prosecuting it, who pays: the company, its workers, the public, or the rule of law?

## Saadi Gadhafi's visits to Canada: what is found, and what is only reported

- **In the plea record, as summarised by others.** According to law-firm commentary on the Agreed Statement of Facts, SNC-Lavalin Construction "also paid for two visits by Gadhafi to Canada", and the costs were booked against Libyan projects [8]. The Globe and Mail reported that the plea record lists "security services, hotels, training, private parties and entertainment" during trips in 2008 and 2009, and payment to decorate his condominium in Toronto [9]. The Agreed Statement of Facts itself was not opened; these are SECONDARY accounts of it.
- **Reported only, from a preliminary inquiry.** In February 2019, after charges against a former vice-president were stayed for delay, La Presse (republished by the Toronto Star) reported evidence heard at his preliminary inquiry: that SNC-Lavalin hired a security firm with four contract bodyguards for the 2008 stay, that a police investigator told the court that roughly $30,000 recorded as "accompaniment services" went to escort services, at $600 to $7,500 a session, including almost $10,000 to one agency in Vancouver, and that the 2008 trip cost $1.95 million in total [10]. A National Post report from 2011 says the stay lasted three months and that SNC-Lavalin paid for the security team [11]. **These are reported allegations from evidence that was never tested at a trial, and no court made findings on them.**

## The jet: what the record shows

**No court, regulator or plea record found that SNC-Lavalin bought or gave Saadi Gadhafi a jet.** The claim comes only from testimony, reported in the press, by Ben Aissa: that Gadhafi asked for a Bombardier private jet as a gift for an airport contract in Libya, that Ben Aissa raised it with the then chief executive, who he said "had no problem with that" but wanted to wait until the contract was signed, and that the 2011 uprising ended the plan. The former chief executive denied approving the jet and said he received no such request. The same reporting says Ben Aissa testified that a yacht had already been provided, and that he made the first payments on a second, larger yacht, worth about $35 million, before the builder seized it after the uprising [9]. All of this is SECONDARY and untested testimony. The jet was never bought.

## Damages and penalties against market effect

- **Gain recorded in the plea, as summarised.** Law-firm commentary on the Agreed Statement of Facts reports about $1.8 billion in revenues and $235 million in gross pre-tax profit from the Libyan projects in 2001–2011, and notes the $280 million fine was about $45 million more than that profit [8]. The Globe and Mail described the contracts as worth roughly $2 billion [9]. Both are SECONDARY accounts; the figures should be checked against the Agreed Statement of Facts before reuse.
- **Fine:** $280 million over five years [7].
- **Debarment:** ten years from World Bank-financed work, reducible to eight [1].
- **Political contributions:** $117,803.49 reimbursed by the company, settled by a compliance agreement rather than prosecution [3].
- **Comparison.** On the summarised figures, the fine exceeded the gross profit. It came eight years after the conduct ended, and the plea to fraud rather than foreign bribery avoided automatic exclusion from Canadian federal contracts, according to the same commentary [8]. This case does not estimate the market position SNC-Lavalin held because of the Libyan contracts.

## Analysis

**Why bribes pay, when they are not caught.** In a worldwide sample of bribery cases, each additional dollar of bribe was associated with a $6–$9 increase in the bribing firm's value, although the net value of a bribe, conditional on being caught, was close to zero for the median firm; benefits were smaller in democracies with more disclosure [12]. Studies of political connections find they are common and valuable to firms [13][14]. The SNC-Lavalin record fits this pattern: influence bought from a ruler's son, booked as project costs, and a fine that arrived long after the contracts.

**Why certainty matters more than size.** Deterrence research finds that the likelihood of being caught matters more than the size of the penalty [15][16]. A large fine years later, after a plea that drops the bribery count, sends a weaker signal than its size suggests.

**Why the 2018–19 affair matters for the same question.** Parliament wrote into the remediation regime that, for foreign-bribery charges, the prosecutor "must not consider the national economic interest, the potential effect on relations with a state other than Canada or the identity of the organization" [17], a rule the Commissioner said was modelled on Article 5 of the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention [2]. The Commissioner found that the Prime Minister and his staff sought to influence the Attorney General's decision and that this improperly sought to further SNC-Lavalin's interests [2]. That is a finding against the Prime Minister under an ethics statute, not a criminal finding, and not a finding against SNC-Lavalin.

**The regime's stated purpose cuts both ways.** Its objectives include holding organisations accountable through "effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties" and reducing harm to "employees, customers, pensioners and others" who did not take part [4]. Both goals are legitimate. The ethical problem in 2018 was not the existence of the regime but pressure on the official meant to apply it independently.

## Fair counterarguments

- **Jobs are a legitimate concern.** The statute itself lists reducing harm to innocent employees and pensioners as an objective [4]. The Prime Minister's counsel argued before the Commissioner that potential job losses were not "national economic interest" within the meaning of the exclusion [2].
- **The participants deny wrongdoing.** Butts testified that "nothing inappropriate occurred" [6]. The Commissioner's finding is the view of a parliamentary officer under one statute; it was not tested in a court.
- **The company changed.** Commentary on the plea records that the executive team and board were replaced after 2012 and a compliance programme put in place [8] (SECONDARY). Penalties fall on today's shareholders and staff for past managers' conduct.
- **Negotiated settlements can serve the public.** The World Bank chose a negotiated debarment with a path to reduction for compliance [1]; such terms can buy cooperation and reform that a contested process might not.

## Proposed solutions

The proposals below are this case's own reasoning. Each carries an evidence rating for how well research supports the measure itself: **strong**, **moderate**, **weak** or **UNTESTED** (no study cited here tests whether the measure works).

1. **Wall off prosecutorial decisions in corporate cases.** Any ministerial direction to a prosecutor in a corporate case should be in writing and published, as the Shawcross doctrine and the Commissioner's reasoning imply [2]. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. Supported by legal principle; no study cited here measures its effect.*
2. **Raise the chance of detection, not just the fine.** Detection matters more than severity [15]; support for whistleblowers, auditors' questions and cross-border cooperation matters more than record fines. *Evidence rating: moderate. Broad deterrence reviews; none tests foreign-bribery enforcement specifically.*
3. **Price fines against the gain, and publish the gain.** The plea record contains revenue and profit figures [8]. Courts and prosecutors should publish the gain found alongside the fine, so the public can compare them. *Evidence rating: weak. Enforcement theory [16]; UNTESTED as a measured change in behaviour.*
4. **Keep cross-debarment.** Debarments recognised across development banks [1] remove the market benefit (access to financed contracts) rather than only charging money. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. No study cited here measures cross-debarment's deterrent effect.*
5. **Close the gap between straw donations and enforcement.** Corporate reimbursement of personal donations ran for seven years before the compliance agreement [3]. Routine matching of donors with employers' payroll records would find it sooner. *Evidence rating: UNTESTED. This is a design proposal; no study cited here tests it.*
