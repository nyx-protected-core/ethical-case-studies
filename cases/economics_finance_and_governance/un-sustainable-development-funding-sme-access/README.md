# UN sustainable development funding: who gets it, and how small firms can too

Date: 2026-11-19

## Summary

There is no single United Nations Sustainability Development Fund that disburses billions of dollars a year. The large money sits in climate and environment funds that mainly contract UN agencies, governments and multilateral banks. Small and mid-size firms can reach some of it, but almost always through intermediaries, not as direct grantees.

## Takeaway

**Large institutions dominate the big windows.** The name "United Nations Sustainability Development Fund" does not appear in official UN financing documents. The closest UN SDG vehicle, the Joint SDG Fund, is measured in tens of millions of dollars a year, not billions, and it funds UN country teams. The funds that do move billions — above all the Green Climate Fund and the Global Environment Facility family — pay accredited international agencies and banks first. Small and mid-size enterprises can benefit, mainly as end-borrowers or suppliers, but they cannot today obtain comparable direct allocations. Official totals below are marked [GOV-flag]: funds have an incentive to report large commitments, high co-financing ratios, and wide reach.

## What that name actually refers to

No UN body currently operates a fund under the title "United Nations Sustainability Development Fund" or "UN Sustainability Development Fund." Two real instruments are easily confused with that name, and neither is a billions-a-year cheque-writing office for private firms.

- The Joint SDG Fund is the current UN system fund for integrated Sustainable Development Goal programmes. It is led by UN Resident Coordinators and implemented by UN country teams with governments and other UN entities. In 2024 it recorded US$89.7 million in deposits and committed US$86.5 million to 129 new joint programmes, plus US$61.1 million in co-funding [GOV-flag]. In 2025 it reported US$51.8 million in signed contributions and 73 new programmes in 55 countries [GOV-flag]. Since 2019 it has committed about US$400 million [GOV-flag]. Its 2024–25 windows were digital transformation, food systems, jobs and social protection, SDG localization, and energy transition.
- The Sustainable Development Goals Fund (SDG-F), set up in 2014 and hosted by UNDP, was the predecessor. Official closing documents report about US$58.8 million received, roughly US$70 million in programmes once matching funds were counted, and 22 joint programmes in 23 countries. Spain initiated a wind-down in December 2017; operations were to close by September 2019, with remaining resources transferred to the Joint SDG Fund.

The "billions of dollars a year" scale sits in a different cluster: UN-linked climate and development funds and vertical funds that UN agencies implement. The main ones reviewed here are the Green Climate Fund (GCF), the Global Environment Facility (GEF) family, the Adaptation Fund, the UN Capital Development Fund (UNCDF), the International Fund for Agricultural Development's Private Sector Financing Programme (IFAD PSFP), and UNDP's role as a large implementer of those vertical funds. UNDP is not itself a separate multi-billion-dollar fund that firms apply to; it is one of the large accredited or implementing agencies through which GEF, GCF and related money is programmed.

## Who typically gets the money

Green Climate Fund official portfolio figures at 31 December 2025 [GOV-flag]. International access entities are mostly multilateral development banks, UN agencies and other international institutions. Direct access entities are national or regional organisations registered in developing countries. Approved funding is not the same as money disbursed, and disbursed money is not the same as money reaching a small firm.

Across the large windows, the first recipients are institutions that can pass fiduciary accreditation and manage multi-million-dollar projects: UN agencies, multilateral development banks, international commercial or development banks, national development banks, and governments. Small and mid-size enterprises almost never sit in that first ring.

|---|---|---|---|
| GCF approved funding | US$15.51 billion | US$3.8 billion | US$19.3 billion |
| GCF disbursements | US$5.4 billion | US$0.8 billion | US$6.26 billion |

The GCF numbers make the pattern visible. By the end of 2025, official reporting said direct access entities were 67% of the accredited network [GOV-flag]. That is a count of institutions, not of money. International access entities still held 80.33% of approved GCF funding and received US$5.4 billion of the US$6.26 billion disbursed, against US$0.8 billion for direct access entities [GOV-flag]. In other words, national and regional entities are numerous; the large international agencies still take most of the cash. The Fund's own report also notes that 61% of direct-access projects are small or micro-sized, while a majority of international-access projects are larger.

The GEF shows the same concentration by a different door. Only 18 designated agencies may submit GEF Trust Fund projects. In the official December 2025 GEF-8 scorecard, UNDP held 29.2%, UNEP 17.3%, FAO 15.9%, the World Bank 7.7% and UNIDO 7.3% of cumulative programming — 77.4% among five large UN or multilateral agencies [GOV-flag]. A handful of national or regional agencies appear in the remainder, each with a small share. Official co-financing ratios (8.5 to 1 overall, and 19 to 1 for blended-finance operations) are mobilisation claims and should be read with a reporting-incentive flag; they are not evidence that small firms received that co-finance.

The Joint SDG Fund is explicit about its recipients: Resident Coordinators lead, UN country teams implement, and more than 30 UN entities were engaged in the 2024–25 rounds [GOV-flag]. The Adaptation Fund is more open to national implementing entities than the GCF is in funding share, and official FY2025 reporting said those national entities executed nearly one-third of newly approved projects [GOV-flag]. Even there, a country still has to work through an accredited national, regional or multilateral entity. A cumulative split of Adaptation Fund money by entity type was reported in an official October 2025 portfolio note; that file was not opened in this review, so those shares are left UNVERIFIED.

Private-sector facilities do not overturn this picture. The GCF approved 12 new private-sector projects in 2025, with nearly US$1 billion of GCF financing and official co-financing of US$8.3 billion; it said the private sector accounted for 36% of the overall portfolio [GOV-flag]. Peer-reviewed work by Kalinowski (2024), using earlier 2022 portfolio data, found that a large part of the co-finance counted under private-sector projects came from public development banks rather than private investors, and that private GCF activity clustered in renewable energy, where returns are clearer than in adaptation. Those 2022 stock figures are outdated as portfolio totals; the structural warning about headline mobilisation ratios still applies.

Omukuti, Barrett, White, Marchant and Averchenkova (2022), in a peer-reviewed article, reached a related conclusion on adaptation: the GCF's policies commit to local delivery, but the Fund lacked a unified definition of "local," published limited detail on how approved adaptation funding is actually spent, and did not treat local delivery as a priority in accreditation or readiness support. Author affiliations for both papers were checked and are not Canadian.

## How a small or mid-size firm gets in

A typical SME will not send a proposal to the GCF, the GEF Trust Fund, or the Joint SDG Fund and receive a direct grant on the scale those funds advertise. The workable routes are intermediated. They can still be useful, but they are narrower, slower, and usually smaller than the headline windows.

1. **Name the real window and the real buyer.** Decide whether the firm is seeking climate or environment project finance, rural or last-mile business finance, a role inside a UN-led programme, or a procurement contract to sell goods or services to the UN. Those are different doors. Grant funds and UN purchasing are not the same thing.
2. **Find the national intermediaries, not the fund headquarters.** For GCF money, start with the country's national designated authority and any direct access entity already accredited (often a national development bank, environment fund or similar institution). For the Adaptation Fund, the route is a national, regional or multilateral implementing entity. For GEF projects, the route is one of the 18 implementing agencies and the country's GEF operational focal point. UNCDF publishes programme contacts in the least-developed countries where it works.
3. **Partner with an accredited or executing entity.** This is the standard path into GCF and Adaptation Fund resources. The accredited entity blends concessional fund money with other capital and may lend, guarantee or invest through executing entities such as local banks, funds or government programmes. UNCDF guidance on GCF access for SMEs stresses this intermediated model, and notes that a national direct access entity is often better placed than an international agency for local-currency lending and for relationships with domestic firms.
4. **Use credit lines and blended facilities at local banks.** Many private-sector GCF and IFAD deals are facilities, not cheques to a single company. A firm applies to the participating bank or fund against published eligibility (sector, climate or rural use, size, collateral, currency). This is how most SMEs will ever touch GCF Private Sector Facility money, IFAD private-sector financing, or UNCDF guarantees.
5. **Use the small-ticket windows that actually accept local applicants.** GEF Small Grants Programme core grants are up to US$75,000, with some strategic projects up to US$150,000 and a civil-society challenge window up to US$300,000 [GOV-flag]. Opened country calls typically restrict applicants to locally registered non-profit organisations, community groups and similar bodies, not for-profit firms. A firm whose work is a community environmental project may need a non-profit partner. GEF-8 also set aside US$10 million for a Small Grants Programme microfinance initiative; the detailed borrower rules were not opened here (UNVERIFIED). UNCDF programmes, including later-stage MSME facilities such as FinWise in East Africa, are built around last-mile enterprises. IFAD's Private Sector Financing Programme can finance companies or intermediaries serving small-scale farmers and rural MSMEs; the official contact on the programme page is nso@ifad.org.
6. **Treat direct accreditation as a path for institutions, not typical firms.** The GCF allows private banks, funds, corporations and other for-profit organisations to apply for accreditation, including as direct access entities if they are legally established in a developing country. Accreditation tests fiduciary systems, safeguards and institutional capacity. It qualifies a partner to propose projects; it does not itself provide funding. For a ordinary SME, the cost and time of becoming an accredited entity are usually disproportionate. Project-specific assessment exists as a narrower alternative for some applicants; it is still an institutional process.
7. **If the aim is to sell to the UN, use procurement, not the funds.** The United Nations Global Marketplace is the common registration point for suppliers. A firm creates a supplier account, completes basic registration, chooses accurate product codes, and responds to notices from individual UN organisations. This can be a real source of revenue. It is not an allocation from the Joint SDG Fund or the climate funds, and it is awarded as value-for-money purchasing, not as sustainable-development grant funding.

## What firms can do now, and what would have to change

### What a firm can do now

1. Map the intermediaries in the countries where the firm already operates: the GCF national designated authority and direct access entities, the Adaptation Fund implementing entity, GEF agency contacts, UNCDF and IFAD country programmes, and any local bank already advertising a climate, green or rural MSME line backed by those funds.
2. Build a proposal the intermediary can use: a clear climate, environment or rural-development use of proceeds, basic environmental and social documentation, and financials that a bank can underwrite. Fund-ready paperwork is often the binding constraint, not a shortage of headline capital.
3. If the work is community-based and non-commercial, watch GEF Small Grants Programme calls and apply through an eligible local organisation. If the work is commercial, do not rely on that programme.
4. In least-developed and other last-mile markets, ask UNCDF and its partner financial institutions about guarantees, concessional loans and technical assistance. The envelopes are much smaller than GCF or GEF, but the mandate is closer to SME finance.
5. Rural agribusinesses and farmer-serving firms can approach IFAD's private-sector unit or the financial intermediaries in IFAD-backed facilities, rather than waiting for a UN grant call.
6. Register on the United Nations Global Marketplace if the firm can supply goods or services the UN buys. Keep that path separate from grant-seeking.
7. Do not spend scarce management time trying to become a GCF or Adaptation Fund accredited entity unless the organisation is already a financial institution or fund with the systems those boards require.

### What would have to change for SMEs to reach comparable funding

"Comparable funding" cannot mean that a single small firm receives a GCF- or GEF-scale project. It can mean that a much larger share of approved money is reserved for, and actually disbursed to, domestic firms and local financial institutions on terms those firms can use. The evidence above, and the two peer-reviewed critiques, point to the same reforms.

1. Move the money, not just the accreditation count. Official GCF figures already show a majority-direct-access network receiving about one-fifth of approved funding and an even smaller share of disbursements. Boards could set, and publish, funding and disbursement floors for national and regional entities, and report SME end-borrower numbers and volumes, not only a private-sector share of the portfolio.
2. Create dedicated SME windows with small tickets, local currency, and shorter decisions. Accreditation and full board projects are designed for large, slow operations. Intermediated lines through national banks, first-loss guarantees, and local-currency facilities are the instruments that actually reach firms. Those exist in fragments (GCF private-sector facilities, UNCDF guarantees, IFAD intermediaries, the GEF non-grant instrument). They are not yet a system.
3. Make accreditation cheaper and faster for domestic financial institutions, including project-specific assessment, and treat local delivery capacity as a requirement, not an afterthought. That follows the Omukuti et al. critique of accreditation and readiness, and UNCDF's own advice that national direct access entities are better placed to serve SMEs.
4. Open a clear for-profit MSME track alongside the GEF Small Grants Programme, or expand and publish the rules of the microfinance initiative, so commercial environmental enterprises are not forced to pretend to be non-profits or to wait for a rare blended-finance deal.
5. Shorten the gap between approval and disbursement. The GCF had approved US$19.3 billion by the end of 2025 and disbursed US$6.26 billion [GOV-flag]. Slow cash is a practical barrier for firms that cannot wait years.
6. Treat mobilisation ratios with caution in policy and in fundraising. Kalinowski's finding that much "private" co-finance was still public development-bank money is a reason to measure additional private capital, local-currency lending, and SME reach, rather than headline co-financing multiples. The Joint SDG Fund's official claim that each dollar unlocks about twenty more [GOV-flag] should be read the same way.
7. Keep public concessional resources in place for adaptation and for markets where private returns are weak. Kalinowski argues that private climate finance from high-income countries is unlikely, on its own, to finance the shift needed in the most vulnerable economies, especially for adaptation. Expanding SME access should not be used as a reason to shrink those public funds.

## Limits of this review

- No figure here was invented. Where a number comes from a fund or official report, it is marked [GOV-flag]. Where a document could not be opened, the point is marked UNVERIFIED.
- This is a global topic, so Canadian data and Canadian-affiliated research were not used. Author affiliations were checked for the two peer-reviewed papers cited.
- The IFAD Executive Board allocation reported as US$90 million for the Private Sector Financing Programme in 2025–27 was not confirmed from the opened Board PDF (UNVERIFIED). The description of the programme itself comes from IFAD's official programme page, which was opened.
- A cumulative Adaptation Fund funding split by national, regional and multilateral entities, cited in an official October 2025 note, was not opened (UNVERIFIED). The FY2025 "nearly one-third of new projects" figure is taken from the official annual performance report.
- Detailed eligibility rules for the GEF Small Grants Programme microfinance initiative were not opened (UNVERIFIED). Country calls that were opened treat core Small Grants Programme windows as non-profit.
- The Kalinowski article's portfolio statistics are from 2022 and are not used as current totals. Current GCF stock figures come from official 2025 reports.
- Nothing in this review was published to an external repository.
