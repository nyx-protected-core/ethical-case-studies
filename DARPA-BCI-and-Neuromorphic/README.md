# DARPA and Brain-Computer Interface Funding: Public Programs, Neuromorphic Devices, N3 and Neuralink (1970s to now)

Date: 19 November 2026
Author: anonymous

## Summary

This case collects the public record of US defence research money for brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and brain-inspired ("neuromorphic") computing, from the ARPA biocybernetics work of the 1970s to the 2020s. For comparison it adds Neuralink, the largest privately funded BCI company, whose money is private and is kept separate from DARPA money throughout.

Every dollar figure has a source and a year, and a figure type:

- **AWARD CEILING**: an "up to" amount. Payment depends on milestones; it is not money spent.
- **AWARD**: a stated contract or grant value, usually from the performer's own press release.
- **OBLIGATED**: money recorded as obligated in a federal spending database.
- **PLANNED**: an amount an agency said it intended to spend, before appropriation.
- **PROGRAM TOTAL (SECONDARY)**: a whole-program figure reported by news, not by the agency.
- **PRIVATE RAISE**: money sold in a private securities offering, as filed with the SEC.

No appropriation-level figure for a single BCI program was extracted for this version (see Open questions). Where no amount could be found, the text says **not publicly stated**.

Primary records (DARPA releases and program pages, White House fact sheets, contract reports, SEC filings, federal spending data, statutes) and peer-reviewed papers are preferred. News is used only where needed and is marked **SECONDARY**. Every figure issued by a government agency carries a [GOV-flag] note on its reporting incentive. Performer press releases are also self-interested: universities and companies have reason to quote the largest possible ceiling.

**Overall evidence strength.** Strong for award ceilings announced by DARPA itself (SUBNETS, RAM, NESD) and for Neuralink's SEC filings. Moderate for figures that come only from performer press releases. Weak for whole-program totals (N3, ElectRx, Revolutionizing Prosthetics), which come from news. DARPA rarely publishes program totals, and actual spending per program is mostly not public.

## Background and definitions

- **Brain-computer interface (BCI).** A system that measures brain activity and turns it into commands for an outside device, without using the body's normal nerve-and-muscle output. Some BCIs also "write" to the brain by stimulating it. These are called bidirectional.
- **Invasive BCI.** Electrodes are placed by surgery on or in the brain (for example the Utah array, or Neuralink's N1 threads). Signals are strong and precise, but surgery carries risks of infection, bleeding and device failure.
- **Non-invasive BCI.** Sensors sit outside the skull (EEG, near-infrared light, magnetometers, ultrasound). No surgery is needed, but signals are weaker and blurrier.
- **"Minutely invasive."** DARPA's term in N3 for approaches that put something inside the body without surgery, such as injected nanoparticles.
- **Peripheral neural interface.** Electrodes on nerves in the limbs rather than in the brain. Several prosthetics programs below (RE-NET, HAPTIX) use these. They are included because DARPA funds them as part of the same neural-interface portfolio.
- **Neuromorphic computing.** Computer chips whose design borrows from the brain, such as spiking "neurons" and "synapses" that compute and store in the same place. These chips do not connect to a brain. They are included because DARPA funded them as part of its brain-inspired portfolio.

## 1. The 1970s: ARPA biocybernetics

**The first "BCI" paper.** Jacques Vidal of UCLA published "Toward Direct Brain-Computer Communication" in the *Annual Review of Biophysics and Bioengineering* in 1973 [P1]. It is widely credited with coining the term. **Correction to a common claim:** the paper's own acknowledgement lists support from NSF grants GJ-32221 and GK-31463X and Air Force Office of Scientific Research grant 72-2384. It does not acknowledge ARPA [P1]. ARPA support for Vidal's later work is often stated in secondary accounts but was not confirmed from a primary record here.

**ARPA's biocybernetics program.** Secondary accounts say ARPA set up a biocybernetics effort, first titled "Close-Coupled Man/Machine Systems Research," in the mid-1970s, with teams at several US universities and research institutes [N1 SECONDARY]. A primary contract report confirms the program's existence and one contract value: Stanford Research Institute's *Feasibility Study for Design of a Biocybernetic Communication System* (final report, August 1975) was prepared for ARPA under ARPA Order No. 2034, contract DAHC-15-72-C-0167, program manager Dr. George Lawrence, running 9 February 1972 to 31 August 1975. The stated **amount of contract was $338,254** [R1] (AWARD) [GOV-flag: a contract value on a contractor's report cover sheet; it is the contract amount, not audited spending]. The total budget of the ARPA biocybernetics program is **not publicly stated** in any source opened here.

## 2. The 1990s and 2000s: brain-machine interfaces and prosthetics

**Brain-Machine Interfaces (2002).** In August 2002 Duke University announced a **$26 million contract** from DARPA's Brain-Machine Interfaces program to Miguel Nicolelis's group, aimed at brain-controlled prosthetic limbs and "neurorobots" [R2] (AWARD; university release) [GOV-flag: the amount comes from the recipient, which has an interest in a large headline figure; DARPA's own release was not opened]. Awards to other performers in the same program were not found; the program total is **not publicly stated**.

**Revolutionizing Prosthetics (2006–).** DARPA's program to build an advanced, eventually neurally controlled, prosthetic arm for injured service members [R3].
- Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory (APL): **$30.4 million** for the first phase of "Revolutionizing Prosthetics 2009," announced 2006 [R4] (AWARD) [GOV-flag: performer release].
- DEKA Research and Development: **$18.1 million** under "Revolutionizing Prosthetics 2007" (the arm later known as LUKE) [R5] (AWARD; DoD news release) [GOV-flag: DoD-issued figure in a story promoting the program].
- APL: up to **$34.5 million** in 2010 to manage human testing of the Modular Prosthetic Limb with a brain-controlled interface [R6] (AWARD CEILING) [GOV-flag: performer release].
- A 2006 trade report described the effort as a "two-phase, four-year, **$70 million**" program [N2 SECONDARY]. The often-quoted "about $100 million" total for the whole program was **not confirmed** from a primary record. The sum of the three awards above is $83.0 million, but that is a sum of announcements, not a verified program total.

**RE-NET (Reliable Neural-Interface Technology, about 2010).** Aimed at making neural interfaces for prosthetic control last for years rather than months, including peripheral interfaces [R7] [P2]. Dollar amount: **not publicly stated**.

## 3. The 2010s: the BRAIN Initiative era

**DARPA's share of the BRAIN Initiative (2013).** The White House fact sheet of 2 April 2013 said DARPA "plans to invest **$50 million** in 2014" in the initiative [R8] (PLANNED, FY2014) [GOV-flag: an announced intention made at a political launch; it is not an appropriation or an outlay, and agencies have reason to present launch figures generously].

**SUBNETS (Systems-Based Neurotechnology for Emerging Therapies, 2014).** Closed-loop brain recording and stimulation devices for psychiatric illness in service members and veterans, building on deep brain stimulation [R9]. Awards announced May 2014: UC San Francisco up to **$26 million** and Massachusetts General Hospital up to **$30 million** [N3 SECONDARY; DARPA's release [R9] names the teams but the opened text did not show the amounts] (AWARD CEILING) [GOV-flag: amounts as reported from the agency and performers at launch]. The program planned five years of milestones ending in submission of devices to the FDA [R9].

**RAM (Restoring Active Memory, 2014).** Implantable "neuroprosthetics" to restore memory after traumatic brain injury or disease [R10]. DARPA announced in July 2014 that UCLA would receive up to **$15 million** and the University of Pennsylvania up to **$22.5 million** over four years, plus a cooperative agreement of up to **$2.5 million** with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, "with full funding contingent on ... meeting a series of technical milestones" [R10] (AWARD CEILING) [GOV-flag: DARPA's own launch release; ceilings are not spending].

**RAM Replay (2015).** Studied how the brain "replays" memories, to improve memory and skill learning, using mostly non-invasive methods [R11]. Dollar amount: **not publicly stated**.

**HAPTIX (Hand Proprioception and Touch Interfaces, 2015).** Implanted peripheral-nerve interfaces to give prosthetic hands a sense of touch and position [R12]. One award was found: the University of Florida was awarded $2.7 million for 18 months, with a potential total of up to **$5.4 million** [R13] (AWARD CEILING) [GOV-flag: performer release]. The program total is **not publicly stated**.

**ElectRx (Electrical Prescriptions, 2015).** Closed-loop stimulation of peripheral nerves to treat pain, inflammation and other conditions; DARPA lists it as complete [R14]. News reported seven teams and **$78.9 million** over five years [N4 SECONDARY] (PROGRAM TOTAL) [GOV-flag: figure as reported at launch, not confirmed on a DARPA page opened here].

**NESD (Neural Engineering System Design, 2016–17).** An implantable interface able to record from up to one million neurons, to restore vision, hearing and speech [R15]. The July 2017 DARPA release named six teams: Brown University, Columbia University, Fondation Voir et Entendre (France), John B. Pierce Laboratory, Paradromics Inc. and UC Berkeley [R16]. News reported the awards at up to **$65 million** in total [N5 SECONDARY] (AWARD CEILING). For Paradromics, news reported about $18 million [N5 SECONDARY], while USAspending shows Department of Defense contract N6600117C4005 with an award amount of **$14,184,508** [R17] (OBLIGATED) [GOV-flag: database figure entered by the awarding agency; it may not include later modifications].

**TNT (Targeted Neuroplasticity Training, 2017).** Non-invasive peripheral nerve stimulation to speed up learning of skills such as languages [R18] [R19]. The University of Florida reported two awards of up to $4.2 million each, up to **$8.4 million** in total [R19] (AWARD CEILING) [GOV-flag: performer release]. The program total is **not publicly stated**.

## 4. 2018 to now: N3 and Bridging the Gap Plus

**N3 (Next-Generation Nonsurgical Neurotechnology, announced March 2018; awards May 2019).** N3 aimed at high-performance, bidirectional brain-machine interfaces for **able-bodied service members**, needing no surgery. DARPA's stated uses include control of unmanned aerial vehicles and "active cyber defense systems," and teaming with computers in complex missions [R20] [R21]. The target was to read from and write to 16 independent channels within a 16 mm³ volume of neural tissue within 50 ms [R20]. DARPA lists the program as complete [R20].

**Program total.** DARPA "declined to comment on the amount of funding" [N6 SECONDARY]. *MIT Technology Review* reported N3 as "a **$104 million** effort" [N7 SECONDARY], and a peer-reviewed governance paper repeats that figure, citing news [P5]. The $104 million total is therefore **reported, not confirmed** by DARPA. The three public team figures sum to about $57.5 million.

**Bridging the Gap Plus (BG+, 2020).** Implantable systems that monitor and stimulate the injured spinal cord in the days after injury, to aid recovery [R25]. UC Davis reported a five-year **$36 million** DARPA contract to its consortium [R26] (AWARD) [GOV-flag: performer release]. Johns Hopkins reported **$13.48 million** to its scientists for implantable ultrasound devices [R27] (AWARD) [GOV-flag: performer release]. The program total is **not publicly stated**.

## 5. Neuromorphic and brain-inspired computing

**SyNAPSE (Systems of Neuromorphic Adaptive Plastic Scalable Electronics, 2008–).** Aimed at chips with the "form, function and architecture" of a mammalian brain. In 2014 DARPA announced IBM's chip built under SyNAPSE (later called TrueNorth): 5.4 billion transistors, one million "neurons," 256 million "synapses," under 100 milliwatts in operation [R28] [GOV-flag: DARPA's own performance claims in a release promoting the program].
- Phase 0 awards (November 2008): IBM **$4.9 million**, HRL Laboratories **$6.2 million**, Hewlett-Packard **$1.8 million** [N8 SECONDARY] (AWARD).
- IBM later phases: **$16.1 million** (second phase) and **$21 million** (third phase, 2011) [N9 SECONDARY] (AWARD).
- IBM's cumulative total: "about **$53 million** in DARPA funding since the project's inception in 2008," including a fourth-phase award of **$12 million** [N10 SECONDARY].
- HRL's later-phase awards are reported in tertiary sources but were not confirmed from a primary or news record opened here: **not confirmed**.

[GOV-flag on SyNAPSE: amounts reported by news from DARPA and company statements at award; no DARPA total for the program was found.]

**UPSIDE (Unconventional Processing of Signals for Intelligent Data Exploitation, 2012).** A DARPA Microsystems Technology Office solicitation (BAA-12-53) for analog and "physics-based" signal processing, including brain-inspired approaches [R29]. Dollar amount: **not publicly stated** in the listing opened.

**Physical Intelligence (about 2009).** A DARPA program on self-organising physical systems, often listed with SyNAPSE. No working DARPA page was found and no dollar figure: **not publicly stated**. It is mentioned only for completeness.

**ERI (Electronics Resurgence Initiative, 2017–).** A broad semiconductor programme, not a neuromorphic program. DARPA described it as a five-year effort of up to **$1.5 billion** [R30] (PLANNED) [GOV-flag: an announced plan, subject to appropriation; agencies present such totals to signal scale]. Only some ERI work is brain-inspired, and the share that is neuromorphic is **not publicly stated**. ERI's headline figure must not be counted as neuromorphic money.

**US government money to Neuralink.** A search of USAspending.gov for "Neuralink" as a recipient, across contracts, grants, loans and direct payments for fiscal years 2009–2026, returned **no awards** [R34]. **Regulatory milestones.**
- **Breakthrough Device designation (July 2020).** Reported by the company at a public event [N12 SECONDARY]. The designation speeds FDA review; it is not an approval.
- **FDA investigational device exemption (IDE), May 2023.** The FDA cleared Neuralink's first-in-human study; the FDA acknowledged the clearance but gave no details [N13 SECONDARY]. Neuralink states the PRIME study runs under an IDE awarded in May 2023 and was approved by an independent institutional review board [R35].
- **Breakthrough Device designation for "Blindsight" (September 2024),** a vision device, as announced by the company [R36].

**Regulatory and animal-welfare matters.** Labels follow the IBM case: FINDING, NO-ADMISSION, ALLEGATION, REPORTED (SECONDARY).
- **REPORTED (SECONDARY): DOT hazardous-materials fine.** Reuters reviewed Department of Transportation records showing that inspections in February 2023 found Neuralink had not registered as a hazardous-materials transporter and had packaged hazardous waste, including xylene, improperly. The fine was **$2,480**, reduced because the company agreed to fix the problems [N14 SECONDARY] [GOV-flag: agency enforcement figure, seen only through news; the record itself was not opened].
- **ALLEGATION, outcome not public: USDA Office of Inspector General probe.** Reuters reported in December 2022 that the USDA Inspector General had opened an investigation into possible Animal Welfare Act violations, at a federal prosecutor's request [N15 SECONDARY]. No public OIG finding was found.
- **REPORTED (SECONDARY): USDA inspection.** In a July 2023 letter to a member of Congress, the Agriculture Secretary wrote that a "focused" inspection had found no violations of animal research rules beyond a 2019 incident the company had already self-reported [N16 SECONDARY] [GOV-flag: an agency describing its own inspection to Congress]. An animal-advocacy group disputed the result [N17 SECONDARY].
- **ALLEGATION, outcome not public: SEC request.** In November 2023 members of Congress asked the SEC to investigate whether Neuralink misled investors about animal deaths [N17 SECONDARY]. No SEC action was found.

## 7. Ethics and dual use

- **Dual use.** Tennison and Moreno (2012, peer-reviewed) set out how national-security funding of neuroscience, including BCIs and neuromodulation, raises dual-use concerns: the same tools that restore function can be used for enhancement, interrogation or deception detection. They argue for open discussion, dual-use awareness among researchers and ethical oversight, rather than a full split between neuroscience and national security [P3].
- **From therapy to "able-bodied" use.** Most programs above began with injured service members and patients. N3 marked a shift: DARPA framed it explicitly for **able-bodied** service members controlling drones and cyber defences [R21]. Its own program manager was quoted saying "working with drones and swarms of drones ... those types of things are what these devices are really for" [N7 SECONDARY].
- **Mental privacy and brain data.** Ienca and Andorno (2017, peer-reviewed) propose new rights to cognitive liberty, mental privacy, mental integrity and psychological continuity [P4]. A peer-reviewed governance paper notes that international humanitarian law does not explicitly protect combatants' mental integrity, and warns of a possible neurotechnology "arms race" in brain data [P5].
- **Self-reporting by the funder.** The main peer-reviewed overview of DARPA's BCI programs was written by DARPA-affiliated authors and funded by DARPA [P2]. It is useful for program descriptions but has an obvious interest in presenting the programs well.
- **Private money and disclosure.** Neuralink raised about $1.24 billion with no public-market reporting duty [R31]. Animal-welfare and safety concerns reached the public mainly through leaks, advocacy groups and news [N15–N17].

## 8. Human-subject protections

- **US Department of Defense research** with human subjects must follow the Common Rule as adopted by DoD at **32 CFR Part 219** (informed consent and review by an institutional review board) [R37].
- **10 U.S.C. § 980** bars DoD funds for research on a human subject unless the subject's informed consent is obtained in advance (with narrow exceptions for subjects who cannot consent) [R38].
- **Implanted devices** tested in people need an FDA **investigational device exemption** under **21 CFR Part 812** when they are significant-risk devices [R39]. Both the DARPA-funded clinical work (for example SUBNETS planned FDA submissions [R9]) and Neuralink's PRIME study [R35] sit under this regime.
- **Open concern:** service members are a population in which consent can be affected by rank and command pressure. The statutes require consent but do not by themselves remove that pressure (see Where people disagree).

## Where people disagree

- **Who credited the first BCI money.** Secondary histories often say ARPA funded Vidal's 1973 work. The paper's own acknowledgement names NSF and the Air Force, not ARPA [P1]. ARPA's biocybernetics contracts of the same years are documented separately [R1].
- **Therapy or enhancement.** DARPA presents most programs as restoring function to injured service members [R9] [R10]. Critics argue that the same work builds toward enhancement and military control uses, which N3 made explicit [R21] [P3].
- **Consent in the military.** Statute requires informed consent for DoD-funded human research [R38]. Ethicists ask whether consent can be fully free inside a chain of command [P3].
- **Neuralink's animal record.** USDA's inspection found no violations beyond a self-reported 2019 incident [N16 SECONDARY]; an advocacy group disputed that [N17 SECONDARY]. No adjudicated finding exists either way.
- **Program totals.** News totals (N3 $104M, ElectRx $78.9M, Revolutionizing Prosthetics $70M or "$100M+") are widely repeated but none was confirmed on a DARPA page.

## Values and proposals (normative)

These are proposals, not findings. Each is **UNTESTED** and rated on the Strong / Moderate / Limited / Very limited scale for how directly the evidence above supports it.

1. **Publish program totals and spending.** DARPA should publish, for each neurotechnology program, the appropriated amount and actual obligations, not only award ceilings. Evidence support: **Strong** (most totals above are missing or come only from news).
2. **Standing ethics review for military BCIs, made public.** Programs aimed at able-bodied service members should have an independent ethics review whose reports are released. Evidence support: **Moderate** [P3] [P5].
3. **Mental-privacy protections for brain data,** for both military and private BCIs, in line with proposed neurorights. Evidence support: **Moderate** [P4] [P5].
4. **Extra consent safeguards for service members,** such as a consent monitor outside the chain of command. Evidence support: **Limited** (raised in the literature; no study of how consent works in these programs was found).
5. **Public reporting of animal-research outcomes by private BCI firms,** comparable to what federally funded labs face. Evidence support: **Limited** [N14–N17].

## Open questions

- What did each BCI program actually receive in appropriations? DARPA RDT&E budget justification books (for example the Biomedical Technology program element) were not extracted for this version, so no appropriation-level figure is given.
- What did Johns Hopkins APL, PARC and Teledyne receive under N3? What did all N3 teams receive after the phase gates?
- Was the N3 total really $104 million? DARPA declined to say.
- What was the total of the 1970s ARPA biocybernetics program?
- Did any DARPA ethics, legal and social implications panels review these programs, and are their reports public?
- What did the USDA Inspector General conclude about Neuralink, and did the SEC act on the November 2023 request?
